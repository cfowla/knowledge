One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. CAP-IT is a high-value pediatric antimicrobial-stewardship trial. In children selected for amoxicillin at discharge, both lower-dose (35-50 mg/kg/day) and 3-day treatment met the trial's prespecified noninferiority criterion versus higher dose (70-90 mg/kg/day) and 7 days for 28-day clinically indicated antibiotic retreatment.

The analysis population comprised 814 children; the primary endpoint was evaluable for 789 (97%). Retreatment was 12.6% with lower dose versus 12.4% with higher dose (risk difference 0.2 percentage points; 1-sided 95% CI upper bound 4.0%) and 12.5% with 3 days versus 12.5% with 7 days (difference 0.1 percentage points; upper bound 3.9%). Both upper confidence bounds remained below the 8% noninferiority margin. Dose and duration did not show a significant interaction (P=.63).

The result is clinically useful but not boundary-free. The 8% absolute noninferiority margin was increased from 4% after pilot data showed a retreatment rate around 15% instead of the originally assumed 5%; this makes the margin an important interpretive choice rather than a minor statistical detail. Severe-CAP subgroup confidence intervals were wider, with the lower-dose comparison extending to a 10% upper bound, and the authors explicitly caution against generalization to very severe disease, important comorbidities, prolonged inpatient treatment, or prolonged IV therapy.

Section condensation

Background and objective

Amoxicillin is widely used for pediatric CAP, but the trial identified uncertainty about both total daily dose and whether short courses used in other settings translate to high-income secondary-care populations. CAP-IT asked two simultaneous noninferiority questions: lower versus higher oral dose and 3 versus 7 days.

Study design, population, and interventions

Children older than 6 months and weighing 6-24 kg were enrolled when clinicians diagnosed CAP and planned amoxicillin monotherapy at discharge. CAP required recent cough, measured or reported fever, and labored breathing or focal chest findings. Key exclusions were more than 48 hours of prior beta-lactam therapy, prior non-beta-lactam therapy, severe chronic disease, amoxicillin contraindication, complicated pneumonia, and bilateral wheezing without focal chest signs.

Participants were randomized simultaneously in a 2×2 factorial design to 35-50 versus 70-90 mg/kg/day and to 3 versus 7 days. Dose volumes were weight-banded; duration blinding used placebo on days 4-7 in the 3-day group. Randomization used blocks of 8 and was stratified by site and prior in-hospital nontrial antibiotics.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint was clinically indicated systemic antibacterial retreatment for respiratory tract infection, including CAP, within 28 days. A blinded endpoint review committee adjudicated nontrial antibiotic prescriptions. Secondary outcomes included parent-reported CAP symptom severity/duration, diarrhea, rash and thrush, adherence, serious adverse events, and day-28 pneumococcal carriage/phenotypic susceptibility.

Follow-up occurred by scheduled contacts through day 28, with parent diaries for the first 14 days and repeat nasopharyngeal sampling at the final visit. No radiographic or laboratory diagnostic test was mandated for eligibility.

Statistical framework and protocol changes

The final noninferiority margin was an 8-percentage-point absolute risk difference assessed using a 1-sided 95% confidence interval. Assuming a 15% retreatment rate and 15% loss to follow-up, 800 randomized children provided an estimated 90% power. The SAP prespecified marginal dose and duration analyses under the factorial design, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup work.

Supplemental material documents a material protocol evolution after the pilot phase: the expected endpoint rate had been 5% and the original noninferiority margin 4%. With the observed retreatment rate near 15%, the PED and WARD strata were combined for the principal analysis and the margin was revised to 8%. The endpoint review committee was also introduced to adjudicate whether antibiotic retreatment was clinically indicated for respiratory infection.

Results

Participants

Among 2642 screened children, 824 were randomized; 10 received no trial medication, leaving 814 in the analysis population. Median age was 2.5 years (IQR 1.6-3.7); 52% were male. At randomization, 591 (73%) were discharged directly from the ED and 223 (27%) had an inpatient stay under 48 hours. The primary endpoint was evaluable in 789 (97%).

No significant dose-duration interaction was detected (P=.63). A post hoc on-treatment analysis among 693 children taking at least 80% of doses also met noninferiority for both comparisons.

Subgroups and symptoms

In the prespecified severe-CAP subgroup, retreatment was 17.3% (31/180) with lower dose versus 13.5% (25/188) with higher dose; difference 3.8 percentage points with a 1-sided 95% CI upper bound of 10.0% (interaction P=.18). For 3 versus 7 days, retreatment was 16.0% (28/177) versus 14.8% (28/191); difference 1.2 points with upper bound 7.4% (interaction P=.73). These interaction tests did not establish heterogeneity, but precision was poorer than in the full cohort.

Among 14 prespecified secondary endpoints, the reported significant duration differences were cough resolution (median 12 vs 10 days; reported HR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0-1.4; P=.04) and sleep disturbed by cough (median 4 vs 4 days; HR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0-1.4; P=.03). The paper does not frame these small differences as outweighing the primary noninferiority result.

Adverse events, adherence, and microbiology

Potential amoxicillin-related events were common but mostly nonserious: diarrhea in 345 children (44%), rash in 193 (24%), and thrush in 57 (7%). Rash was reported in 27% of the longer-duration group versus 22% of the shorter-duration group. The article reports no deaths among serious adverse events.

At baseline, 272 of 647 sampled participants (42%) carried S. pneumoniae and 46 isolates were penicillin-nonsusceptible. At day 28, 129 of 437 sampled children (29.5%) carried S. pneumoniae; 21 samples had penicillin nonsusceptibility, no penicillin-resistant pneumococci were identified, and no significant dose- or duration-group difference was detected for carriage or nonsusceptibility.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusion

The authors interpret the primary result as support for reduced amoxicillin exposure after discharge in appropriately selected children. The trial also supports twice-daily dosing in this context, while noting that oral absorption at high doses and local pneumococcal susceptibility may matter.

Four limitations are especially load-bearing: bacterial CAP could not be unequivocally identified; the trial does not define total treatment duration for prolonged inpatient/IV treatment; it was not powered for noninferiority within the inpatient-discharge subgroup; and findings should not be generalized to very severe disease or important comorbidity. These limitations materially narrow practice translation.

Validity

Strengths: randomized factorial allocation, blinding, multicenter enrollment, high primary-outcome ascertainment, blinded endpoint adjudication, prespecified SAP, sensitivity/subgroup analyses, and linked resistance endpoints. Risks to interpretation: clinical CAP diagnosis has imperfect bacterial specificity; the retreatment endpoint is influenced by subsequent prescribing behavior; and the noninferiority margin was widened from 4% to 8% after pilot-phase event-rate information.

Clinical translation

Use now: The trial supports considering 3 days and 35-50 mg/kg/day oral amoxicillin after discharge for children who resemble the CAP-IT population, when local practice and guidance accept this evidence. Do not use as evidence for: very severe CAP, significant chronic disease, prolonged inpatient treatment, prolonged IV treatment, or situations where an 8-percentage-point absolute noninferiority margin is clinically unacceptable.

Key appraisal constraint: the severe-CAP dose subgroup had an upper 1-sided 95% CI of 10.0%, so the full-cohort noninferiority conclusion should not be mechanically extended to higher-severity subgroups.

Confidence in appraisal: High for what the packet reports; moderate-high for practice translation because the applicability boundary and noninferiority-margin choice remain clinically consequential.

Do not overextendAvoid extrapolating the dose result to very severe CAP, major comorbidity, prolonged admission, or prolonged IV therapy.
