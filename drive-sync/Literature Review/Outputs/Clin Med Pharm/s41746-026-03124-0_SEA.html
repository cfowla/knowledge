One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Generative AI can lower the technical barrier for health professions educators to build interactive web-based simulations and visualizations that make abstract AI concepts manipulable. The educational value, however, depends on educator facilitation, careful interface design, and systematic verification of generated logic, concepts, and data.

The article targets a specific gap between emerging AI-literacy competencies and practical pedagogy. Rather than using generative AI mainly to teach conventional medical content, the authors propose using it to teach how AI itself behaves—for example, how changing a prediction threshold shifts sensitivity and specificity. Their preferred instructional frame is Kolb’s experiential learning cycle: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation.

The paper is strongest as a design memo and failure-analysis document. It gives concrete examples of useful interfaces, explicitly documents errors produced during generation, identifies cognitive-load problems, and recommends prompt specificity, minimal interfaces, unit-test-like checks, and expert verification. It is weak as evidence of educational efficacy: there is no prospective learner study, comparator, sample size, outcome metric, statistical analysis, or external validation. The appropriate use is therefore build guidance and hypothesis generation, not proof that the approach improves education.

Section condensation

Opening summary

The authors argue that health professions education must prepare learners to use AI safely and propose a practical teaching strategy for non-technical audiences: use generative AI to rapidly create interactive simulations, dynamic visualizations, and case-based interfaces through which learners can manipulate AI concepts rather than only read about them.

The push for AI literacy in healthcare

The paper frames AI literacy as increasingly necessary for clinicians who interpret diagnostic algorithms, use clinical decision support, and communicate with technical teams. It cites the AAMC’s 2025 AI competencies for medical educators, particularly the domain of understanding AI fundamentals, statistical properties, data approaches, strengths, and weaknesses.

The identified pedagogic gap is not a missing competency list but a missing method for teaching technical concepts to learners without computer-science backgrounds. The authors position generative AI as a way to make these concepts concrete by producing manipulable interfaces at low programming cost.

The potential of generative AI to create interactive educational experiences for AI literacy in HPE

The authors distinguish their proposal from the broader literature on using generative AI for curriculum generation, personalized learning, simulated patients, assessment, and educational resources. Their target is narrower: using AI-generated interfaces to teach quantitative and engineering-oriented ideas such as probabilistic model outputs, performance metrics, interpretable-model behavior, and operating-point changes on ROC curves.

The pedagogic rationale is experiential learning. The paper maps interactive manipulation to Kolb’s four-stage cycle and emphasizes that experience alone is insufficient: educator support, participation, and guided reflection are needed to convert interaction into transferable understanding. The authors note related use of experiential learning in HPE and engineering/AI education but state they are unaware of published applications specifically to teaching AI within HPE.

Educator facilitation through an AI-generated sensitivity-specificity simulation

Using a short ChatGPT prompt, the authors created a browser-based binary prediction-model simulator in which learners change a decision threshold and observe sensitivity, specificity, true/false positives, and true/false negatives. The clinical teaching point is that model behavior depends on the operating threshold and that improving sensitivity generally costs specificity, with concrete implications such as sepsis-alert false alarms versus missed cases.

The lesson is explicitly staged through Kolb’s cycle: begin with a clinical case and value judgment about false negatives versus false positives; explore the simulator; verbalize the general threshold trade-off; then return to the interface to make and test predictions. The authors assign the educator a central role in contextualization and facilitation, allowing freer learner experimentation mainly at the final stage.

Limitations and risks: content correctness

The authors report encountering multiple hallucinations while developing the supplementary interfaces, including incorrect formulas, wrong or implausible data points, incorrect simulation logic, and misleading visualizations. Figure 3 illustrates a faulty decision-tree visualization in which terminal classifications were structurally wrong, plus a decision-boundary display whose background shading did not change meaningfully across the boundary.

The key safety claim is that reliable correction requires enough domain knowledge to recognize the problem. The authors therefore argue against unsupervised learner generation of these tools and recommend expert review and stress testing before classroom use. They also caution that retrieval-augmented generation may constrain an LLM to reference material but does not eliminate hallucination.

Limitations and risks: interface and cognitive load

A recurring generation failure was visual and informational excess. Without explicit instructions to minimize the interface, generated artifacts tended to include more controls, metrics, and visual detail than the learning objective required. The authors interpret this using cognitive load theory: unnecessary interface elements increase extraneous load and consume working-memory capacity that could instead support productive conceptual processing.

The design implication is not to remove intrinsic difficulty but to calibrate it and reduce presentation-induced load. Figure 4 is presented as a concrete example of an overbuilt fairness-metrics interface whose visual density obscures the central lesson.

Limitations and risks: burden on educators

The technology lowers the barrier to building interactive software but does not eliminate expert work. Each tool still needs specification, error detection, verification, iterative refinement, and pedagogic alignment; these tasks recur for every module and scale with curriculum size.

The authors therefore reject a simple labor-saving interpretation. Their safe-use model redistributes educator effort toward specification, testing, review, and facilitation rather than necessarily reducing total effort.

Design principles for generating interactive educational experiences with AI

The authors recommend: specify the learning objective, interaction flow, hard constraints, and exclusions; explicitly request a simple, minimal interface; create unit-test-like checks for expected behaviors and edge cases; verify all generated educational concepts; and verify the illustrative data used by the interface. Their examples include checking that threshold changes alter performance metrics correctly, fairness calculations produce expected edge-case values, and visualization branches correspond to intended model logic.

These recommendations are explicitly experience-informed, based on interface development, informal classroom use by the first author, and supervision of students creating interfaces. They are not presented as empirically validated instructional design standards. The paper closes by calling for prospective evaluation of whether this approach improves learning outcomes.

Data availability and disclosures

No datasets were generated or analyzed. The authors acknowledge support for a UCSF AI and Education Faculty Catalyst Program, declare no competing interests, and disclose use of ChatGPT to refine manuscript phrasing while stating that the ideas are the authors’ own.

WORKFLOW / INSTRUCTIONAL CYCLE

   → discuss a clinical case and the relative cost of false negatives vs false positives

2. Reflective observation

Implementation takeaways

Build around one learning objectiveDefine the concept and the exact learner action before asking an LLM to generate interface code.
Constrain the interface aggressivelySpecify a minimal, simple interaction surface; visual completeness is not the goal.
Treat generated teaching tools like softwareCreate explicit behavioral checks for thresholds, branches, calculations, edge cases, and expected output changes.
Verify concepts and data separatelyCorrect logic does not guarantee correct labels, formulas, or demonstration data; each layer requires review.
Keep expert facilitation in the loopThe proposed pedagogy depends on guided interpretation and reflection, not learner exposure to an interface alone.
Do not equate faster prototyping with lower workloadGeneration shifts work toward specification, review, testing, and iterative refinement.

What to use now

  • The four-stage case → explore → conceptualize → test cycle as an instructional-design template.
  • The authors’ verification checklist as a pre-deployment safety heuristic.
  • The distinction between intrinsic learning difficulty and avoidable interface-induced cognitive load.
  • Unit-test thinking for educational simulations, especially calculations and decision logic.

What not to overclaim

  • That AI-generated interfaces improve learning outcomes.
  • That they reduce faculty workload.
  • That RAG or prompting can reliably prevent hallucinations.
  • That novice learners can safely generate and use such tools without expert review.
  • That the specific commercial-model examples generalize to other versions, tools, or future systems.

Most useful next evidence

  • Prospective comparison against static teaching materials or manually authored interactive tools.
  • Pre/post knowledge plus delayed retention and transfer testing.
  • Structured measurement of hallucination/error rates before and after expert verification.
  • Educator time-on-task and revision burden.
  • Multi-site testing across learner levels and health professions.
