One-page synthesis

Core thesis: antifungal stewardship appears capable of improving antifungal selection, use, diagnostics, therapeutic drug monitoring, toxicity management, and expenditure in hospital practice, but the evidence directly addressing chronic respiratory fungal disease is sparse and low certainty.

Clinical question / PICO

Population

Intended focus: patients with chronic respiratory disease and fungal infection, allergy, or sensitization. Actual included evidence: heterogeneous hospital populations, predominantly invasive fungal disease and Candida-focused cohorts.

Intervention

Antifungal stewardship, including multidisciplinary review, pharmacist/ID involvement, therapeutic drug monitoring, diagnostic stewardship, rapid diagnostics, susceptibility testing, and standardized antifungal-use practices.

Comparator

Variable and often implicit: pre-intervention care, usual care, no specialist consultation, or no stewardship intervention. Several studies were observational without a strong concurrent comparator.

Outcomes

Antifungal appropriateness/use, expenditure, therapeutic exposure, diagnostic speed, toxicity, mortality/morbidity, and resistance-related outcomes.

Review question

What evidence exists for the use and impact of antifungal stewardship within chronic respiratory disease care?

Answer supported by this source

Potential benefit is plausible and repeatedly observed in broader invasive-fungal-disease stewardship, but direct respiratory evidence is too limited to define efficacy or a standard implementation model.

Section condensation

Abstract

The review frames fungal infection and sensitization as common and clinically important in bronchiectasis, COPD, and cystic fibrosis, where prolonged antifungal exposure may occur. The stated gap is that antifungal stewardship has expanded in invasive fungal disease but is poorly defined in chronic respiratory care.

The authors report a PRISMA-guided search of Medline, Embase, and GlobalHealth for 1949–2020, with CASP/GRADE assessment and qualitative synthesis. The abstract reports 28 included papers and only two Aspergillus-focused studies; this count conflicts with the 29 records reported in the Results and PRISMA figure.

Introduction

The background describes a spectrum of Aspergillus-related respiratory disease—from sensitization and ABPA to CPA and invasive disease—and emphasizes prolonged triazole exposure, emerging triazole resistance, toxicity, and the role of therapeutic drug monitoring. Stewardship is positioned as a means to optimize indication, drug selection, PK/PD exposure, duration, route, toxicity, and resistance risk.

The respiratory rationale is clinically coherent within the source, but several background citations are not obviously aligned with the statements they support when cross-checked against the paper’s own reference list; for example, references 11 and 12 are cited after a TDM/PK-PD stewardship statement although their listed titles concern ESBL bloodstream-infection risk factors and benzodiazepine/wine use, respectively. This is an internal citation-integrity concern.

Methodology

The authors describe a PRISMA-based systematic literature review searching Medline via Ovid, Embase, and GlobalHealth for publications from 1949 through 2020. Eligible papers implemented antifungal stewardship with reported outcomes or reported clinical outcomes of antifungal TDM. Exclusions included non-English papers, case reports, and narrative/systematic reviews. Covidence was used for screening.

Because populations, interventions, and outcomes differed, the review used descriptive summary and qualitative synthesis rather than meta-analysis. The paper says included studies were assessed with GRADE and mentions CASP in the abstract, but the main text does not provide a reproducible per-domain risk-of-bias assessment, detailed CASP results, or explicit upgrade/downgrade reasoning for the GRADE labels.

Results — disease area and directness

The search yielded 3229 records. After duplicate removal, screening, and exclusions, Figure 1 reports 29 final records: 27 focused on invasive fungal disease and only 2 on chronic respiratory disease. The narrative states 25 of 29 articles were predominantly Candida/invasive-fungal-disease studies, while 14 included some patients with chronic respiratory illness.

The two specifically respiratory-focused studies were Nwankwo et al. and Browne et al. This creates the review’s central finding: antifungal-stewardship evidence exists, but the direct evidence base for chronic pulmonary fungal disease is extremely small relative to the broader hospital stewardship literature.

Results — respiratory-specific studies

Nwankwo et al. is summarized as an 18-month prospective cohort of 178 patients at a tertiary cardio-pulmonary hospital. A mycologist and pharmacist provided weekly stewardship rounds, a multidisciplinary meeting twice weekly, and an outpatient clinic. CPA represented 32% of underlying fungal disease. The programme generated 285 specific recommendations, increased therapeutic azole dosing, and reduced monthly antifungal expenditure by 44% without a reported deleterious clinical outcome.

Browne et al. used a complex lung-infection multidisciplinary team comprising three respiratory physicians, an infectious-diseases specialist, a microbiologist, and two pharmacists. The team met six times in 12 months, discussed 32 cases, and classified 13 as complex fungal lung infection (including SAIA, CPA, CPA/ABPA overlap, and ABPA/bacterial-infection overlap). TDM was implemented; the review reports a significant reduction in resistance development but does not provide the effect size in its narrative.

Results — quality and outcomes

The majority of included studies were observational and classified as low or very-low evidence. The Results section says three studies reached moderate GRADE quality; Table 1 visibly labels Menichetti, Swoboda, and Reed as moderate. The Discussion later says four studies reached moderate grade evidence, creating another internal inconsistency.

Eleven articles were summarized as supporting more appropriate antifungal use and less overuse/misuse without worsening quality of care or outcomes. Rapid diagnostics such as T2 Candida and MALDI-TOF were associated with faster diagnosis and reduced inappropriate antifungal use. Multidisciplinary stewardship was associated with less hepatotoxicity and lower 30-day in-hospital mortality in candidaemia in some included studies. These signals are not pooled and arise from heterogeneous study designs and populations.

Discussion

The authors interpret the broader invasive-fungal-disease literature as supportive of antifungal stewardship for optimizing therapy, reducing toxicity, and possibly improving mortality-related outcomes. Protocolized indication/dose review and TDM are emphasized. They explicitly state that whether optimized long-term therapy reduces acquisition or development of antifungal resistance remains unclear.

For chronic respiratory care, the authors emphasize the absence of a robust evidence base, variability in diagnosis and management, and the need for prospective work. They also note that no included paper evaluated stewardship effects on fungal sensitization control, despite antifungal use in allergic respiratory disease such as ABPA.

Conclusion

The review concludes that antifungal stewardship is a logical extension of antimicrobial stewardship and appears beneficial in invasive fungal disease, but its application and evidence base remain limited in chronic respiratory disease despite prolonged antifungal exposure and resistance concerns.

The source’s most defensible conclusion is therefore a research and implementation gap rather than proof of respiratory-specific effectiveness: further work is needed to define resistance drivers and determine the impact of antifungal stewardship in chronic respiratory populations.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a field map and a pointer to the Nwankwo and Browne respiratory-focused experiences. Do not use it as sufficient evidence for a standardized chronic-respiratory antifungal-stewardship protocol or as proof that stewardship reduces resistance or mortality in that population.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build around reviewable decisions

Useful stewardship targets in this literature include indication confirmation, diagnostic interpretation, drug selection/de-escalation, dose/exposure optimization, duration, toxicity, and resistance surveillance.

Make TDM central when exposure is uncertain

The source repeatedly treats TDM as a core optimization tool, especially for triazoles. The respiratory examples use TDM within multidisciplinary review rather than as an isolated laboratory process.

Use multidisciplinary respiratory expertise

The two directly relevant programmes combine respiratory medicine with pharmacy and microbiology/mycology/ID expertise. This is a more defensible takeaway than any specific drug algorithm.

Measure both utilization and clinical outcomes

Cost and antifungal-use reductions are common outcomes, but future respiratory programmes should also track toxicity, treatment success, hospitalization, mortality, resistance, and patient-centered outcomes.

Separate invasive-fungal evidence from chronic pulmonary evidence

Do not automatically generalize candidemia or ICU stewardship results to CPA, ABPA, bronchiectasis, COPD, or CF. Use broader evidence to generate intervention components, not to claim respiratory efficacy.

Research gap worth targeting

Prospective, multisite chronic-respiratory stewardship studies with standardized diagnostic definitions, azole TDM targets, resistance endpoints, clinical outcomes, and implementation fidelity would directly address the gap identified by this review.

Bottom line: the paper supports antifungal stewardship as a promising, rational framework for respiratory fungal care, but its strongest contribution is demonstrating how little direct evidence existed. Use it to design questions, stewardship components, and a reading list—not as a stand-alone practice standard.
