2. One-page synthesis

Bottom line: MBD-Check is a promising, externally validated screening adjunct for adults already referred or suspected to have a mild bleeding disorder before surgery. It is not evidence enough to independently clear an unselected surgical patient, replace hematology consultation when clinical concern remains high, or implement without local validation and governance.

Core thesis

The authors developed an interpretable machine-learning model to predict mild bleeding disorders using a streamlined bleeding history plus aPTT, platelet function analysis with epinephrine-collagen cartridge, and sex.

Most useful result

In the external validation cohort of 217 Swiss adults referred for suspected mild bleeding disorder, MBD-Check achieved AUROC 0.85, sensitivity 90.2%, specificity 54.3%, PPV 68%, and NPV 84% at a 47% threshold.

Clinical meaning

The model favors sensitivity over specificity. A positive result mainly supports hematology referral; a negative result reduces likelihood but does not eliminate mild bleeding disorder when history, procedure risk, or confounders remain concerning.

Practice caution: The validation population was not an unselected preoperative population. The authors explicitly limit intended use to patients with suspected mild bleeding disorders. Broad routine surgical screening would likely produce many false positives and could still miss clinically relevant cases.

5. Section-by-section condensation

Summary / abstract

The paper addresses preoperative detection of mild bleeding disorders, which are common inherited bleeding conditions that can present unexpectedly during surgery. The authors aimed to build, externally validate, and implement an interpretable ML-based decision support tool using data reasonably available during pre-anesthesia evaluation.

The final model used aPTT, PFA-EPI, sex, and streamlined bleeding-history items. In external validation, sensitivity was 90.2%, specificity 54.3%, AUROC 0.85, and median System Usability Scale score was 82.5, indicating high usability among surveyed surgeons, anesthesiologists, and hematologists.

Introduction

The introduction frames a real clinical gap: routine coagulation parameters such as PT/INR and aPTT do not reliably identify mild bleeding disorders, while the ISTH-BAT is too complex for routine preoperative use by non-hematologists. The authors position ML as a way to combine bleeding-history signals with selected laboratory inputs while retaining usability and explainability.

The rationale is clinically plausible, but the need is not the same as proof that an ML triage tool improves outcomes. The introduction correctly names barriers to AI adoption: unclear need, bias, weak external validation, lack of transparency, and poor workflow fit.

Methods: study design and participants

The work combines two independent prospective cross-sectional cohorts from Swiss tertiary centers. The development cohort included consecutive adults referred for suspected mild bleeding disorder to Inselspital, Bern University Hospital from Jan 1, 2012 to March 31, 2017. The external validation cohort included 217 adults at Cantonal Hospital Lucerne from Jan 1, 2011 to Sept 30, 2013.

Eligibility required age 18 years or older and referral for suspected bleeding disorder. Diagnostic investigations followed current guidelines as reported by the authors, with final diagnosis established by an expert panel. This target-population match is a strength, but the study does not address unselected preoperative patients or pediatric populations.

Methods: model development

The training cohort was split 80/20 into model-development and internal-validation datasets. Candidate predictors were first constrained by stakeholder focus groups to variables feasible for pre-anesthesia evaluation, then selected with the Boruta feature selection algorithm. Preprocessing used bagged-tree imputation fit only on the training predictors, followed by centering/scaling and Yeo-Johnson transformation.

Seventeen supervised algorithms were screened with five-times repeated ten-fold cross-validation using AUROC. The final model was a radial-kernel support vector machine selected after hyperparameter tuning and internal validation. The selected 47% threshold came from a cost-based approach that weighted false negatives 25% more costly than false positives.

Methods: external validation

External validation used the independent Lucerne cohort, with performance assessed by sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, AUROC, and precision-recall metrics. Comparator performance was also evaluated for ISTH-BAT, INR, aPTT, and PFA-EPI.

The external validation is a central strength because it uses a separate site and prospective cohort. Remaining limits include shared national practice context, older data, absent ethnicity data, and incomplete information in the main text regarding calibration and net clinical benefit.

Methods: implementation and usability

The final model was implemented as an R-Shiny web application with a Shapley-value explanation plot for individual predictions. Usability testing used an online survey platform with four case vignettes and the System Usability Scale.

Participants were recruited by purposive convenience sampling through professional networks. This is appropriate for early usability assessment but weaker than representative workflow testing in live preoperative clinics.

Results

The development cohort included 555 patients; 288 (52%) were diagnosed with mild bleeding disorders, most commonly platelet function disorders (118/288, 41%) and von Willebrand factor disorders (50/288, 17%). The external validation cohort included 217 patients. The final selected features included PFA-EPI, aPTT, sex, family history, and simplified ISTH-BAT bleeding-history domains.

External validation showed AUROC 0.85, area under precision-recall curve 0.89, sensitivity 90.2%, specificity 54.3%, PPV 68%, and NPV 84%. Comparator AUROCs were lower: ISTH-BAT 0.79, PFA-EPI 0.73, aPTT 0.63, and INR 0.49. The usability survey included 86 respondents; median completion/interpretation time was 72 seconds and median SUS score was 82.5.

Discussion and limitations

The authors conclude that MBD-Check could support preoperative referral decisions for suspected mild bleeding disorders and propose using the 47% threshold to decide whether hematology referral should be considered. They emphasize sensitivity and accept limited specificity as a deliberate screening tradeoff.

The main limitations are material for practice: both cohorts are old; sample size is small for ML relative to modern clinical AI standards; populations are Swiss and relatively homogeneous; ethnicity data were not collected; the tool was not developed for unselected surgical patients; and the study did not measure clinical outcomes after deployment. These limitations keep the tool in the validation/pilot category rather than a definitive practice-changing intervention.

7. Diagnostic validity and clinical translation

Internal validity

Reasonable for a diagnostic prediction study. Consecutive prospective cohorts, expert-panel diagnosis, separation of training/internal/external data, training-only preprocessing, uncertainty estimates, and sensitivity analyses support validity. Weak points are sample size relative to ML complexity, missing PFA data in training, and incomplete main-text access to all diagnostic-definition details.

External validity

Constrained. External validation was at a second Swiss center, but both cohorts were old, geographically narrow, adult-only, and limited to referred/suspected patients. Ethnicity data were unavailable. General surgical pretest probability would be lower than in these cohorts, changing PPV/NPV.

Calibration and thresholding

The 47% threshold was selected to penalize false negatives more than false positives. This matches screening logic. The article does not provide enough main-text calibration, decision-curve, or site-level threshold-transportability evidence to treat the cutoff as universally portable.

Outcome evidence

Not established. The study predicts diagnostic classification, not perioperative bleeding, transfusion, procedural delay, hospital length of stay, cost, patient anxiety, or hematology workload after deployment.

Clinical risk translation: External-validation sensitivity of 90.2% implies roughly 1 in 10 patients with mild bleeding disorder could be classified below threshold. With NPV 84% in this enriched referred cohort, a negative result is insufficient as a sole clearance criterion when procedure risk or bleeding history is high.

8. Practice implications

What should change now

  • For knowledge management: Preserve this as a high-value diagnostic prediction paper for preoperative bleeding-disorder triage and clinical AI implementation design.
  • For clinical workflow design: Consider MBD-Check as a candidate adjunct for a local pilot in adults with suspected mild bleeding disorder, especially where referral volume is high and hematology access is constrained.
  • For education: The study reinforces that routine PT/INR and aPTT alone are poor screening tools for mild bleeding disorders; structured bleeding history remains central.

What should not change yet

  • Do not use MBD-Check as a standalone rule-out for surgery.
  • Do not apply it to unselected surgical populations, pediatrics, active bleeding, emergency surgery, or patients with obvious acquired coagulopathy without separate validation.
  • Do not interpret a positive result as a diagnosis; it supports further evaluation.
  • Do not treat the 47% threshold as locally validated without checking assay calibration, referral prevalence, case mix, and clinical consequences.

Implementation constraints and safety controls

Laboratory harmonization

PFA-EPI and aPTT are assay- and institution-sensitive. Local validation needs local reference intervals, preanalytic rules, medication restrictions, and handling of missing values.

Clinical governance

Deployment should include ownership by hematology/anesthesia, version control, audit trail, clinician override, periodic performance review, and a defined process for incident review.

Workflow fit

The usability result is encouraging, but live workflow testing should measure whether the tool changes referral patterns, documentation, surgical delays, and patient outcomes.

Strengths

  • Clinically focused target problem with clear workflow rationale.
  • Prospective data collection and independent external validation.
  • Feature set constrained by stakeholder feasibility rather than pure model convenience.
  • Comparison with existing screening tools and routine tests.
  • Interpretability and usability were treated as part of implementation, not an afterthought.

Limitations

  • Diagnostic prediction, not outcome-impact evidence.
  • Limited specificity creates referral burden if implemented broadly.
  • Validation cohort is geographically and temporally narrow.
  • Not validated in unselected preoperative patients, pediatrics, diverse populations, or varied laboratory environments.
  • Appendix, code, live web app, and raw data were not audited in this artifact.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate-high for judging the article’s diagnostic-prediction evidence; moderate for practice translation.
