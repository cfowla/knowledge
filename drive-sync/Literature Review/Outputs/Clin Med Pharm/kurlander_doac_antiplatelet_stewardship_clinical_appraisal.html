3. One-page clinical synthesis

Core thesis. In a large VHA population of adults prescribed DOACs, a two-stage, multilevel antithrombotic stewardship program was associated with faster reduction in antiplatelet use than control sites. The signal was strongest in patients with stable CAD, the subgroup most aligned with current guideline-based antiplatelet deimplementation.
Primary effect
-0.58 pp
Additional semiannual change in antiplatelet use at intervention sites versus controls after both stages.
Stable CAD subgroup
-2.1 pp
Additional semiannual change; equivalent to a 5.5% reduction relative to baseline antiplatelet prevalence in that subgroup.
Setting
7 vs 128
Seven VISN 8 intervention health systems compared with 128 control VHA systems.
Outcome type
surrogate
Prescribing prevalence, not bleeding, ischemic events, hospitalization, or mortality.

Clinical value: The study is highly relevant for anticoagulation clinic, specialty pharmacy, and acute-care transitions workflows because it shows a scalable way to identify and reduce potentially inappropriate aspirin/P2Y12 overlap in DOAC patients. The strongest actionable use is not automatic deprescribing; it is creation of a systematic review process that verifies antiplatelet indication, duration, bleeding risk, and ownership of the decision.

Primary limitation: The evidence is implementation evidence, not direct comparative-effectiveness evidence for patient outcomes. It supports a workflow; it does not by itself establish that stopping antiplatelet therapy is safe in every patient flagged by an EHR or dashboard.

6. Population, exclusions, and applicability

Included population

  • Adults prescribed a VHA-dispensed DOAC on the first day of each month from July 2020 to July 2023.
  • Patients were attributed to intervention if the DOAC prescription originated from a VISN 8 medical center.
  • Baseline characteristics in December 2020: intervention sites had more AF (75.4% vs 69.7%), more CAD (31.5% vs 28.1%), similar VTE (~21%), and a markedly different DOAC mix, including more dabigatran and less apixaban.

Study-level exclusions

  • Ten VHA health systems were excluded because they had implemented similar antiplatelet-safety initiatives or transitioned to a separate EHR system.
  • Supplementary details on diagnosis/procedure code definitions and statistical methods were referenced but not available in the uploaded main PDF.

Clinical generalizability limits

  • The VHA population was predominantly older and male. Female patients represented 2.6% in both intervention and control cohorts.
  • VISN 8 intervention patients were entirely classified as urban in the baseline table, unlike the broader controls.
  • The VHA has integrated pharmacy data, anticoagulation-focused clinical pharmacists, population-management dashboards, and pharmacist prescriptive authority. Community hospitals or fragmented outpatient networks may need adaptation.
  • OTC aspirin and non-VHA medication documentation can be misclassified. This matters because aspirin is frequently obtained outside prescription claims.

8. Dosing, monitoring, contraindications, interactions, renal/hepatic considerations

Medication-scope warning. This study did not test DOAC dose changes, antiplatelet doses, renal dose algorithms, reversal strategies, or monitoring protocols. The following points are practice considerations for implementing the stewardship workflow, not trial-derived dosing recommendations.

Dosing

  • No intervention dose: The intervention was educational outreach, EHR language, letters, templated documentation, and a pharmacist-facing dashboard flag.
  • DOAC dosing: Confirm that the DOAC dose is appropriate for indication, age, weight, renal function, hepatic function, and interacting drugs before changing antiplatelet therapy. The paper included apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban but did not compare drug-specific dose safety.
  • Antiplatelet exposure: Low-dose aspirin in the paper was defined as not more than 100 mg; P2Y12 inhibitors were included as a class. Verify actual OTC aspirin use and whether aspirin is being taken for primary prevention, secondary prevention, post-PCI, PAD, or outside specialist instruction.

Monitoring and documentation

  • Document antiplatelet indication, intended stop date, prescriber ownership, and patient counseling in the anticoagulation note or medication-management encounter.
  • Track bleeding history, CBC/hemoglobin trend, renal function, hepatic disease/coagulopathy, fall/trauma risk, NSAID exposure, SSRI/SNRI exposure, alcohol use, and recent procedures.
  • For dashboard implementation, monitor alert volume, action rate, discontinuation rate, override reasons, time to action, and adverse-event review triggers.

Contraindications and interaction checks relevant to flagged patients

  • Active pathological bleeding or major unresolved bleeding risk should prompt urgent antithrombotic reassessment, not routine dashboard handling.
  • Concomitant antiplatelet agents, chronic NSAIDs, fibrinolytics, heparin/LMWH, and other anticoagulants increase bleeding risk with DOACs.
  • Apixaban and rivaroxaban are affected by combined P-gp and strong CYP3A inhibitors/inducers; dabigatran is strongly affected by P-gp interactions and renal function. These considerations should be checked during any stewardship encounter.
  • Rivaroxaban should generally be avoided in moderate-to-severe hepatic impairment or hepatic disease associated with coagulopathy; apixaban is not recommended in severe hepatic impairment; dabigatran exposure rises substantially as renal function worsens.
  • APS requires caution. For suspected or confirmed high-risk APS, especially triple-positive APS, reassess the appropriateness of DOAC therapy rather than focusing only on aspirin/P2Y12 overlap.

Renal/hepatic considerations

  • Renal function affects DOAC exposure and eligibility, especially dabigatran and rivaroxaban. Recalculate CrCl using the local institutional method when therapy changes are considered.
  • Hepatic impairment can increase bleeding risk through both altered drug handling and coagulopathy. Dashboard flags should have an escalation pathway for cirrhosis, severe hepatic impairment, or unexplained INR elevation.
  • The study did not evaluate whether deprescribing effects differed by renal function, hepatic disease, frailty, age band, or bleeding-risk score.

9. Section-by-section condensation

Abstract and Key Points

The abstract frames the problem as overuse of antiplatelet therapy among DOAC patients and evaluates a multicomponent stewardship strategy. In 7 intervention VHA systems and 128 controls, combined interventions were associated with an additional -0.58 percentage-point semiannual reduction in antiplatelet use, with the largest subgroup effect in stable CAD (-2.1 pp per 6 months).

The abstract appropriately describes the prescribing outcome but uses clinical language such as potentially harmful therapy. The paper's own data support reduced prescribing; clinical harm reduction is inferred from prior evidence, not directly measured in this study.

Introduction / Background

The authors note that anticoagulants are a leading cause of medication-harm ED visits and that nearly one-third of OAC patients also take antiplatelet therapy. They cite prior trial/meta-analytic evidence that OAC plus antiplatelet therapy increases bleeding without clear thromboembolic benefit in chronic CAD requiring long-term anticoagulation.

The rationale is strong for stable CAD and no-indication groups, but more nuanced for recent ACS/PCI/CABG, PAD, and APS. The introduction identifies real-world barriers: fear of thromboembolic events, uncertainty about indications, unclear clinician ownership, and lack of familiarity with trials.

Methods: design and setting

The study used retrospective multiperiod comparative interrupted-time-series analysis from July 2020 to July 2023. Intervention occurred in VISN 8, including 7 health care systems and 90 outpatient clinics; 128 other VHA systems served as controls after excluding 10 systems with similar initiatives or EHR transition.

This design is stronger than uncontrolled pre-post QI but remains nonrandomized. It can estimate whether trends changed more at intervention sites than controls, yet cannot fully remove confounding from regional differences, secular trends, local implementation intensity, or unmeasured patient mix.

Intervention

Stage 1 included academic detailing by a clinical pharmacist, educational outreach to clinicians and patients, EHR changes in DOAC prior authorization and pharmacist consult templates, and letters to non-VHA clinicians and patients. Stage 2 added a pharmacist-facing electronic flag in the DOAC population-management dashboard identifying patients receiving antiplatelet medications.

The workflow fits a pharmacist-led stewardship model: identify high-risk overlap, perform medication and indication review, contact clinicians/patients as needed, and deprescribe if within pharmacist scope. Stage 2 was additive to ongoing stage 1 interventions, so the flag was not tested as a stand-alone intervention.

Outcomes and clinical subgroups

The primary outcome was monthly site-level percentage of DOAC patients prescribed aspirin or P2Y12 inhibitors. For VHA-dispensed antiplatelets, exposure was inferred from dispensing date plus twice the days' supply; documented non-VHA medications were assumed continued until removed from the medication list.

Clinical subgroups were hierarchical: recent ACS/PCI/CABG within 12 months; PAD or APS; stable CAD; and no listed indication. This structure is clinically useful but depends on administrative data and misses privately paid or Medicare-covered care not captured in VHA data.

Results

Preintervention antiplatelet use was 26.1% in intervention sites and 30.1% in control sites. Stage 1 was associated with -0.29 pp per 6 months versus controls, stage 2 added -0.29 pp per 6 months, and the combined stage 2 period effect was -0.58 pp per 6 months versus controls.

The stable CAD subgroup had the clearest signal: -2.1 pp per 6 months, equal to a 5.5% additional change relative to baseline subgroup prevalence. Changes were not statistically significant in recent coronary event/revascularization, PAD/APS, or no-indication subgroups. PPI and NSAID off-target outcomes did not show a meaningful combined-intervention change.

Discussion

The authors interpret the findings as evidence that academic detailing/EHR changes and pharmacist-facing flags have complementary roles in antithrombotic stewardship. They emphasize that effects were clinically meaningful, especially for stable CAD, and that there remains substantial room for improvement.

The discussion appropriately highlights implementation challenges: alert fatigue, need for local tailoring, VHA-specific pharmacist infrastructure, and the need for more precise criteria to target patients most likely to benefit. It also notes that patients without an antiplatelet indication should remain a future target despite weaker observed effects.

Limitations and conclusion

Key limitations include potential aspirin misclassification, imperfect indication classification, absence of clinical outcomes due to insufficient power, VHA-specific context, inability to identify uncommon high-ischemic-risk patients who merit long-term combination therapy, and inability to isolate the electronic flag as a stand-alone effect.

The conclusion is reasonable if confined to implementation: a multilevel stewardship intervention was associated with reduced antiplatelet use among DOAC patients. It should not be read as proof that dashboard-triggered antiplatelet discontinuation improves outcomes in all populations.

11. Safety signals and clinical-risk interpretation

What the study directly measured

  • Monthly antiplatelet prescribing prevalence among DOAC patients.
  • Off-target changes in NSAID use and PPI use among high-GI-risk patients.
  • Subgroup differences based on administrative indication categories.

What the study did not measure

  • Major bleeding, clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding, GI bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, transfusion, hospitalization, ED visits, stroke, MI, stent thrombosis, limb ischemia, VTE recurrence, or mortality.
  • Clinical safety of deprescribing in patients with complex PCI, high ischemic risk, APS, symptomatic PAD, or specialist-directed long-term antiplatelet plans.

Practical safety signals

  • The intervention did not appear to worsen NSAID or PPI process metrics in the combined analysis, but those measures are crude and do not prove safety.
  • Stable CAD was the best-supported target. Recent coronary event/revascularization should be protected from automatic discontinuation logic.
  • Medication-list accuracy is a safety-critical dependency because OTC aspirin and non-VHA care can determine whether a patient is truly exposed or whether an indication exists.
Insufficient evidence for clinical action: Do not infer from this paper that all DOAC + antiplatelet combinations should be stopped, that PPI gastroprotection is unnecessary, or that dashboard flags can replace indication-specific review.

13. What should and should not change in practice

Should change
  • Screen DOAC patients for aspirin/P2Y12 overlap.
  • Prioritize stable CAD and no-indication cases for pharmacist review.
  • Document antiplatelet indication and stop date.
  • Use EHR language, templates, and dashboards to support consistent decisions.
Should not change
  • Do not stop antiplatelet therapy solely from a flag.
  • Do not apply stable-CAD logic to recent ACS/PCI/CABG.
  • Do not treat this study as patient-outcome proof.
  • Do not ignore outside records, OTC aspirin, or specialist plans.
High-yield pharmacist role
  • Medication reconciliation.
  • Indication and duration verification.
  • Bleeding-risk mitigation.
  • Provider/patient education.
  • Policy and dashboard refinement.
Clinical caution zones
  • Complex PCI or stent thrombosis history.
  • Recent ACS/CABG/PCI.
  • Symptomatic PAD with planned vascular strategy.
  • APS or questionable DOAC appropriateness.
  • Severe renal/hepatic dysfunction.
Practice translation: Use this as evidence to build or refine an antithrombotic stewardship process. Use guidelines, labels, and patient-specific data to make the actual pharmacotherapy decision.

15. Bottom line for clinical practice

  1. Build the workflow: A pharmacist-facing, population-level DOAC + antiplatelet review process is reasonable where medication data and pharmacist capacity support it.
  2. Start with stable CAD: Stable CAD patients on therapeutic DOACs appear to be the highest-yield and most guideline-aligned initial target for antiplatelet deimplementation review.
  3. Do not automate the clinical decision: The alert should trigger a review, not a discontinuation order.
  4. Protect recent coronary indications: Recent ACS/PCI/CABG, complex PCI, stent thrombosis history, and specialist-directed plans require careful review and usually escalation.
  5. Measure outcomes locally: Track both process outcomes and sentinel bleeding/thrombotic events after antiplatelet discontinuation.
  6. Evidence limit: This study is sufficient to justify workflow improvement; it is insufficient as sole evidence for individual-patient pharmacotherapy changes.
