Definitions in paper context

Definitions are paraphrased for use while reading this paper. The “context” column explains how the term functions in the article rather than giving a stand-alone textbook entry.

TermWorking definitionUse in this paperReading note
physiologically-based pharmacokinetic modeling A mechanistic modeling approach that represents the body as connected physiologic compartments and uses drug-specific ADME properties plus population physiology to predict concentration-time profiles. The paper evaluates PBPK models for predicting AUC and Cmax changes in renal and hepatic impairment, then compares simulated organ-impaired-to-healthy exposure ratios with observed clinical data. The key question is not whether PBPK can fit healthy data, but whether disease physiology is represented well enough to extrapolate to organ impairment.
uremic toxins Solutes that accumulate when kidney function declines and can alter nonrenal drug disposition. The paper describes renal impairment as involving reduced filtration/tubular secretion plus accumulation of uremic toxins that can down-regulate or inhibit CYP metabolism and inhibit renal organic anion transporters. This is why renal impairment can affect drugs cleared by metabolism, not only drugs cleared unchanged by the kidney.
alpha-acidic glycoprotein The paper’s wording for alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AAG/AGP), an acute-phase plasma protein that binds many basic and lipophilic drugs. In renal insufficiency, the article expects AAG to increase; changes in plasma proteins alter free fraction and therefore exposure interpretation in PBPK models. Use caution with terminology: “alpha-acidic glycoprotein” is less standard wording; AAG or alpha-1-acid glycoprotein is the usual term.
liver parenchyma The functional tissue of the liver, mainly hepatocytes and supporting hepatic architecture responsible for metabolism, synthesis, and biliary handling. The paper says chronic liver diseases can destroy liver parenchyma, leading to cirrhosis and reductions in functional hepatocytes, hepatic blood flow, protein synthesis, and biliary excretion. Loss of parenchyma is a mechanistic input for reduced hepatic metabolic capacity in impairment models.
biliary cirrhosis Cirrhosis involving the biliary system, with impaired bile formation or flow and progressive liver damage. The article groups hepatic and biliary cirrhosis as chronic liver impairment states that can alter hepatic blood flow, albumin/AGP, functional hepatocytes, and biliary excretion. In PK terms, biliary impairment matters for drugs or metabolites relying on bile-mediated excretion or bile-dependent absorption.
alpha-1-glycoprotein In this paper, alpha-1-glycoprotein refers to AGP/AAG, a plasma binding protein whose concentration can change with organ impairment. The hepatic impairment section says liver cirrhosis is characterized by progressive decline in AGP along with albumin, hematocrit, hepatic blood flow, functional hepatocytes, and biliary excretion. Direction matters: renal insufficiency is expected to increase AAG, whereas cirrhosis is described as reducing AGP.
intra-hepatic vascular resistance Resistance to blood flow within the liver vasculature, increased by fibrosis, nodules, and architectural distortion in cirrhosis. The paper connects cirrhotic tissue/nodules to hepatic venous outflow obstruction, increased intrahepatic vascular resistance, and raised sinusoidal pressure. Higher resistance changes hepatic blood flow and can make hepatic clearance predictions harder, especially for flow-sensitive drugs.
hepatic venous outflow obstruction Impaired drainage of blood out of the liver through the hepatic venous system. In the paper’s cirrhosis physiology, connective tissue and nodules can obstruct hepatic venous outflow, thereby raising intrahepatic resistance and sinusoidal pressure. This is part of portal-hypertensive physiology and helps explain why hepatic impairment is not just “less enzyme.”
sinusoidal pressure Pressure within hepatic sinusoids, the small vascular channels where blood interacts with hepatocytes. The article notes that increased intrahepatic resistance raises sinusoidal pressure, which can trigger formation or insertion of porto-caval shunting to decompress the system. Elevated sinusoidal pressure can redirect blood away from hepatocytes, changing first-pass and systemic hepatic clearance.
side-to-side porto-caval shunt A portosystemic connection between portal venous flow and the caval venous system that decompresses portal/sinusoidal pressure by bypassing part of the liver. The paper describes it as a compensatory or inserted shunt that relieves sinusoidal pressure, but becomes insufficient with progressive cirrhosis and altered splanchnic/peripheral flow. For PK modeling, shunting matters because portal blood may bypass hepatic metabolism and transporter exposure.

Interpretation notes

The pathophysiology terms are model inputs. Uremic toxins, plasma protein changes, loss of liver parenchyma, altered hepatic blood flow, vascular resistance, and shunting are not just background pathology; they are mechanisms PBPK models try to encode when predicting AUC and Cmax in organ impairment.
Hepatic impairment is the harder extrapolation. The paper reports better prediction performance for renal impairment than hepatic impairment, with overprediction more common in moderate and severe hepatic impairment. Terms related to cirrhosis vascular architecture help explain that complexity.
Terminology normalization. Alpha-acidic glycoprotein and alpha-1-glycoprotein in the requested list should be read as alpha-1-acid glycoprotein / AGP / AAG in the pharmacokinetic binding-protein sense.
