One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a conceptual and benchmark-design paper for clinical LLM safety. It is not practice-changing clinical evidence.

Core thesis. Chain-of-thought reasoning LLMs can appear less cognitively biased than physicians in paired clinical vignettes, but the mechanism is often intransigence: the model gives the same all-or-none recommendation across scenario variants instead of preserving reasonable clinical diversity. DeepSeek-R1 extinguished 6 of 10 physician-observed biases, yet four persisted: post hoc fallacy, decoy effects, Occam’s razor fallacy, and hindsight bias. OpenAI o1 and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking showed broadly similar concerns, although the pattern of persistent biases differed by model.

Why it matters. Many clinical AI safety narratives imply that “reasoning” models should be less prone to human cognitive biases. This paper adds a needed counterpoint: reduced bias on a discrepancy metric can come from rigid recommendation behavior, not from calibrated judgment. For clinical decision support, the operational warning is to test not only whether paired prompts change the answer, but also whether the model appropriately expresses uncertainty, acknowledges alternatives, and adapts to clinically meaningful patient differences.

Best use

Evaluation design for LLM clinical reasoning systems, prompt-harness testing, safety reviews, and teaching about “bias reduction by rigidity.”

Do not use for

Choosing a model for live clinical decision support, accepting model recommendations as more rational than physicians, or modifying patient-care workflows.

Most reusable concept

Measure both paired-scenario bias and response diversity/intransigence; a model can score well on one while failing the other.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate. The paper is clear and quantitative, but the uploaded file lacks the online supplement and the study design is narrow.

Section condensation

Abstract

The study asks whether chain-of-thought reasoning LLMs mitigate cognitive biases observed in practicing physicians. Ten paired medical scenarios were presented to reasoning models from DeepSeek, OpenAI, and Google; each pair differed by a specific bias-inducing feature. Responses were classified, bias was measured as absolute discrepancy between paired scenario versions, intransigence was measured with Shannon entropy, and deviance was measured against practicing physician responses from prior source scenarios (n=2507).

DeepSeek-R1 mitigated 6 of 10 physician-observed biases, but did so largely through all-or-none recommendations. Four biases persisted in DeepSeek-R1: post hoc fallacy (34% vs 0%, p<0.001), decoy effects (44% vs 5%, p<0.001), Occam’s razor fallacy (100% vs 0%, p<0.001), and hindsight bias (56% vs 0%, p<0.001). The authors conclude that clinicians should remain vigilant because reasoning models can be biased and intransigent in nuanced medical decisions.

Introduction

The introduction positions chain-of-thought reasoning as a newer LLM capability that may improve complex task performance and resembles structured clinical reasoning. The paper contrasts this promise with evidence that both physicians and earlier LLMs can express cognitive biases, including framing effects and other decision distortions in uncertain medical scenarios.

The key gap is whether reasoning-oriented models avoid these biases or merely reproduce new failure modes. The rationale is clinically relevant: LLMs are increasingly used to inform differential diagnosis, treatment plans, discharge summaries, and decision support, yet apparent expertise can mask errors when nuanced cases require judgment rather than rote guideline application.

Methods: scenarios and models

The authors developed 10 nuanced medical scenarios spanning multiple specialties and decisions. Each scenario appeared in two versions differing by one cognitive-bias stimulus, such as survival versus mortality framing. The scenarios were adapted from earlier sources that identified cognitive biases in practicing physicians, with the physician comparator sample reported as n=2507. Scenarios were presented without learning examples, conversation history, or non-text information.

DeepSeek-R1 was the primary model and was evaluated as 100 synthetic physicians per scenario version. Validation used OpenAI o1 and Google Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking with the same synthetic-physician approach. Model parameters were kept at default, including mid-range randomness for DeepSeek-R1. DeepSeek-R1’s API displayed reasoning traces, allowing the authors to inspect chain-of-thought text; OpenAI and Google reasoning traces were not available.

Methods: prompting and statistical analysis

The prompt framing used a simple professional role: “You are a physician,” followed by the clinical scenario and a postscript asking for a clear recommendation. Alternative persona prompts varying training, specialty, experience, and location reportedly produced no major differences and were discarded for simplicity. Responses were classified with a validated text-analysis algorithm described in the online supplement.

The primary comparison contrasted model responses across the two versions of each scenario. Bias was the absolute percentage-point discrepancy between paired versions; lower discrepancy indicates less stimulus sensitivity. Intransigence was quantified with Shannon entropy, with lower entropy indicating more uniform recommendations. Deviance compared average model responses with average practicing physician responses. P values and 95% CIs were calculated as independent analyses with no adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Results: DeepSeek-R1 reasoning overview and recommendation diversity

DeepSeek-R1 generated 2000 clinical responses averaging 248 words, equivalent to 554 tokens, with coefficient of variation 28%. Reasoning traces averaged 1845 words with coefficient of variation 52%; 91% contained at least one rhetorical question. Carotid endarterectomy required the most reasoning effort and vitamin supplementation the least, reported as 1827 versus 651 words respectively.

Vague non-answers were rare at 1.5%, but recommendation diversity was low: Shannon entropy 0.37 for DeepSeek-R1 versus 0.89 for practicing physicians. In the aggregate–individual discrepancy example, DeepSeek-R1 rejected testing in both versions (0% vs 0%, p=1.000), whereas physicians were more prone to testing the individual patient than the aggregate group (30% vs 17%, p=0.004). This illustrates the core finding: bias can disappear because the model refuses to move.

Results: extinguished and persistent biases

DeepSeek-R1 extinguished 6 of 10 physician-observed biases. Examples include framing effects, where physicians favored surgery more when framed by survival than mortality (84% vs 50%, p<0.001), while DeepSeek-R1 almost always recommended surgery (98% vs 100%, p=0.498); capitulating-to-pressure, where physicians ordered more cardiac tests when pressured (65% vs 52%, p=0.004), while the model almost never ordered them (0% vs 3%, p=0.246); and base-rate neglect, where the model judged probability correctly in both prevalence conditions (100% vs 100%, p=1.000).

Four DeepSeek-R1 biases persisted: post hoc fallacy for vitamin B12 continuation (34% vs 0%, p<0.001), decoy effects for firefighter prioritisation (44% vs 5%, p<0.001), Occam’s razor fallacy (100% vs 0%, p<0.001), and hindsight bias (56% vs 0%, p<0.001). Reasoning effort did not significantly differ between persistent and extinguished biases (1301 vs 998 words, p=0.332), weakening any simple claim that longer reasoning alone prevents bias.

Results: chain-of-thought details and model replication

DeepSeek-R1 reasoning traces often contained self-reflective terms: “wait” appeared in 81%, “revise” in 15%, and “double-check” in 3%. The model often referenced clinical trials; for example, the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial appeared in 94% of reasoning traces for the endarterectomy scenario. It sometimes referenced society guidelines, such as ACC/AHA guidance in preoperative assessment. Explicit cognitive-bias terms were rare except the placebo effect, which appeared in 53% of vitamin-supplementation reasoning traces.

OpenAI o1 responses averaged 140 words, vague non-answers were 0.9%, and Shannon entropy was 0.24. It extinguished 8 of 10 biases, with notable persistent Occam’s razor fallacy (100% vs 0%, p<0.001) and hindsight bias (88% vs 6%, p<0.001). Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking responses averaged 501 words, vague non-answers were 2.2%, and entropy was 0.61. Most physician-observed biases persisted in Gemini 2.0 except base-rate neglect and hindsight bias; Occam’s razor fallacy remained large (100% vs 27%, p<0.001), and status quo bias was more modest (100% vs 89%, p<0.001).

Discussion and limitations

The discussion interprets the findings as evidence that reasoning LLMs can mitigate some cognitive biases by producing hardline recommendations. The authors argue this can be beneficial when evidence-based conclusions are established but hazardous in nuanced medicine, where reasonable clinicians can disagree and individual patient values may matter. The paper emphasizes that reliance on detailed reasoning text may create an illusion of expertise and that people may accept AI recommendations more readily when they align with intuition.

Stated limitations include the use of only 10 scenarios, brief vignette language, model-version and prompt sensitivity, comparison against physician groups rather than individual physician behavior, no multiple-comparison adjustment, only three evaluated models, no evaluation of human-AI team workflows, and default parameters without fine-tuning. Additional practical limitations are that the uploaded PDF does not include the online supplement and that raw model outputs were available only by request under a data-use agreement.

AI/ML appraisal lens

Task

Paired-vignette evaluation of whether reasoning LLMs alter clinical recommendations when scenarios differ only by a bias-inducing stimulus.

Data

Ten medical scenario pairs adapted from prior physician-bias studies; physician comparator responses from practicing physicians reported as n=2507. No real patient data or EHR context.

Models

DeepSeek-R1 as primary; OpenAI o1 and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking as validation models. Default parameters used.

Prompting

Synthetic physician role prompt: “You are a physician,” with a clear-recommendation postscript. No few-shot examples, no conversation history, no non-text inputs.

Metrics

Absolute discrepancy between paired versions for bias; Shannon entropy for intransigence; deviance versus practicing physicians; response classification by validated text-analysis algorithm.

Generalization risk

High. Scenario bank is small; output behavior is model-version dependent; prompt wording and deployment context may change results.

What not to overclaim. The study does not prove that chain-of-thought models are safer than physicians, that intransigent recommendations are wrong in every case, or that any tested model is appropriate for unsupervised clinical decision support.

Main strengths

Clear paired-vignette design; explicit comparison with physician responses; interpretable primary metrics; replication across three reasoning-model families; strong focus on clinical safety nuance rather than raw accuracy.

Main limitations

Small scenario set; brief artificial scenarios; historical physician comparator; unadjusted multiple testing; limited reproducibility without supplement/raw outputs; no deployed workflow evaluation.

Evidence maturity

Hypothesis-generating to benchmark-useful. Strong enough to influence evaluation design; not strong enough for clinical deployment decisions.

Bottom line

Reasoning-model evaluations should include paired bias tests, response-diversity metrics, uncertainty behavior, and “reasonable alternative” checks before clinical use.

Implementation and practice takeaways

For clinical AI evaluation

Add paired-vignette tests for framing, pressure, hindsight, base-rate neglect, post hoc reasoning, status quo, and decoy effects. Score both answer shift and entropy/diversity.

For prompt harnesses

Do not reward only answer consistency. Require the model to state uncertainty, clinically plausible alternatives, what evidence would change the recommendation, and when clinician judgment should override.

For workflow governance

Version-lock tested models, retain prompts/outputs, monitor for drift, and include disagreement review when a model gives hardline recommendations in preference-sensitive cases.

For clinical practice

Do not change patient care based on this paper alone. Use it as a caution that detailed reasoning text and confident recommendations can be misleading in nuanced scenarios.

For research follow-up

Test larger scenario banks, real clinical notes, multimodal inputs, patient-preference variation, team decision-making, and mitigation strategies such as counterargument generation or ensemble disagreement.

For reading priority

Read full text if building or evaluating a clinical LLM system. Skim deeply if the immediate task is paper surveillance or AI-safety concept capture.

Actionable evaluation rule. A clinical LLM should not pass a bias evaluation solely because it gives the same answer to both versions of a vignette; the evaluation also needs to ask whether the answer is justified, flexible when patient-specific facts matter, and appropriately uncertain.
