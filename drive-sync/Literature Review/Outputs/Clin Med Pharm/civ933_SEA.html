One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Candidiasis is not a single treatment problem: initial drug choice, step-down, duration, source control, susceptibility testing, and diagnostic interpretation change materially with host status, Candida species, site of infection, and tissue/urinary drug penetration. The guideline’s practical architecture is therefore “site + host + species/susceptibility + source control + clearance,” not a universal antifungal ladder.

What carries the guideline

Candidemia bundleEchinocandin-first for most adults; species/susceptibility testing; clearance cultures; source control; step-down when stable and susceptible; 14 days after clearance for uncomplicated disease.
Site penetration mattersEchinocandins are strong systemic agents but poor choices for urine, eye, and CNS; fluconazole is unusually useful in urine and penetrates CSF/vitreous; amphotericin formulation matters by site.
Colonization ≠ invasive diseaseRespiratory Candida almost always reflects colonization; asymptomatic candiduria usually does too. These are major antifungal-stewardship opportunities.
Source control is a treatmentCatheter removal, abdominal drainage/debridement, infected cardiac hardware removal, joint drainage, obstruction relief, and CNS device removal repeatedly determine success.
Evidence maturity is unevenCandidemia and mucosal candidiasis have randomized-trial support; endocarditis, osteoarticular, CNS, ocular, and other rare manifestations rely heavily on case series, extrapolation, and expert judgment.
Diagnostics remain imperfectBlood cultures are insensitive overall for invasive candidiasis; nonculture tests can accelerate diagnosis but have specificity, standardization, and population-selection limitations.

Scope and clinical question frame

This is a multi-PICO guideline rather than a single trial. The 17 questions cover nonneutropenic and neutropenic candidemia; CVC management; hepatosplenic disease; ICU empiric therapy and prophylaxis; neonatal disease; intra-abdominal disease; respiratory isolation; intravascular, bone/joint, eye, CNS, urinary, vulvovaginal, oropharyngeal, and esophageal candidiasis. The panel repeatedly distinguishes common syndromes with trial data from rare syndromes where treatment is necessarily extrapolated.

Guideline development and methods

Panel and process. IDSA convened 12 candidiasis experts: 11 adult infectious-diseases physicians and 1 pediatric infectious-diseases physician. Members were assigned topic areas, reviewed literature, evaluated evidence, graded recommendations, and drafted evidence summaries. The panel met face-to-face twice and held conference calls over a two-year period; discrepancies were resolved by discussion and final recommendations represented panel consensus. Three external peer reviewers provided feedback, and the document was reviewed/approved through IDSA governance and endorsed by AAP, PIDS, and MSG.

Search strategy and GRADE. PubMed/Medline was searched for each PICO question using strategies developed by two independent health-sciences librarians. All languages and publication years were eligible. Initial searches were constructed August–November 2013 and finalized/delivered late November 2013 through January 2014; authors continued to add relevant literature afterward. GRADE was used to separate evidence certainty (very low/low/moderate/high) from recommendation strength (weak/strong). The guideline explicitly notes that recommendation strength is not dictated solely by evidence certainty; benefit-harm balance, patient values/preferences, and resources also contribute.

Methodological constraint. The published article provides a clear search and consensus framework, but the main text does not present a full systematic-review evidence profile or risk-of-bias table for every PICO. Much of the rare-disease guidance therefore depends on transparent expert synthesis rather than reproducible pooled effect estimates.
Conflict-of-interest process

The panel followed IDSA disclosure requirements, with review of financial and other relationships and case-by-case decisions about whether roles should be limited. The article lists multiple industry relationships among several panel members while also describing the society-level COI review process. Transparency is a strength; it does not by itself remove the potential for influence.

I

Candidemia in nonneutropenic patients

Recommendations 1–1212 strong; 0 weak4 high; 3 moderate; 6 low grade clauses

Source synthesis. The guideline makes an echinocandin the preferred initial regimen for most nonneutropenic adults with candidemia, while allowing fluconazole as initial therapy in selected stable patients unlikely to harbor a resistant species. It recommends susceptibility testing, early step-down to fluconazole when the patient is stable and the isolate is susceptible with bloodstream clearance, and lipid amphotericin B for intolerance or suspected multidrug resistance. Follow-up cultures are advised daily or every other day; uncomplicated candidemia is treated for 14 days after documented clearance and resolution of attributable symptoms.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. This is one of the best-supported sections in the guideline. Randomized candidemia trials generally showed treatment success in the same broad range without a single universally superior agent; pooled analyses favored initial echinocandin therapy as a predictor of survival. The panel still emphasizes patient-specific factors—recent azole/echinocandin exposure, severity, species and susceptibility, organ involvement, and local epidemiology—because species distribution and resistance vary by setting.

Practice translation. Operationalize as a bundle: start appropriate systemic therapy promptly, obtain species/susceptibility data, document clearance cultures, reassess for step-down, look for metastatic sites, and define the treatment clock from clearance rather than from the first dose.
II

Central venous catheter removal in nonneutropenic candidemia

Recommendations 131 strong1 moderate

Source synthesis. When a central venous catheter is presumed to be the source and can be removed safely, the guideline recommends removal as early as possible, with individualized judgment rather than an absolute rule detached from source likelihood and access needs.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The evidence is not based on a prospective trial designed around catheter removal. Retrospective analyses were heterogeneous, although most showed shorter candidemia duration and/or a trend toward improved outcomes, and a pooled analysis of seven candidemia trials found a survival association with catheter removal at some point during treatment. The evidence supports source control while leaving room for clinical judgment.

Practice translation. Treat catheter management as source-control reasoning, not a checkbox. Record whether the line is plausibly infected, whether safe removal is feasible, and whether persistent candidemia changes that assessment.
III

Candidemia in neutropenic patients

Recommendations 14–236 strong; 4 weak2 moderate; 8 low

Source synthesis. The guideline favors an echinocandin for initial therapy, with lipid amphotericin B as an effective but less attractive alternative. Fluconazole has a narrower role—principally stable patients without prior azole exposure or as step-down after susceptibility and clearance are established. Treatment continues for at least 14 days after clearance, resolution of attributable symptoms, and recovery from neutropenia. Eye examination is deferred until neutrophil recovery, and catheter removal is individualized because gastrointestinal translocation is often a competing source.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The section is constrained by the absence of adequately powered randomized trials focused on neutropenic candidemia. Recommendations are derived from small neutropenic subgroups of larger trials, pooled analyses, retrospective cohorts, and empiric-therapy studies. Recovery of neutrophils is repeatedly associated with treatment success, which explains the emphasis on hematologic recovery and the optional role of granulocyte transfusions in selected persistent cases.

Practice translation. Do not mechanically import the nonneutropenic catheter rule. Track neutrophil trajectory, prior prophylaxis, species susceptibility, and whether persistent fungemia reflects gut translocation, line infection, or deep-seated disease.
IV

Chronic disseminated (hepatosplenic) candidiasis

Recommendations 24–273 strong; 1 weak4 low

Source synthesis. Initial therapy is a lipid amphotericin B formulation or an echinocandin for several weeks, followed by fluconazole when resistance is unlikely. Therapy should continue until lesions resolve on repeat imaging—often for months. Necessary chemotherapy or hematopoietic-cell transplantation should not be delayed solely because of hepatosplenic candidiasis; antifungal therapy continues through the high-risk period. Short courses of anti-inflammatory therapy may be considered for debilitating persistent fevers.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The evidence base is largely case reports and open-label series. The syndrome often emerges with neutrophil recovery, and the guideline discusses an immune-reconstitution component to persistent fever and hepatic inflammation. This explains strong practical recommendations despite low-quality direct comparative evidence.

Practice translation. Expect long treatment horizons and image-guided stopping decisions. Separate microbiologic control from inflammatory persistence, and avoid unnecessary delay of cancer therapy when antifungal treatment can continue.
V

Empiric therapy for suspected invasive candidiasis in nonneutropenic ICU patients

Recommendations 28–335 strong; 1 weak3 moderate; 3 low

Source synthesis. Empiric antifungal therapy is recommended for selected critically ill patients with meaningful risk factors and no alternative explanation for fever, with rapid initiation in patients with compatible risk factors and septic shock. An echinocandin is preferred; fluconazole is an alternative when recent azole exposure and resistant colonization are absent. If the patient improves, a 2-week course is suggested; if there is no response after 4–5 days and subsequent evidence for invasive candidiasis is lacking—or a high-negative-predictive-value nonculture test is negative—stopping therapy should be considered.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The source stresses how difficult this decision is: risk-factor models often trade high specificity for poor sensitivity, colonization alone is inadequate, and blood cultures are insensitive and slow. The guideline cites very high mortality in Candida septic shock when source control and active therapy are delayed, but acknowledges that prospective empiric-therapy trials and predictive tools have produced conflicting results.

Practice translation. Use empiric therapy as a time-limited diagnostic-therapeutic trial with an explicit reassessment date. Risk factors should raise suspicion; they should not become an automatic antifungal trigger without a stop rule.
VI

ICU prophylaxis

Recommendations 34–360 strong; 3 weak2 moderate; 1 low

Source synthesis. Fluconazole prophylaxis may be used in high-risk adult ICU populations when the unit has a high invasive candidiasis rate (>5%); an echinocandin is an alternative. Daily chlorhexidine bathing is also presented as an option to reduce bloodstream infections including candidemia.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. All recommendations are weak. The value of prophylaxis depends heavily on baseline event rate, patient selection, resistance ecology, drug exposure, and local epidemiology. This is an explicit example of why the guideline repeatedly directs clinicians to local Candida epidemiology rather than universal prophylaxis.

Practice translation. Do not deploy prophylaxis without measuring local incidence and defining the target population. Stewardship should monitor breakthrough species, azole exposure, and whether the baseline >5% threshold remains true.
VII

Neonatal candidiasis, CNS disease, and NICU prophylaxis

Recommendations 37–5312 strong; 5 weak1 high; 5 moderate; 11 low

Source synthesis. For neonatal disseminated candidiasis, amphotericin B deoxycholate 1 mg/kg/day is preferred, with fluconazole 12 mg/kg/day as an alternative when prior fluconazole prophylaxis is absent; lipid amphotericin B is used cautiously, particularly with urinary involvement. Lumbar puncture, retinal examination, imaging for persistent fungemia, and CVC removal are emphasized. CNS disease is treated initially with amphotericin B, with fluconazole step-down for susceptible isolates, and therapy continues until clinical, CSF, and radiologic abnormalities resolve. In nurseries with invasive candidiasis rates >10%, fluconazole prophylaxis is recommended for neonates <1000 g.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The section reflects neonatal-specific pathophysiology and pharmacology rather than simple extrapolation from adults. The guideline notes that CNS disease can occur without positive blood cultures and that very-low-birth-weight infants have major mortality and neurodevelopmental risk. Treatment evidence nevertheless relies substantially on small trials and multicenter cohorts; one comparative-effectiveness analysis in the source found worse outcomes with lipid amphotericin B than with deoxycholate amphotericin B or fluconazole.

Practice translation. Treat neonatal candidemia as potentially disseminated/CNS disease. Weight, gestational maturity, renal function, prophylaxis history, CNS evaluation, and line removal are core parts of the treatment plan.
VIII

Intra-abdominal candidiasis

Recommendations 54–574 strong3 moderate; 1 low

Source synthesis. Empiric antifungal therapy should be considered in patients with clinical intra-abdominal infection plus major Candida risk factors such as recent abdominal surgery, anastomotic leak, or necrotizing pancreatitis. Treatment requires source control through drainage and/or debridement. Antifungal selection follows candidemia/ICU empiric principles, and duration is determined by source-control adequacy and clinical response rather than a fixed calendar duration.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The guideline explicitly characterizes the clinical evidence as limited: many studies are small, uncontrolled, single-center, or population-specific. Sterile-site abdominal cultures obtained at operation or from drains placed within 24 hours are treated as clinically meaningful when infection is present, whereas older drains are difficult to interpret. Prophylaxis meta-analyses in high-risk surgical ICU populations were conflicting.

Practice translation. Source control is the load-bearing intervention. Document specimen provenance (sterile operative specimen vs longstanding drain), anatomic control, species/susceptibility, and the reason therapy is being continued.
IX

Candida isolated from the respiratory tract

Recommendations 581 strong1 moderate

Source synthesis. Growth of Candida from respiratory secretions usually represents colonization and rarely warrants antifungal therapy. In severely immunocompromised patients it should prompt evaluation for invasive candidiasis rather than automatic treatment of “Candida pneumonia.”

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The supporting evidence is unusually concrete for a stewardship recommendation: diagnosis of Candida pneumonia requires histopathologic invasion, and autopsy studies show very poor predictive value for sputum/BAL Candida. One prospective autopsy study cited by the guideline found no Candida pneumonia among 77 ICU decedents who had clinical/radiologic pneumonia plus Candida recovered from BAL or sputum.

Practice translation. This is a high-value antifungal-stewardship rule. A respiratory Candida culture should usually trigger “colonization unless proven invasive,” not a new antifungal order.
X

Candida intravascular infections: endocarditis, cardiac devices, thrombophlebitis

Recommendations 59–7314 strong; 1 weak1 moderate; 13 low; 1 very low

Source synthesis. Candida endocarditis is treated with lipid amphotericin B (with or without flucytosine) or a high-dose echinocandin, with step-down to an active oral azole after stability and bloodstream clearance. Valve replacement is recommended when feasible; long-term suppressive fluconazole is used when surgery is impossible and for prosthetic-valve disease. Pacemaker/ICD infection requires complete device removal, while nonremovable ventricular-assist devices generally require chronic suppression. Suppurative thrombophlebitis requires catheter removal and drainage/resection when feasible plus prolonged systemic therapy.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. Most recommendations rest on case reports, case series, retrospective reviews, animal/biofilm rationale, and clinical experience rather than controlled trials. The source explicitly states that medical therapy alone has failed in reported cardiac-device infection cases and that relapse of Candida endocarditis can occur months to years later, supporting prolonged follow-up and suppression in selected patients.

Practice translation. Hardware and source control dominate management. Separate an induction regimen, surgical/device strategy, step-down eligibility, suppressive plan, and long-term relapse surveillance.
XI

Candida osteomyelitis and septic arthritis

Recommendations 74–816 strong; 2 weak2 moderate; 6 low

Source synthesis. Osteomyelitis is treated with fluconazole for 6–12 months or an initial echinocandin followed by prolonged fluconazole; lipid amphotericin B induction is a less attractive alternative. Selected cases require surgical debridement. Septic arthritis is treated for about 6 weeks, with drainage in all cases; prosthetic-joint infection generally requires device removal, with chronic fluconazole suppression when removal is impossible and the isolate is susceptible.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. Evidence is mostly case reports/series and extrapolation. The prolonged durations reflect difficult tissue infection and relapse risk more than randomized duration trials. Surgical recommendations are based on source-control principles and accumulated clinical experience.

Practice translation. Do not reduce these infections to a drug-choice problem. Establish anatomy, hardware status, drainage/debridement feasibility, susceptibility, and a months-long adherence/monitoring plan where required.
XII

Candida endophthalmitis

Recommendations 82–9110 strong10 low

Source synthesis. The guideline recommends ophthalmologic assessment for candidemic patients—within the first week for nonneutropenic patients and after neutrophil recovery for neutropenic patients. Therapy depends on chorioretinitis, macular involvement, and vitritis. Fluconazole or voriconazole is used for susceptible isolates; liposomal amphotericin B with or without flucytosine is used for resistant isolates. Macular involvement or vitritis adds intravitreal amphotericin B or voriconazole, and vitrectomy is considered for vitritis. Treatment lasts at least 4–6 weeks and continues according to lesion resolution.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. All ten recommendations are strong despite low-quality evidence. The section is driven by the high consequence of missed sight-threatening disease, ocular penetration differences among antifungals, and case-series experience rather than comparative ophthalmic trials. This is a clear example of GRADE strength reflecting benefit-harm judgment beyond evidence certainty alone.

Practice translation. Treatment must be anatomically specific. “Candidemia + eye findings” needs ophthalmology/ID co-management, classification of macular/vitreous involvement, and an agent that reaches the relevant ocular compartment.
XIII

Central nervous system candidiasis

Recommendations 92–964 strong; 1 weak5 low

Source synthesis. Initial therapy is liposomal amphotericin B 5 mg/kg/day with or without flucytosine, followed by fluconazole 400–800 mg/day after response. Therapy continues until signs, symptoms, CSF abnormalities, and radiologic abnormalities resolve. Infected CNS devices should be removed when possible; intraventricular amphotericin B is a weakly supported option when a ventricular device cannot be removed.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The evidence is low quality and is shaped by pharmacokinetic penetration, animal data, case reports, and experience with device-associated infection. The stopping rule is resolution-based rather than a fixed duration because CNS infection is heterogeneous and difficult to sterilize.

Practice translation. Prioritize penetration and hardware removal. Define what “resolved” means prospectively across clinical status, CSF, and imaging so therapy is not stopped on symptoms alone.
XIV

Candida urinary tract infections

Recommendations 97–11415 strong; 3 weak1 moderate; 17 low

Source synthesis. Asymptomatic candiduria is usually managed by eliminating predisposing factors—especially bladder catheters—rather than antifungal therapy. Treatment is reserved for groups at high risk for dissemination, including neutropenic patients, very-low-birth-weight infants, and patients undergoing urologic manipulation. Symptomatic cystitis and pyelonephritis are treated with fluconazole when susceptible; amphotericin B deoxycholate and/or flucytosine are options for selected resistant species. Obstruction, stents, nephrostomy tubes, and fungus balls require mechanical source-control strategies.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. The section is mostly low-quality evidence but is pharmacologically coherent: fluconazole reaches urine concentrations far above serum, echinocandins generally do not, and lipid amphotericin B formulations have poor renal excretion. The guideline also emphasizes that candiduria often represents colonization and that urine tests are poor at distinguishing colonization from infection in catheterized patients.

Practice translation. First classify: asymptomatic colonization, cystitis, ascending pyelonephritis, hematogenous renal candidiasis, or obstructing fungus ball. Agent selection follows urinary exposure, not just systemic Candida susceptibility.
XV

Vulvovaginal candidiasis

Recommendations 115–1216 strong; 1 weak4 high; 3 low

Source synthesis. Uncomplicated disease can be treated with topical azoles or a single 150-mg fluconazole dose; severe disease receives repeated fluconazole doses. For azole-unresponsive C. glabrata, options include intravaginal boric acid, nystatin, or topical flucytosine with or without amphotericin B. Recurrent disease is managed with induction followed by weekly fluconazole for 6 months.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. Unlike many invasive-disease sections, common vulvovaginal candidiasis has a substantial randomized-trial evidence base. The guideline nevertheless stresses diagnostic confirmation because symptoms are nonspecific and recommends culture when microscopy is negative despite persistent suspicion. Evidence becomes much thinner for non-albicans and refractory disease.

Practice translation. Confirm the syndrome before repeated empiric therapy. Separate uncomplicated, severe, recurrent, and non-albicans disease because the regimen and evidence base differ materially.
XVI

Oropharyngeal candidiasis

Recommendations 122–1308 strong; 1 weak4 high; 5 moderate

Source synthesis. Mild disease is treated with topical clotrimazole or miconazole (with nystatin alternatives), while moderate-to-severe disease receives oral fluconazole. Fluconazole-refractory disease can be treated with itraconazole solution, posaconazole, voriconazole, amphotericin B suspension, or parenteral echinocandin/amphotericin B depending on the circumstance. Chronic suppression is usually unnecessary but can be used for recurrent infection; denture disinfection is recommended when relevant.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. This section is comparatively evidence-rich, with multiple controlled mucosal-candidiasis trials and extensive HIV-era experience. The guideline links recurrent/refractory disease to immune status and prior azole exposure, and notes that effective antiretroviral therapy reduces recurrence in HIV infection.

Practice translation. Match treatment intensity to severity and prior azole exposure. For repeated recurrence, reassess immune status, adherence, denture hygiene, species, and resistance rather than simply escalating dose.
XVII

Esophageal candidiasis

Recommendations 131–1409 strong; 1 weak7 high; 2 moderate; 1 low

Source synthesis. Systemic therapy is always required, and a diagnostic fluconazole trial may precede endoscopy in a compatible presentation. Oral fluconazole 200–400 mg/day for 14–21 days is preferred; IV fluconazole or an echinocandin is used when oral therapy is not possible, with step-down to oral fluconazole when feasible. Refractory disease is treated with itraconazole solution, voriconazole, an echinocandin, amphotericin B, or posaconazole depending on susceptibility and response; recurrent esophagitis may require suppressive fluconazole.

Evidence/appraisal within the source. This is one of the strongest evidence sections, supported by comparative treatment trials and a clinically useful response criterion: most patients improve within about 7 days of antifungal therapy. The guideline also notes the marked reduction in disease after effective antiretroviral therapy in HIV populations.

Practice translation. Use oral fluconazole when possible, but define a response checkpoint. Failure should trigger reconsideration of adherence, diagnosis, resistance, and need for endoscopy or alternative systemic therapy.

Purpose: Explain how evidence quality and recommendation strength are rated.

  Randomized trials → start HIGH
  Observational studies → start LOW

  Upgrade when appropriate for: large effect, dose-response,

  Considers evidence certainty + balance of benefits/harms/burdens

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first — with a currency check before present-day practice use. As a source document, this is exceptionally useful because it integrates therapeutic regimens, pharmacology, diagnostics, source control, species/susceptibility reasoning, and explicit GRADE labels across nearly the full Candida disease spectrum. Its central limitation in 2026 is age, not lack of clinical structure.
10/10
Relevance

Directly addresses diagnosis, antifungal selection, dosing, duration, source control, monitoring, and stewardship across major Candida syndromes.

Evidence basis: 17 clinical questions and 140 numbered recommendations. Limiting factor: relevance to current practice depends on post-2016 verification. Would lower: a task unrelated to candidiasis/antifungal stewardship.
5/10

Principal strengths

  • Decision architecture is clinically coherent: organism, host, infection site, susceptibility, source control, and drug penetration are repeatedly integrated rather than treated as separate facts.
  • GRADE labels are visible next to recommendations: the reader can see where strong advice rests on low-quality evidence.
  • Pharmacology is not decorative: urinary, ocular, and CNS recommendations explicitly depend on tissue exposure.
  • Stewardship boundaries are clear: respiratory colonization, asymptomatic candiduria, empiric-therapy stop rules, and targeted prophylaxis are all addressed.
  • Rare disease uncertainty is usually acknowledged: evidence summaries often state when recommendations rely on retrospective series, case reports, animal data, or expert experience.

Principal limitations

  • Age: the document is a 2016 update; antifungal resistance, susceptibility standards, diagnostics, newer agents, and professional-society positions can change.
  • Low certainty in many invasive syndromes: endocarditis, hardware infections, osteoarticular disease, endophthalmitis, CNS disease, and much of candiduria are dominated by low-quality evidence.
  • Strong/low-quality combinations are common: often defensible because the consequences of undertreatment or the source-control logic are compelling, but these should not be mistaken for high-certainty comparative evidence.
  • Guideline-development reporting is less granular than a modern systematic review: the article does not expose a detailed risk-of-bias assessment and evidence-to-decision table for every recommendation.
  • Panel composition: expertise is deep but concentrated in infectious-diseases physicians; the article does not describe direct patient representation in the panel.
Do not overclaim. This guideline does not prove that every strong recommendation has high-certainty evidence, that its 2016 breakpoints remain current, or that local Candida epidemiology can be ignored. It also does not make culture-negative invasive candidiasis easy to diagnose; it explicitly documents the limitations of the diagnostic gold standard.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Build a candidemia bundleInitial active therapy → species/susceptibility → repeat cultures → source control → metastatic-site assessment → step-down eligibility → duration from clearance.
Make “site” a first-class fieldUrine, CNS, eye, cardiac hardware, bone/joint, abdomen, and mucosa should route to different agents and stopping rules because penetration and source control differ.
Hard-code stewardship non-indications carefullyRespiratory Candida usually means colonization; asymptomatic candiduria usually does not require antifungals. Preserve high-risk exceptions.
Require a reassessment date for empiric antifungalsAt 4–5 days, explicitly re-evaluate clinical response, microbiology, imaging/source data, and high-NPV diagnostics rather than allowing indefinite empiricism.
Track source control separately from drug adequacyCatheters, abdominal collections, valves/devices, infected joints, urinary obstruction, and CNS hardware can make otherwise active antifungal therapy fail.
Separate source-era facts from current policyIf converting this guideline into CDS or institutional guidance, every breakpoint, dosing exception, diagnostic assay statement, and ophthalmology recommendation needs current-source verification before deployment.
