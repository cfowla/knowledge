4. One-page synthesis

The study evaluates a pragmatic EHR order-set intervention designed to reduce the dangerous mismatch between rocuronium paralysis duration and short induction-agent duration. The intervention linked rocuronium RSI orders to post-intubation sedation orders and added explicit initial-dose guidance, including a propofol starting rate of 20 mcg/kg/min when used with paralysis. In 713 adults, propofol use within 1 hour increased, very-low initial propofol starts decreased, time to the study-defined adequate sedation threshold shortened, and continuous vasopressor initiation did not increase.

The most practice-relevant finding is not the 2-minute reduction in time to any sedation, which the authors themselves regard as unlikely to be clinically meaningful, but the 18-minute reduction in time to study-defined adequate sedation: median 32 minutes preimplementation versus 14 minutes postimplementation. This aligns with known concern for AWP after rocuronium, but it remains a surrogate. The study cannot establish that the order panel reduced AWP, psychological trauma, oversedation, ventilator duration, or mortality.

5. Section-by-section condensation

Purpose / abstract

The authors frame AWP as a serious complication of RSI with neuromuscular blockers, especially rocuronium because its paralysis duration can outlast induction agents. They studied whether an RSI CDS update tied to rocuronium orders changed sedative selection and initial dosing in the first hour after paralysis.

The abstract reports 713 included patients: 308 preimplementation and 405 postimplementation. After CDS implementation, propofol use increased from 56.8% to 73.1%, median initial propofol rate increased from 5 to 20 mcg/kg/min, midazolam use decreased from 32.1% to 19.3%, time to adequate sedation decreased from 32 to 14 minutes, and hypotension requiring vasopressors was similar at roughly 25% in both periods.

Introduction / background

The clinical rationale is strong: rocuronium has rapid onset and prolonged paralysis, while induction sedatives such as etomidate and propofol have shorter durations. If post-intubation sedation is delayed or underdosed, a patient can remain conscious but unable to move, communicate, or breathe spontaneously.

The article cites prior estimates suggesting AWP in approximately 5% to 8% of patients receiving rocuronium for RSI, and discusses psychological harms including PTSD, anxiety, depression, and phobias. The local problem was low post-intubation propofol starts, including 5 mcg/kg/min, with an order panel that gave rapid access to RSI drugs but was not linked to post-intubation sedation.

Methods

This was a retrospective, multicenter, observational before-after study in a 5-hospital health system. The preimplementation cohort covered July 1, 2022 to January 1, 2023. The postimplementation cohort covered January 1 to July 1, 2024. The order-set change occurred in stages: February 13, 2023 linked sedation to rocuronium and increased default guidance; December 12, 2023 added explicit nursing-facing instructions that propofol should start at 20 mcg/kg/min when used with paralysis for RSI.

Medication data were collected from 15 minutes before through 120 minutes after rocuronium. Adequate sedation was defined operationally as propofol at least 20 mcg/kg/min or intermittent IV midazolam boluses of 4 mg once followed by 2 mg 30 minutes later. Hypotension was defined as MAP below 65 mm Hg in the first hour after sedative initiation requiring continuous vasopressor initiation. Push-dose vasopressors, objective sedation measures such as BIS, and actual AWP events were not included.

Results

Baseline age, sex, height, weight, BMI, ventilator duration, and intubation location were similar between cohorts. ICU intubations represented about 55% of both cohorts and ED intubations about 42%. The postimplementation group had higher weight-based rocuronium dosing and a shift in induction agent use: etomidate decreased and ketamine increased.

After implementation, propofol became more common and initial propofol rates increased. Time to any sedation decreased by 2 minutes, while time to adequate sedation decreased by 18 minutes. Continuous vasopressor initiation within 1 hour of sedation did not differ. The percentage receiving any sedation within 1 hour improved from 78.6% to 84.7%, leaving 15.3% of postimplementation patients without documented sedation within 1 hour after rocuronium.

Discussion / interpretation

The authors argue that higher initial propofol rates, fewer titrations, and shorter time to adequate sedation support CDS as a safety strategy in the peri-intubation period. They emphasize that the reduction in time to adequate sedation is more clinically meaningful than the smaller change in time to any sedation.

Important confounders include the order panel's simultaneous rocuronium default-dose change, etomidate shortage, evolving ketamine evidence, clinicians from multiple specialties, and retrospective documentation timing. The study's central clinical assumption is plausible, but the outcome tested was a sedation-process surrogate rather than AWP prevention.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that the updated RSI order panel led to faster initiation of higher sedative doses without increasing measured hemodynamic instability. They recommend future studies directly assessing AWP and long-term psychological outcomes.

The practice interpretation should be narrower: the article supports building and auditing post-rocuronium sedation CDS, but it does not establish a patient-outcome benefit or a universal sedation regimen.

9. What should and should not change in practice

Reasonable practice change: Hospitals using rocuronium for RSI should audit whether post-intubation sedation is ordered, available, started, and titrated fast enough to cover the expected paralysis window. If gaps are present, a rocuronium-triggered CDS/order-panel build with default sedative guidance is reasonable.

Should change

  • Add linked post-intubation sedation orders to rocuronium RSI pathways, especially in ED/ICU order sets.
  • Default away from token propofol starts such as 5 mcg/kg/min when the intent is hypnosis during ongoing paralysis.
  • Include clear nursing instructions for initial rate, titration, hemodynamic contact parameters, and what to do when paralytic effect may persist.
  • Build audit metrics: any sedation within 10-15 minutes, adequate-dose sedation within 15 minutes, no sedation by 30/60 minutes, hypotension treatment, and AWP follow-up where feasible.
  • Use pharmacist presence or remote verification to support high-risk intubations, but design the order set so safety does not depend entirely on pharmacist bedside availability.

Should not change based on this study alone

  • Do not claim that the CDS intervention reduces AWP, PTSD, mortality, ventilator duration, or ICU length of stay.
  • Do not force propofol 20 mcg/kg/min in every patient regardless of shock, bradycardia, vasopressor burden, or competing sedation strategy.
  • Do not interpret the study as evidence that rocuronium is superior to succinylcholine, or that ketamine is superior to etomidate.
  • Do not replace sedation assessment, analgesia assessment, or medication reconciliation with order defaults.
  • Do not treat the study definition of adequate sedation as proof of unconsciousness during paralysis.

10. Explicit warnings where evidence is insufficient for clinical action

Insufficient for clinical action: This study should not be used as direct evidence that the order panel prevents AWP. AWP was not measured, patient recall was not assessed, and long-term psychological outcomes were not collected.
Insufficient for universal dosing: The propofol 20 mcg/kg/min threshold and midazolam bolus sequence are operational definitions from the protocol and cited prior work; they are not a guarantee of unconsciousness during paralysis and require patient-specific titration.
Insufficient for safety equivalence: Similar continuous vasopressor initiation does not exclude clinically important hypotension, push-dose vasopressor use, fluid resuscitation, bradycardia, oversedation, delayed awakening, or other sedation-related harms.
Insufficient for causal certainty: The retrospective before-after design, simultaneous rocuronium default-dose change, induction-agent changes, possible etomidate shortage, and timestamp limitations prevent a strong causal claim.

Best use

Use this article to justify a local medication-use safety project: audit post-rocuronium sedation gaps, build linked sedation orders into RSI order panels, monitor hemodynamic balancing measures, and create feedback loops for ED/ICU/anesthesia/pharmacy/nursing stakeholders.

Do not use for

Do not use it as proof that a specific sedative dose prevents awareness, as a substitute for sedation monitoring, as comparative evidence among induction agents or neuromuscular blockers, or as a definitive safety analysis of higher initial propofol rates.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate. The main text, tables, and figures are available and internally consistent. Confidence is limited by the absence of the referenced eAppendix and the study's own limitations in outcome measurement.
