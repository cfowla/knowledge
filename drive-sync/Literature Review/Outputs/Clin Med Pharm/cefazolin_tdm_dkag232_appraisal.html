3. One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In a selected cohort of 70 patients receiving cefazolin TDM for severe infections, total cefazolin concentrations below the institution's target were common despite median daily doses around 6 g/day. Continuous infusion produced higher measured cefazolin levels than intermittent infusion at similar daily doses, and hypoalbuminemia correlated with lower total cefazolin concentrations in the albumin-measured subset.

The study is most useful as a stewardship and PK/TDM signal paper, not a practice-changing outcomes study. The population was clinically relevant: 76% had bloodstream infection, 46% had definitive endocarditis, and 86% had MSSA. Subtherapeutic total concentrations occurred in 39% of patients; therapeutic and supratherapeutic concentrations occurred in 37% and 24%, respectively. No patient required cefazolin discontinuation for toxicity.

The most actionable observation is the continuous-infusion association: among continuous versus intermittent recipients, median daily cefazolin dose was similar at 6 g/day, but median cefazolin concentration was higher with continuous infusion: 61 mcg/mL (IQR 38-80) versus 38 mcg/mL (IQR 19-71), P=0.012. The paper also flags high creatinine clearance as a likely exposure risk: patients with subtherapeutic levels had higher median creatinine clearance, 110 versus 67 mL/min, P=0.004.

Main caution. The study measured total cefazolin concentrations only. In hypoalbuminemia, total concentration can be difficult to interpret because altered protein binding changes the relation between total and free drug. The article supports concern about standard dosing and target attainment, but it does not prove that the 40-80 mcg/mL total target is clinically correct or that continuous infusion improves patient-centered outcomes.
Best useRationale for local discussion of cefazolin TDM, continuous infusion, and aggressive dosing in selected severe MSSA infections with high renal clearance or hypoalbuminemia.
Do not use forUniversal cefazolin continuous infusion, validated dose targets, or outcome-based superiority over intermittent dosing.
Clinical bottom lineUseful and relevant, but hypothesis-generating. Treat the 40-80 mcg/mL target as a working institutional target, not a validated outcome threshold.

5. Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract reports an observational experience with cefazolin TDM in severe infections and defines subtherapeutic total concentrations as <40 mg/L, equivalent to <40 mcg/mL. Troughs for intermittent regimens were accepted within 90 minutes before the next dose; steady-state continuous-infusion samples were drawn after at least 8 hours of infusion. Hypoalbuminemia was defined as albumin <3 g/dL within 72 hours of TDM.

Among 70 patients, 76% had bloodstream infection, 60% of those bloodstream infection cases had definitive endocarditis, and 86% had MSSA. Subtherapeutic levels occurred in 39%. Continuous infusion had higher median cefazolin concentrations than intermittent infusion despite similar median daily dose. The authors conclude that subtherapeutic levels were common and that hypoalbuminemia and intermittent infusion were associated with lower target attainment.

Introduction

The introduction frames cefazolin as a frontline therapy for MSSA bacteremia after a clinical trial showing non-inferiority to cloxacillin, while emphasizing unresolved questions about optimal dosing in complicated patients. The paper cites consensus guidance using a total cefazolin therapeutic range of 40-80 mcg/mL to achieve free drug concentrations above four times the pathogen MIC, based on high cefazolin protein binding.

The authors highlight PK/PD concerns relevant to severe staphylococcal infection: MSSA MIC90 around 1-2 mcg/mL at standard inoculum, possible high-inoculum MIC increases up to 32 mcg/mL, prior links between augmented renal clearance or obesity and low exposures, and observational associations between hypoalbuminemia and delayed blood-culture sterilization in cefazolin-treated MSSA bacteremia.

Methods

This was a prospective observational study of unique patients treated with cefazolin guided by TDM for bacteremia or other severe infections between August 2023 and March 2026 at two UPMC centers. Cefazolin levels were processed externally, and TDM requests were made by Infectious Diseases consultants and approved by an antimicrobial stewardship TDM team. Only the first cefazolin level was used; levels outside the prespecified sampling windows were excluded.

The local TDM team interpreted levels and adjusted doses after discussion with the ID consultant, but results became available roughly 72 hours after sampling. Cefazolin <40 mcg/mL was subtherapeutic, 40-80 mcg/mL therapeutic, and >80 mcg/mL supratherapeutic. Statistical comparisons used chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, and stepwise multivariable logistic regression for predictors of hypoalbuminemia among variables with bivariate P<0.10.

Results

Of 71 managed patients, 70 were analyzed. Bacteremia occurred in 53/70, definitive endocarditis in 32/70, and MSSA infection in 60/70. Continuous infusion was used in 48/70 patients; intermittent dosing was used in 22/70, including 9 prolonged infusions and 13 bolus infusions. Cefazolin concentrations were subtherapeutic in 27/70, therapeutic in 26/70, and supratherapeutic in 17/70.

Patients with subtherapeutic levels had higher median creatinine clearance, less AKI, fewer vasopressors, lower endocarditis frequency, and lower continuous-infusion use. Continuous infusion produced higher median levels than intermittent infusion at similar daily dose. Among patients with measured albumin, 40/55 had hypoalbuminemia; hypoalbuminemia was associated with endocarditis and, in multivariable modeling, lower cefazolin level. Mortality differences were not statistically significant.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the high subtherapeutic rate as evidence that standard cefazolin dosing can fail to achieve institutional target concentrations in selected severe infections. They argue that continuous infusion is more likely to achieve PK/PD targets and is preferred at their institution for severe infections with hypoalbuminemia, while noting peripheral continuous cefazolin has been reported as tolerated in outpatient therapy.

Limitations are substantial: small sample size, single health-system selection into TDM, total rather than free cefazolin levels, delayed result availability, no target-validation design, and no demonstrated mortality association. The paper explicitly states that future studies are needed to validate or refute cefazolin therapeutic targets and that MIC-specific targets may differ from the current broad 40-80 mcg/mL total range.

Funding, conflicts, supplementary data

The work had no direct funding and was reported as part of routine work. The transparency section lists multiple consulting, speaking, and investigator-initiated funding relationships for two authors; remaining authors reported no potential conflicts.

The article notes that Table S1 and assay details are available online as supplementary data. These supplemental materials were not included in the evaluated PDF, so they are not summarized here.

7. Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. This is a high-relevance stewardship/ID pharmacy paper for cefazolin TDM and continuous-infusion discussions, but the evidence is too small and observational to drive broad practice change by itself.
9/10RelevanceDirectly relevant to ID stewardship, MSSA bacteremia, cefazolin dosing, PK/PD, and pharmacist-led TDM. Evidence basis: severe-infection cohort with bacteremia/endocarditis concentration data. Limiting factor: selected TDM population. Raise/lower: multicenter pragmatic TDM protocol data with outcomes would raise; less severe infection mix would lower.
7/10NoveltyAdds real-world cefazolin TDM experience and a direct continuous-versus-intermittent exposure comparison in treatment rather than prophylaxis. Evidence basis: local implementation cohort and concentration categories. Limiting factor: supplementary Table S1 not evaluated here and sample is small. Raise/lower: unbound-level data and external cohorts would raise; duplicate single-center experience would lower.
5/10Method strengthProspective observational design and defined sampling windows are strengths, but treatment selection, TDM selection, and administration strategy were not randomized. Evidence basis: n=70, two centers, first eligible level, bivariate comparisons, regression focused on hypoalbuminemia. Limiting factor: confounding and limited multivariable modeling for the subtherapeutic endpoint. Raise/lower: randomized infusion strategy or robust multivariable PK model would raise; unclear sampling/dosing protocol would lower.
5/10Evidence strengthThe data support that subtherapeutic total levels were common and that continuous infusion was associated with higher levels, but clinical outcome linkage is weak. Evidence basis: 39% subtherapeutic rate, higher CI levels, non-significant 30-day mortality differences. Limiting factor: target validation absent and total concentrations only. Raise/lower: free cefazolin, MIC-linked target attainment, culture-clearance, relapse, toxicity, and mortality endpoints would raise; no assay reliability detail would lower.
4/10External validityThe cohort reflects real severe infections but comes from two sites in one health system and only patients selected for TDM. Evidence basis: UPMC-only workflow and external assay processing. Limiting factor: local stewardship practices and patient selection. Raise/lower: independent health-system replication and broader patient inclusion would raise; unavailable assay or no TDM infrastructure would lower.
7/10Implementation valueProvides enough operational detail to inform a local TDM pilot, sampling instructions, and a continuous-infusion discussion. Evidence basis: thresholds, sampling windows, turnaround, dose-change patterns, and subgroup signals are reported. Limiting factor: no validated dose-adjustment algorithm or free-level target. Raise/lower: nomogram, dosing algorithm, free-level strategy, and prospective outcomes evaluation would raise; lack of local assay access would lower.

Strengths

  • Clinically relevant population enriched for bacteremia, endocarditis, and MSSA.
  • Clear operational definitions for sampling windows and concentration categories.
  • Preserves actual dosing and route context, showing that identical nominal daily doses can yield different concentrations by infusion strategy.
  • Transparent about major limitations, including total-only concentrations and unvalidated targets.

Limitations and interpretive cautions

  • Small sample size and selected TDM population limit causal inference and generalizability.
  • Administration route was not randomized; continuous infusion selection may reflect clinician risk stratification or evolving institutional preference.
  • Total cefazolin level is an incomplete exposure marker in hypoalbuminemia because free fraction is not measured.
  • Approximately 72-hour assay turnaround reduces ability to intervene during the earliest bacteremia period.
  • The data do not validate the 40-80 mcg/mL target, four-times-MIC target, or a universal continuous-infusion strategy.
  • Clinical outcome evaluation is underpowered; mortality and sterilization endpoints cannot establish benefit from higher concentrations.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate. The main text gives enough information to appraise design, results, and tables, but the online supplement was not included in the evaluated PDF. Confidence would improve with assay-performance details, Table S1, and independent full-text supplementary review.

8. Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now as a signalStandard cefazolin 6 g/day can produce total concentrations below a 40 mcg/mL institutional target in selected severe infections, especially with higher renal clearance.
Build carefullyA local TDM pathway should define eligible patients, sampling windows, dose-adjustment rules, turnaround expectations, documentation, and stewardship ownership.
Prioritize free-level logicIn hypoalbuminemia, total concentrations alone are difficult to translate into active exposure. Consider whether the local assay or send-out strategy can provide unbound levels or validated interpretation rules.
Continuous infusion discussionContinuous infusion is plausible for severe MSSA, endocarditis, hypoalbuminemia, high clearance, or target-attainment concerns, but this paper alone does not prove clinical superiority.
Target cautionThe 40-80 mcg/mL total range should be treated as a working target pending validation against MIC, free concentrations, culture clearance, relapse, toxicity, and mortality.
Operational constraintIf results take roughly 72 hours, early empiric or definitive dosing strategy matters more than reactive TDM for the first several days.

Practical questions for a local stewardship review

  1. Which patients would qualify for cefazolin TDM: MSSA bacteremia, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, obesity, augmented renal clearance, hypoalbuminemia, renal replacement therapy, or persistent bacteremia?
  2. Would the institution use total or free cefazolin concentrations, and how would results be interpreted when albumin is low?
  3. What continuous-infusion dose, loading dose, line requirements, stability limits, nursing workflow, and outpatient infusion feasibility would apply?
  4. Which endpoint should define success: concentration attainment, time to culture sterilization, relapse, adverse events, length of stay, or mortality?
  5. How would cefazolin TDM integrate with beta-lactam allergy evaluation, MSSA bacteremia bundle management, source control, and ID follow-up?
