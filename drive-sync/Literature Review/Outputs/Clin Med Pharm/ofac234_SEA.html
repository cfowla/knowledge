One-page synthesis

Core thesis. When fungal diagnostics are embedded in active antifungal stewardship—rather than used as isolated laboratory tests—they can shorten identification/targeted-therapy time, support de-escalation or discontinuation, reduce antifungal exposure and cost, and potentially improve clinical outcomes. The strongest evidence in this review is for process and utilization outcomes; mortality and length-of-stay signals are mostly non-significant and causally uncertain.

The review identified 17 studies after screening 422 citations. Most were single-center studies from Europe or the United States/Japan, and 11 of 17 were limited to Candida-related indications. Diagnostic approaches ranged from conventional culture and imaging to BDG, galactomannan, T2Candida, MALDI-TOF, PCR, and PNA-FISH. Rapid identification technologies produced the clearest time gains: MALDI-TOF reduced species-identification time from 84.0 to 55.9 hours, and PNA-FISH from 4 days to 0.2 days; PNA-FISH also reduced targeted-therapy time from 2.3 to 0.6 days. T2Candida shortened mean time to appropriate therapy from 34 to 6 hours in one study.

Across the review, antifungal consumption reductions ranged from 11.6% to 59.0% in seven studies reporting a quantifiable reduction, and five studies reported percentage cost savings of 13.5%–50.6%. Multiple studies showed shorter empiric therapy or enabled discontinuation after negative diagnostics. Clinical endpoints were less persuasive: the paper describes mortality and LOS as generally improving, but most individual comparisons were not statistically significant, study designs were heterogeneous and usually observational, and the review did not perform meta-analysis, formal risk-of-bias assessment, publication-bias analysis, or certainty grading. The practical message is therefore operational rather than causal: diagnostics are most valuable when turnaround is fast, results reach a stewardship actor promptly, and the program measures de-escalation, duration, exposure, diagnostic TAT, and safety outcomes.

Validity frame. The review's question is clinically coherent, but the included interventions bundle diagnostics with stewardship personnel, education, electronic alerts, audit/feedback, and treatment protocols. This makes it difficult to isolate the effect of the diagnostic itself. The authors explicitly acknowledge this limitation.

Section condensation

Introduction / rationale

The article frames IFI as high-morbidity, high-cost disease in which early appropriate antifungal therapy matters, while antifungal use is constrained by toxicity, cost, limited drug classes, and resistance concerns. AFS is presented as a specialized stewardship domain with diagnostic uncertainty, difficult de-escalation, and limited resistance reporting as distinguishing challenges.

The rationale for diagnostic-driven AFS is that conventional microscopy/histopathology/culture may be slow or insensitive, prolonging empiric therapy. Biomarkers and rapid/non-culture diagnostics could reduce uncertainty and enable earlier targeted therapy, but the value depends on timely reporting and incorporation into an active stewardship process.

Methods

PubMed and EMBASE were searched for January 2010–January 2021, with manual reference-list searching. Eligible studies had to involve an AFS program (independent or within AMS), patients with an IFI indication, a diagnostic approach, and at least two prespecified outcomes. Reviews, conference abstracts, and non-English articles were excluded. Two researchers independently screened titles/abstracts, full texts were reviewed, and disagreements were resolved by a third researcher.

Data were extracted into Excel for study characteristics, diagnostic approaches, performance outcomes, clinical outcomes, and selected other outcomes. The authors state that PRISMA methodology was followed. Synthesis was qualitative and narrative; no statistical methods were used. The paper does not report a formal risk-of-bias instrument, certainty-of-evidence framework, publication-bias assessment, or meta-analysis.

Results — study set and diagnostics

Of 422 citations, 63 underwent full-text assessment and 17 were included. Eight studies were from Europe, four from the United States, four from Japan, and one from Thailand; most were single-center. Eleven studies were Candida-only. Eleven used pre/post intervention designs. Routine culture/identification methods were common, while BDG was the most frequently used biomarker; other approaches included GM, CT, T2Candida, MALDI-TOF, PCR, and PNA-FISH.

Rapid species-identification methods produced the clearest timing effects. MALDI-TOF reduced identification time from 84.0 to 55.9 hours (P<.001), and PNA-FISH from 4 days to 0.2 days (P<.001). PNA-FISH reduced targeted-therapy time from 2.3 to 0.6 days (P=.0016). T2Candida reduced time to appropriate therapy from 34 to 6 hours (P=.00147). By contrast, once-weekly or outsourced BDG/GM workflows produced multi-day turnaround, attenuating potential stewardship value.

Results — antifungal use, cost, duration, de-escalation

Ten studies reported reduced antifungal consumption, with seven quantifying reductions of 11.6%–59.0%. Ten studies reported decreased antifungal cost, with five percentage savings of 13.5%–50.6%; individual reports included €2 million over seven years, US$31,615 over 18 months, US$48,400 over eight months, US$138,991 annually, and US$415 per patient. Several studies shifted therapy from echinocandins toward fluconazole or reduced IV DOT.

Three studies reported shorter empiric treatment after diagnostic-supported intervention, and seven demonstrated de-escalation/discontinuation. Examples include discontinuation in 46.7% of patients without IFI after BDG-supported assessment, avoidance/discontinuation in roughly 58%–60% of T2Candida-negative patients, and higher de-escalation in Whitney et al. (87% vs 50%, P=.004). These are the most actionable and internally consistent findings of the review.

Results — clinical outcomes

Most mortality comparisons moved in a favorable direction but were not statistically significant. Huang et al. reported significant improvement in overall 30-day mortality for the combined bacteremia/candidemia cohort (20.3% to 12.7%, P=.021), whereas the yeast-only mortality difference (33.3% to 17.7%) was not significant. Six studies were described as showing shorter LOS, but most intervention-associated LOS comparisons were not statistically significant.

Selected additional outcomes were more convincing: Ito-Takeichi et al. reported 60-day clinical failure falling from 80.0% to 36.4% (P<.001) and adverse events from 51.4% to 13.6% (P=.004); Martín-Gutiérrez et al. reported a significant fall in hospital-acquired candidemia incidence (P=.009). These remain study-specific observational signals rather than pooled effects.

Discussion

The authors interpret the review as evidence that diagnostics-driven AFS can reduce unnecessary antifungal use and cost without detectable harm, and that rapid diagnostics are most useful when integrated with real-time stewardship. They emphasize that delayed batch testing and outsourcing can erase the theoretical speed advantage of biomarkers, making laboratory infrastructure and result communication part of the intervention.

The discussion also highlights geographic inequity in AFS and fungal-diagnostic capacity, especially outside resource-rich regions, and argues for standardized metrics. Importantly, the authors acknowledge that small samples, heterogeneous study designs, crude mortality measures, absence of controls, Candida dominance, and inability to separate the diagnostic effect from the AFS bundle limit definitive conclusions.

Conclusions

The authors recommend fostering diagnostic tests that can produce timely results continuously and pairing them with AFS programs that track clinical indicators in addition to cost savings. They also identify diagnostic-capacity gaps in developing countries and conclude that AFS initiatives should be encouraged because the reviewed studies did not show a negative impact on patient outcomes.

Appraisal boundary: the conclusion is directionally reasonable for stewardship implementation, but the review does not provide strong causal evidence that diagnostic-driven AFS reduces mortality or LOS. Its strongest support is for prescribing-process, utilization, and economic outcomes.

Source integrity and internal consistency

Counts are not fully internally consistent. The abstract reports galactomannan use in 4/17 studies, while the Results section states 3/17. The abstract/Discussion describe mortality improvement in 13 studies (abstract: 13/16), whereas the Mortality subsection states that 12 studies reported improvement, then separately discusses Huang et al.
Whitney et al. TAT labels conflict. The narrative Results report median TAT of 13 days for BDG and 12 days for GM, while Table 2 lists GM as 13 days and BDG as 12 days. This artifact preserves the discrepancy rather than choosing one value.

These inconsistencies do not overturn the review's broad process findings, but they reduce confidence in precise review-level counts and argue against using headline numerators without checking the underlying row-level data.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for antifungal-stewardship program design; do not use alone for practice-effect claims.
Best use: framing a diagnostics-driven stewardship program, selecting metrics, and understanding how turnaround/communication determine diagnostic utility.
Do not use for: proving mortality benefit, choosing a single preferred diagnostic platform, or assuming results generalize to mold-heavy/resource-limited settings.

Main strengths

  • Focused question at the intersection of diagnostics and antifungal stewardship.
  • Transparent eligibility criteria and PRISMA-style study flow.
  • Preserves operational outcomes often omitted from clinical reviews: TAT, de-escalation, DOT/DDD, cost, and stewardship response.
  • Includes diverse diagnostic modalities and concrete program workflows.
  • Explicitly recognizes diagnostic infrastructure and turnaround as implementation variables.

Main limitations

  • Primary evidence is mostly observational, single-center, pre/post, and heterogeneous.
  • No formal review-level risk-of-bias assessment, GRADE, publication-bias assessment, or meta-analysis.
  • English-only inclusion and only two bibliographic databases may reduce completeness.
  • Candida dominates the evidence base; mold stewardship is underrepresented.
  • Diagnostic effects are entangled with active stewardship interventions, education, alerts, and care bundles.
  • Clinical outcome improvements are largely non-significant and underpowered.
  • Internal count/TAT inconsistencies reduce confidence in some headline summaries.
  • Industry sponsorship and author financial relationships warrant attention when interpreting optimistic framing, although they do not by themselves invalidate the findings.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the article's internal methods/results; moderate for practice translation. The full 15-page source, main tables, PRISMA figure, graphical abstract, disclosures, and limitations were reviewed. Practice translation remains limited by the age and design of the underlying evidence and was not externally updated here.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build around a response pathwayA test result should trigger a defined stewardship action: review, escalation, narrowing, discontinuation, or documented rationale for continuation.
TAT is a clinical variableMeasure order-to-result and result-to-action separately. A biomarker batched weekly or sent out for ~2 weeks may not function as a rapid stewardship tool.
Use negative-result stewardship deliberatelySeveral studies derive value from stopping or avoiding empiric antifungals after negative T2Candida/BDG-supported evaluation; this requires validated local interpretation and clinical context.
Do not optimize spend aloneTrack exposure, prescribing quality, diagnostic process, microbiology, clinical outcomes and cost together, using Table 3 as a starter metric set.
Separate process success from patient benefitShorter therapy and lower cost are better supported here than mortality reduction. Use balancing measures for missed/delayed IFI, recurrent infection and clinical failure.
Match the assay to infrastructureLocal laboratory capacity, batching frequency, weekend coverage, transport, result routing and pharmacist/ID availability are part of the intervention.

What could change a local AFS workflow from this paper

  • Create a fungal-diagnostic stewardship queue with accountable review of positive and selected negative tests.
  • Define test-specific action windows and escalation criteria rather than simply making assays available.
  • Measure empiric antifungal days avoided, time to targeted therapy, de-escalation rate, TAT, guideline adherence, clinical failure, and IFI-related mortality.
  • Audit whether send-out/batched assays actually return soon enough to influence therapy.
  • Use Candida-focused findings cautiously when designing pathways for invasive mold disease.
What should not change on this evidence alone. The review does not justify a universal rule to stop antifungals based on a single negative biomarker, does not establish superiority of one assay across populations, and does not prove that diagnostic-driven AFS independently reduces mortality.
