2. Opioid withdrawal: why the patient looks “sympathetic”

Chronic μ-opioid receptor stimulation suppresses neuronal activity in circuits including the locus coeruleus. With repeated exposure, compensatory signaling develops. When agonist effect falls abruptly, that compensation is unmasked: norepinephrine output rises and the patient can develop tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety/restlessness, sweating, piloerection, mydriasis, GI hypermotility, nausea/vomiting, abdominal cramping, aches and insomnia.

Clinical cognition: opioid withdrawal is usually profoundly uncomfortable but is not usually the direct lethal syndrome that alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal can be. The danger in this case comes from what withdrawal provokes or obscures: dehydration, missed insulin, electrolyte shifts, severe hypertension, aspiration risk, premature discharge, and complicated medication transitions.

Why fentanyl changes buprenorphine initiation

Buprenorphine has very high μ-receptor affinity but only partial agonist activity. If it displaces a full agonist while substantial full-agonist effect remains, the net receptor signal can fall abruptly and cause precipitated opioid withdrawal (POW). ASAM’s high-potency synthetic opioid guidance therefore emphasizes individualized initiation based on clinical withdrawal state and setting rather than a rigid clock.

Case-specific transition issue: the MAR/note indicates methadone 15 mg around 20:34, with buprenorphine/naloxone 8/2 mg q8h planned to begin the next morning. That is only about 9.5 hours after a long-acting full agonist dose, on top of same-day fentanyl exposure. The important teaching point is not that a specific number of hours is “right” or “wrong”; it is that the patient should be treated as being in an induction state, not a simple “resume home dose” state. Objective withdrawal immediately before the first buprenorphine dose matters greatly. The current SUBOXONE label specifically notes greater susceptibility to precipitated/prolonged withdrawal in patients dependent on methadone or other long-acting opioids.

If withdrawal worsens after buprenorphine

Distinguish four possibilities: undertreated ordinary withdrawal, precipitated withdrawal, nausea/adverse effect from buprenorphine, or withdrawal/intoxication from another substance. ASAM notes that persistent opioid withdrawal after buprenorphine often responds to additional buprenorphine; severe cases require monitored escalation and supportive therapy. This is one reason documenting a pre-dose and post-dose COWS plus objective signs is more useful than relying on symptom adjectives alone.

The 2024 international consensus defines DKA using three components: diabetes/hyperglycemia, significant ketonemia (β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/L or equivalent urine ketones), and metabolic acidosis (pH <7.3 and/or bicarbonate <18 mmol/L). Importantly, a known diabetes diagnosis can satisfy the “D” component even if glucose is not very high; therefore, near-normal glucose does not by itself exclude DKA.

At the 21:49 chemistry snapshot, this patient does not meet the biochemical criteria for DKA from the supplied data. β-hydroxybutyrate is below 3.0 mmol/L and serum CO₂ is 22 mmol/L. The mildly elevated anion gap and 2+ urine ketones establish ketosis, not ketoacidosis. A VBG/ABG would be the cleaner acid-base confirmation if clinical concern persisted.

So what is the ketosis?

The most plausible model is a continuum rather than a binary label:

  1. Missed basal insulin reduces tonic suppression of lipolysis and ketogenesis.
  2. Vomiting and poor intake lower carbohydrate availability and promote starvation physiology.
  3. Stress hormones from withdrawal/illness increase glucagon, catecholamines and cortisol, pushing toward ketogenesis.
  4. Fluids improve renal perfusion and reduce counterregulatory stress, which can improve the chemistry before insulin has fully corrected the underlying tendency.
High-yield T1DM rule: basal insulin is physiologic replacement, not merely “glucose coverage.” ADA 2026 hospital standards explicitly state that people with type 1 diabetes should continue to receive basal insulin even when feedings are discontinued. In this patient, missing the prior night’s glargine is therefore a mechanistically important event even though the glucose is only in the 100s.

Potassium is part of the DKA/insulin story even when DKA is absent

K fell from 4.3 to 3.7 mmol/L while the patient was receiving fluid and before/around renewed insulin therapy. Insulin drives potassium intracellularly, vomiting can reduce total-body potassium, and catecholamine shifts can make serum measurements dynamic. A “normal” serum K does not guarantee normal total-body stores.

4. Severe hypertension: number versus organ injury

Acute opioid withdrawal can produce a marked catecholaminergic state, and pain, vomiting, agitation, nicotine, medication nonadherence and possible stimulant exposure can all amplify blood pressure. The AHA’s acute-care statement distinguishes markedly elevated BP from hypertensive emergency, which requires new or worsening target-organ damage.

Why nicardipine can be rational yet still require diagnostic discipline: IV antihypertensives allow titratable control when clinicians believe rapid, controlled reduction is needed. But if severe BP is primarily reactive to withdrawal, pain or agitation and no acute organ injury exists, the BP can fall substantially once the driver resolves. That makes simultaneous treatment of the underlying withdrawal and frequent reassessment important.

Clonidine sits at the intersection of two problems

Clonidine is an α2-adrenergic agonist that decreases central sympathetic outflow. ASAM recognizes α2 agonists as effective adjuncts for opioid withdrawal symptoms. In this case it can theoretically improve both autonomic withdrawal symptoms and BP, but its hemodynamic effect can become additive with nicardipine, labetalol and resumed home antihypertensives as the withdrawal surge resolves.

5. Volume depletion / transient creatinine rise

Vomiting, limited intake and an adrenergic state can reduce effective circulating volume. The improvement in BUN/SCr from 22/1.3 to 13/1.1 after IV fluids is compatible with a prerenal component. The initially high-normal Hgb/Hct (16/49.8) is also compatible with hemoconcentration, though neither finding is specific.

The teaching tension is that this patient also has HFimpEF. “Heart failure history” does not mean fluids are forbidden; it means fluid therapy should be tied to perfusion, congestion and response rather than given reflexively.

6. HFimpEF: improved EF is remission, not erasure

The supplied history says EF improved from reduced EF to about 55%. The AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline defines HFimpEF as previous LVEF ≤40% with follow-up LVEF >40% and recommends continuing HFrEF guideline-directed medical therapy to prevent relapse, even if the patient becomes asymptomatic.

For this admission, losartan, metoprolol and spironolactone matter in two ways: they are chronic HF/BP therapies, but temporary holding can be reasonable when oral tolerance, hemodynamics, renal function or potassium are unstable. The educational point is to reassess and reintroduce chronic disease-modifying therapy once the acute physiology permits rather than treating the improved EF as a cure.

7. Fever and the danger of attribution

Withdrawal can produce chills, diaphoresis and temperature dysregulation, but a documented fever should not automatically be labeled “withdrawal.” This patient received empiric cefepime/vancomycin while infection was assessed. The supplied data are reassuring in several respects—WBC 8.1, no obvious source, negative chest imaging, nonperitonitic abdominal exam—but none of those individually excludes infection.

Anti-anchoring rule: “The symptoms fit withdrawal” is not the same statement as “withdrawal explains every abnormality.” Revisit fever, altered mentation, persistent hypotension/hypertension, rising lactate, focal pain, bacteremia risk and new organ dysfunction on their own merits.

8. QTc, methadone, and the tachycardia trap

The second ECG shows HR 107 bpm, QT 364 ms and machine QTc 485 ms. That number is almost exactly what the Bazett formula produces. Bazett correction tends to overestimate QTc in sinus tachycardia, so recalculating with Fridericia is useful:

That substantially changes the impression from “near-500 ms QTc” to “borderline/mildly prolonged depending on method and manual measurement.” It does not make QT risk irrelevant. Methadone has a labeled risk of QT prolongation/torsades, and the label highlights hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, cardiac hypertrophy and concomitant QT-active drugs as modifiable contributors. Ondansetron also has dose-dependent QT effects.

Pharmacist lens: after a tachycardic automated QTc result, verify the correction method and—when the decision matters—manually review the QT interval. In this case, K was 3.7 and Mg was not supplied, while methadone and antiemetics were being used. That is a better reason for deliberate electrolyte/ECG review than the machine’s 485-ms value alone.

9. A negative UDS does not overrule the history

The note reports a negative UDS despite same-day fentanyl use. This can be entirely compatible with true fentanyl exposure if the hospital’s “opiate” immunoassay does not include a fentanyl-specific assay. Many routine opiate screens primarily detect morphine-like compounds and require a separate fentanyl test. Always interpret a UDS by its actual analyte list and assay characteristics.

13. Mini-quiz

1) Glucose is only 167 mg/dL. Can this patient still have DKA?

Yes in principle. Current consensus allows known diabetes to satisfy the diabetes/hyperglycemia component even without marked hyperglycemia. But this patient’s supplied β-hydroxybutyrate (2.18) and CO₂ (22) do not meet the ketonemia/acidosis thresholds for DKA at that snapshot.

2) Why is restarting “home Suboxone 8 mg TID” not the same as resuming losartan?

Because the patient is no longer simply in a stable maintenance pharmacologic state. Heavy fentanyl use has re-established full-agonist receptor occupancy/dependence. Buprenorphine now functions as an induction, where timing and withdrawal state determine whether it relieves or precipitates withdrawal.

3) Does SBP >200 automatically mean hypertensive emergency?

No. Hypertensive emergency is severe BP elevation with acute target-organ damage. The number determines urgency of assessment; organ injury determines the syndrome.

4) Is a machine QTc of 485 ms at HR 107 automatically the value you should use?

No. The displayed QT/HR reproduce a Bazett QTc near 485 ms, and Bazett commonly overcorrects during tachycardia. Fridericia from the same values is about 441 ms. Manual QT verification and clinical context are appropriate when the result drives a medication decision.

5) Why is glargine important even if the patient is not eating?

In type 1 diabetes, basal insulin suppresses hepatic glucose production, lipolysis and ketogenesis continuously. It is physiologic replacement, so complete omission can permit ketosis/DKA despite limited caloric intake.

14. Bottom line

The unifying diagnosis is fentanyl-associated opioid withdrawal with a strong autonomic component, but the educational value is in the overlaps.

  • The patient has ketosis without demonstrated DKA at the sampled labs, yet T1DM + missed basal insulin makes progression a real concern.
  • The BP is severely elevated, but the supplied data do not establish hypertensive emergency because acute target-organ injury is not demonstrated.
  • The buprenorphine plan should be thought of as re-induction after fentanyl/full-agonist exposure, not automatic resumption of an old maintenance dose.
  • Methadone, antiemetics, tachycardia and potassium/magnesium status create a useful QT interpretation problem; the machine Bazett QTc likely overstates the corrected interval here.
  • HFimpEF changes how you monitor fluids and chronic medications, but improved EF represents remission with persistent relapse risk, not disappearance of HF biology.
