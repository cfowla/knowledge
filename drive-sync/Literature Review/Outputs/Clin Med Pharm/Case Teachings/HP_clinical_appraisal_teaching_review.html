Clinical Appraisal & Teaching Review

42-year-old man with rapidly progressive penile/scrotal swelling, fever, severe inflammatory response, and untreated recently diagnosed diabetes

Bottom line

The leading diagnosis is severe penile/scrotal cellulitis arising from a skin lesion at the penile base in the setting of substantially uncontrolled diabetes. The degree of systemic inflammation appropriately makes Fournier gangrene the “must-not-miss” alternative, but the current bedside and imaging findings make established necrotizing infection less likely. Importantly, neither a reassuring CT nor minimal pain can independently exclude early Fournier gangrene; serial examinations and surgical/urologic involvement are the correct safety strategy.

The metabolic abnormalities are best described as stress/infection-associated hyperglycemia with mild ketosis and transient lactic/anion-gap acidosis—not diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). The measured sodium of 130 mmol/L is largely explained by hyperglycemia; corrected sodium is approximately 134–136 mmol/L.

The H&P’s label of “sepsis with sepsis-induced organ dysfunction” is the most debatable part of the assessment. The patient clearly has infection plus a systemic inflammatory response and an initially elevated lactate, but the documented variables do not demonstrate an obvious ≥2-point SOFA increase. An INR of 1.5 and lactate of 3.3 mmol/L may be clinically important, but they are not themselves equivalent to Sepsis-3 organ dysfunction.

2. Appraisal of the H&P

What was done well

  • Recognized Fournier gangrene early and obtained urology plus general-surgery involvement rather than relying on antibiotics alone.
  • Appropriate broad empiric therapy while necrotizing infection remained in the differential: linezolid + piperacillin/tazobactam is explicitly listed by IDSA as an acceptable broad empiric regimen for necrotizing fasciitis/Fournier gangrene.
  • Reasonable immediate physiologic support: crystalloid, lactate reassessment, glucose treatment, cultures, and close inpatient monitoring.
  • Appropriate targeted diagnostic workup: CT, planned scrotal ultrasound, blood cultures, GC/CT NAAT, HIV/syphilis/HCV screening.
  • Scrotal elevation is a useful supportive measure for inflammatory edema and epididymal/scrotal discomfort.

What needs refinement

  • The note appears to overstate “sepsis-induced organ dysfunction.” The data show systemic infection and hyperlactatemia, but no clear ≥2-point SOFA rise is documented.
  • LRINEC = 8 should not be treated as diagnostic. It raises vigilance but cannot establish Fournier gangrene and should not override bedside surgical assessment.
  • “Acidosis” is too nonspecific. The pattern is better characterized as a mild anion-gap metabolic process with concurrent respiratory alkalosis, not DKA.
  • “Hyponatremia” should be contextualized as hyperglycemia-associated translocational hyponatremia; corrected sodium is near normal.
  • If epididymo-orchitis from GC/CT is truly suspected, doxycycline alone is not the CDC gonorrhea-containing regimen. The diagnosis and intended STI coverage should be explicit.

Documentation quality issues

  • “EKG not performed” conflicts with the later documented ECG at 00:25 (rate 93, QTc 457 ms).
  • Past medical history says “none,” despite the HPI and assessment documenting recently diagnosed diabetes.
  • Social history lists alcohol use as “Yes,” while the HPI states he stopped approximately one month earlier.
  • “LINREC” appears to be a typographical reference to LRINEC.
  • The assessment initially says repeat lactate pending, while the data later show normalization to 1.4 mmol/L; the final synthesis should incorporate the response.

3. The Core Diagnostic Problem: Cellulitis vs Fournier Gangrene

Clinical frame: “Genital cellulitis” and “early Fournier gangrene” can look similar initially. The decisive question is not whether the infection is severe—it clearly is—but whether infection has entered and is propagating through fascial planes such that urgent operative source control is required.

Why severe cellulitis is currently more likely

  • The swelling began at a visible skin lesion, a plausible portal for bacterial cellulitis.
  • No severe pain out of proportion, crepitus, bullae, necrosis, skin anesthesia, hemodynamic instability, or altered mental status is documented.
  • CT shows diffuse edema but no drainable collection or reported gas.
  • Urology judged the examination to be low concern for Fournier gangrene.
  • Lactate rapidly normalized from 3.3 to 1.4 mmol/L after initial resuscitation.

Why Fournier gangrene still cannot simply be “ruled out”

  • Diabetes is a major predisposing condition for Fournier gangrene.
  • The involved anatomy—penis, scrotum, inguinal extension—is exactly the relevant fascial territory.
  • Early necrotizing infection can precede crepitus, necrosis, or obvious gas on imaging.
  • IDSA emphasizes that clinical judgment and operative findings are more important than imaging when suspicion remains high.

Red flags that should immediately increase concern

New or escalating pain out of proportion; rapidly advancing erythema/edema; woody induration; skin anesthesia; dusky discoloration, ecchymosis, bullae, or necrosis; crepitus; new hypotension or encephalopathy; rising lactate; failure to improve on appropriate antibiotics; or unexpected worsening despite an initially reassuring scan.

LRINEC = 8: useful signal, poor arbiter

The reported score is internally plausible:

CRP 31.61 mg/dL ≈ 316 mg/L → 4 points WBC 21.9 ×10⁹/L → 1 point Hemoglobin 14.1 g/dL → 0 points Sodium 130 mmol/L → 2 points Creatinine 0.6 mg/dL → 0 points Glucose 347 mg/dL → 1 point TOTAL = 8 points

That looks alarming, but note what is driving the score: CRP + hyperglycemia + glucose-related sodium lowering. These abnormalities can occur in severe non-necrotizing cellulitis. Modern validation studies show inadequate sensitivity for LRINEC to rule out disease and imperfect specificity to rule it in. Treat it as a prompt for vigilance—not as a diagnosis.

4. Sepsis: What the Patient Has vs What the Label Implies

What is clearly present

  • Suspected/likely bacterial infection.
  • Fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis/neutrophilia, and markedly elevated CRP.
  • Lactate 3.3 mmol/L with subsequent clearance.

What Sepsis-3 requires

Sepsis-3 defines sepsis as infection causing life-threatening organ dysfunction, operationalized as an acute rise in SOFA score of at least 2 points.

Appraisal: On the documented data, a Sepsis-3 SOFA increase ≥2 is not demonstrated. The patient may still satisfy an institution-specific sepsis/CDI definition, but the H&P should distinguish such a definition from Sepsis-3 physiology.

Lactate ≠ shock

Lactate can rise from impaired perfusion, adrenergic stress, fever, increased glycolysis, reduced clearance, seizures, medications, and other mechanisms. Septic shock requires sepsis plus vasopressor-dependent hypotension to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg and lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate resuscitation. This patient did not meet that construct.

Was 30 mL/kg crystalloid reasonable?

Yes as an ED resuscitation strategy given fever, marked tachycardia, elevated lactate, vomiting, and possible relative volume depletion. The teaching nuance is that the Surviving Sepsis Campaign specifically recommends at least 30 mL/kg for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock, followed by reassessment; it is not a mandate for unlimited fluid in every infected patient.

5. Diabetes, Hyperglycemia, Ketosis, and Acid–Base Interpretation

Why diabetes matters to this infection

Marked hyperglycemia impairs host defense, including neutrophil function, and diabetes is strongly associated with more severe skin/soft-tissue infections and Fournier gangrene. Infection then increases counter-regulatory hormones, worsening glucose—a bidirectional cycle.

This is not DKA

Current ADA criteria require diabetes/hyperglycemia, significant ketosis, and metabolic acidosis. The patient has hyperglycemia, but:

  • β-hydroxybutyrate = 1.4 mmol/L, below the ≥3.0 mmol/L DKA threshold.
  • Venous pH = 7.408, not <7.3.
  • VBG bicarbonate = 20.3 mmol/L, not <18 mmol/L.

The urine ketones (2+) and β-hydroxybutyrate elevation indicate ketosis, which can occur from reduced oral intake, acute illness, relative insulin deficiency, or early metabolic decompensation without full DKA.

The acid–base pattern is mixed

VBG HCO3 ≈ 20.3 mmol/L Winter expected PCO2 ≈ (1.5 × 20.3) + 8 ≈ 38.5 ± 2 mmHg Observed venous PCO2 = 32.3 mmHg

The lower-than-expected PCO2 suggests a concurrent respiratory alkalosis, common with fever/systemic inflammation. Earlier lactate and ketosis can account for part of the anion-gap metabolic component. The near-normal pH therefore does not mean “no acid–base disturbance”; it reflects competing processes.

Correct the sodium before calling this true hyponatremia

Measured Na = 130 mmol/L Glucose = 347 mg/dL Using +1.6 mmol/L Na per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100: Corrected Na ≈ 134.0 mmol/L Using +2.4 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL: Corrected Na ≈ 135.9 mmol/L

This is best called hyperglycemia-associated translocational hyponatremia, not pseudohyponatremia. The distinction matters because the immediate treatment is correction of the hyperglycemia and volume status—not direct treatment of a true hypotonic sodium disorder unless a separate process is demonstrated.

Inpatient glucose strategy

The plan to check A1C is essential. A short period of correction insulin while trends are being established is understandable, but the 2026 ADA Standards strongly discourage prolonged correction-only insulin in most hospitalized patients. If glucose remains persistently elevated, a scheduled basal-containing regimen (with prandial insulin when eating, or basal + correction when not eating) is generally preferred.

Diabetes phenotype pearl

“Type 2 diabetes” is plausible, but a 42-year-old with BMI ~24 kg/m², no therapy after a recent diagnosis, substantial hyperglycemia, and measurable ketosis deserves attention to phenotype if the subsequent course is atypical. If he rapidly becomes insulin-dependent, has recurrent ketosis, or the A1C/history do not fit ordinary type 2 diabetes, outpatient reassessment for autoimmune or ketosis-prone diabetes may be reasonable.

6. STI / Epididymo-Orchitis Differential

Why it remains on the list

  • Sexual exposure approximately two weeks earlier.
  • Condoms reduce but do not eliminate all STI transmission.
  • Scrotal inflammation can accompany epididymo-orchitis.

Why it is not the most classic presentation

  • CDC describes acute epididymitis as typically unilateral testicular/epididymal pain and tenderness, often with hydrocele and palpable epididymal swelling.
  • This patient’s dominant finding is diffuse penile + scrotal wall edema beginning from a penile-base skin lesion.
  • No urethral discharge, pyuria, leukocyte esterase, or nitrite is present.

How to interpret doxycycline here

Doxycycline is appropriate therapy for C. trachomatis, but if the working diagnosis is sexually transmitted epididymitis, CDC recommends ceftriaxone plus doxycycline. Piperacillin/tazobactam should not be casually treated as a guideline-equivalent substitute for dedicated gonococcal therapy. Practically, the pending ultrasound and GC/CT NAAT should determine whether STI-directed therapy remains necessary.

Scrotal ultrasound

Ultrasound is most valuable for excluding torsion or other structural pathology when the diagnosis is uncertain. A negative ultrasound does not itself rule out epididymitis. Conversely, “pain improves with elevation” (historically called Prehn’s sign) is not sufficiently reliable to distinguish epididymitis from torsion.

7. The Penile-Base Lesion and Syphilis Question

The team appropriately asked whether a negative syphilis antibody excludes a chancre. It does not absolutely exclude very early primary syphilis. CDC notes that using only one class of serologic test can produce false-negative results in primary disease, and direct detection from a suspicious lesion (darkfield examination or validated PCR where available) is the most direct approach to early infection.

For this case, however, the lesion was described as a “pimple” that preceded a rapidly inflammatory cellulitic syndrome. That history is more naturally compatible with a follicular/skin portal of entry than with a classic painless indurated chancre—although syphilis lesions can be atypical. If morphology or exposure history keeps syphilis suspicion meaningful despite the initial negative screen, repeat serology after an interval and/or lesion-directed testing is reasonable.

Hepatitis B testing nuance

A negative hepatitis B surface antibody means protective surface antibody was not detected. It does not by itself establish whether the patient is merely nonimmune, previously infected, or currently infected. Full status requires the appropriate combination of HBsAg, total anti-HBc, and anti-HBs, interpreted with vaccination history.

Stewardship trajectory

  1. First hours: breadth is justified because missing Fournier gangrene is far more dangerous than brief overtreatment.
  2. After serial exams + imaging + cultures/NAAT: determine whether the syndrome is superficial cellulitis, epididymo-orchitis, abscess, or necrotizing infection.
  3. If Fournier is excluded and patient improves: narrow therapy to the most likely pathogen set rather than continuing triple therapy by inertia.
  4. If progression occurs: antibiotics are adjunctive; urgent operative exploration/source control becomes the priority.

9. Mild INR Elevation / “Coagulopathy”

INR 1.5 is abnormal and worth following, but the surrounding data are reassuring: platelets are normal, aPTT is normal, bilirubin is normal, and there is no documented bleeding. This could reflect acute illness, nutritional/vitamin K factors, hepatic synthetic dysfunction, or other causes. It should not automatically be treated as proof of sepsis-mediated organ failure.

If the INR remains elevated, the useful question is “why?” rather than merely relabeling it: repeat PT/INR, review medications/diet, reassess liver tests and clinical bleeding, and obtain a broader coagulation evaluation if DIC or hepatic dysfunction becomes clinically plausible.

10. Incidental Hepatomegaly and Hepatic Steatosis

CT also showed hepatomegaly and fatty liver. In a patient with diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is an important possibility, but the note does not document enough about alcohol quantity, prior liver disease, triglycerides, or fibrosis risk to assign a cause. Normal bilirubin and near-normal transaminases do not exclude steatotic liver disease.

This is not the acute problem, but it should not disappear at discharge: establish alcohol history, metabolic risk factors, liver chemistries over time, hepatitis status, and outpatient fibrosis-risk assessment after the acute illness.

11. What I Would Want Answered on the Next Round

  1. Trajectory of the genital exam: Is swelling/erythema spreading? Any new severe pain, anesthesia, bullae, duskiness, necrosis, induration, or crepitus?
  2. Ultrasound result: Epididymal/testicular hyperemia? Hydrocele? Torsion excluded? Any focal collection?
  3. Microbiology: Blood cultures, GC/CT NAAT, and whether the penile-base lesion is drainable/culturable.
  4. Metabolic trend: A1C, serial glucose, bicarbonate/anion gap, potassium, and whether ketosis resolves with hydration/insulin.
  5. INR trend: Does it normalize after resuscitation/nutrition, or persist?
  6. Antibiotic off-ramp: What objective finding will allow linezolid, piperacillin/tazobactam, and/or doxycycline to be narrowed?

13. Overall Assessment of the Admission Plan

Appropriate / defensible

  • Inpatient admission and close observation.
  • Early urology and surgery involvement.
  • Linezolid + piperacillin/tazobactam while Fournier remains a concern.
  • Blood cultures and serial lactate.
  • Scrotal ultrasound and GC/CT testing.
  • Insulin treatment and A1C assessment.
  • Scrotal elevation and symptom control.

Needs clarification or revision

  • Do not let LRINEC = 8 overrule serial bedside assessment.
  • Reframe “sepsis-induced organ dysfunction” unless an actual SOFA rise or other defensible organ dysfunction is demonstrated.
  • Do not call the sodium 130 true hypotonic hyponatremia without correcting for glucose.
  • Do not call this DKA; continue monitoring because infection can drive progression.
  • Define the purpose of doxycycline and ensure guideline-concordant gonorrhea therapy if epididymitis is genuinely suspected.
  • Build an explicit antibiotic de-escalation plan once Fournier/abscess/STI data return.
