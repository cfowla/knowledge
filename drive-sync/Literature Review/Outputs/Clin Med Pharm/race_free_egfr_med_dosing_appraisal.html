One-page synthesis

Bottom line: For routine adult medication-related decisions in patients with stable kidney function, the source makes a credible case to move away from Cockcroft-Gault estimated creatinine clearance and toward race-free CKD-EPI eGFR adjusted to the patient’s body surface area. The recommendation is strongest as a health-system implementation standard and weakest as evidence for drug-specific outcome benefit.

The paper argues that Cockcroft-Gault estimated creatinine clearance has become a poor default for contemporary medication dosing because it was derived in 249 white men using pre-standardization serum creatinine assays, was based on measured creatinine clearance rather than measured GFR, uses body weight inconsistently across calculators and practitioners, and is often further distorted by empiric serum creatinine “rounding.” The authors propose a unified approach: use race-free CKD-EPI eGFR equations for both medical and medication-related decisions, convert standardized eGFR from mL/min/1.73 m² to patient-specific mL/min when body size is meaningfully different from 1.73 m², and use cystatin C or measured clearance when creatinine is unreliable.

The practical value for an inpatient pharmacist is high. It gives language for policy change, EHR build, lab-vendor alignment, and exception handling. It also directly addresses a common pharmacy operational problem: C-G results vary substantially depending on selected weight, arbitrary serum creatinine floors, and local calculator conventions. However, the evidence base is not strong enough to support blind dose conversion for every medication. The clinical-use pathway should include risk stratification, drug-specific review, therapeutic drug monitoring where available, and measured GFR or measured creatinine clearance for high-stakes or physiologically unstable patients.

Clinical-action warning: Evidence is insufficient to use a single static eGFR value for acute kidney injury, rapidly changing renal function, aggressive diuresis with fluctuating volume status, critical illness with unstable filtration, kidney replacement therapy, ECMO, or narrow-therapeutic-index decisions without monitoring or measured clearance. For those settings, the source supports caution, not automatic conversion.

Recommendation strength and evidence quality

Local practice recommendation

Conditional / implementation-focused recommendation: Transition routine adult renal dosing decision support from C-G eCrCL to race-free CKD-EPI eGFRBSAadj for patients with stable kidney function, with an explicit high-risk exception pathway.

Operationally strong elements: stop non-evidence-based serum creatinine rounding; standardize one default estimator; display units clearly; use BSA-adjusted values for medication decisions; document which equation was used.

Evidence quality

Moderate that C-G eCrCL is outdated, variable, and less aligned with contemporary measured-GFR–validated equations.

Low to moderate for improved prediction of selected drug clearance using race-free CKD-EPI eGFRBSAadj, because supporting PK comparisons are mostly single-center and drug-specific.

Very low / insufficient for patient-centered outcomes, universal drug-label conversion, and use in unstable kidney function.

Current external context: FDA’s 2024 renal impairment pharmacokinetic guidance recommends eGFR over estimated creatinine clearance for renal-function assessment in PK studies and describes BSA conversion for dosing units. KDIGO 2024 CKD guidance supports validated eGFR equations using serum creatinine and/or cystatin C for medication dosing, with attention to BSA adjustment and non-GFR determinants. These sources support the direction of the paper but do not create drug-by-drug dosing rules.

Contraindications to simplistic use of eGFRBSAadj as a sole dosing determinant

  • Rapidly changing serum creatinine, acute kidney injury, renal recovery, or aggressive diuresis with unstable volume status.
  • Critical illness where creatinine, cystatin C, distribution volume, augmented renal clearance, or renal trajectory is changing.
  • Narrow-therapeutic-index drugs without TDM or clinical safety margin.
  • Markedly abnormal muscle mass, amputation, cachexia, severe frailty, bodybuilding/creatine supplementation, spinal cord injury, or severe obesity when the biomarker choice is uncertain.
  • Conditions that distort cystatin C, including high-dose steroids, uncontrolled thyroid disease, smoking, high adiposity, malignancy, coronary artery disease, and systemic inflammation.

Interactions and assay interferences relevant to kidney estimates

This source is not about drug-drug interactions in the usual pharmacokinetic sense. The relevant “interactions” are biomarker and assay interferences. Creatinine can be affected by muscle mass, diet, creatine supplements, tubular secretion inhibition, non-kidney elimination changes, and Jaffé assay interferences such as glucose, ketoacids, abnormal proteins/lipids, and some medications. Cystatin C can be affected by non-GFR factors listed above. These should change which estimator is selected, whether a cystatin C is ordered, and whether measured clearance or TDM is needed.

Renal and hepatic considerations

Renal: The paper’s central claim is about estimating renal filtration for medication decisions. The method should be used as an estimate of filtration, not as a direct measure of all renal drug elimination pathways such as secretion, reabsorption, renal metabolism, transporter effects, or dialytic clearance.

Hepatic: Advanced cirrhosis can reduce creatinine generation and make creatinine-based estimates misleading. The source flags cirrhosis/liver transplant among conditions where creatinine and cystatin C may both be biased, so measured GFR or measured creatinine clearance should be considered for critical treatment decisions.

Safety signals and foreseeable failure modes

Underdosing risk

Using standardized eGFR in mL/min/1.73 m² without BSA adjustment can underestimate absolute GFR in patients with BSA above 1.73 m². The practical risk is avoidable underdosing or premature discontinuation when drug labels or protocols use mL/min thresholds.

Overdosing risk

Using standardized eGFR without BSA adjustment can overestimate absolute GFR in patients with low BSA. The practical risk is excessive dosing in underweight, frail, or low-BSA patients.

Biomarker misclassification

Creatinine and cystatin C are both vulnerable to non-GFR determinants. Choosing an equation without considering the patient’s phenotype can create false precision.

Label discordance

Many labels and compendia still reference C-G eCrCL. A system-level transition can create dose discordance unless the EHR, policy, pharmacist documentation, and exception rules are aligned.

High-risk drug exposure

The drug-clearance comparisons are supportive for selected antimicrobials, but they do not prove improved toxicity, efficacy, mortality, length of stay, or resistance outcomes.

Operational drift

If eGFRBSAadj is introduced without decommissioning C-G calculators, serum creatinine rounding rules, and ambiguous renal dosing notes, the system may become less consistent rather than more consistent.

Section-by-section condensation

Purpose / summary

The source’s purpose is to support a unified approach to estimating kidney filtration in adults with stable kidney function by replacing C-G eCrCL with race-free eGFR for medication-related decisions. It also argues that eGFR should be adjusted for individual BSA when used for drug dosing, particularly when BSA differs from 1.73 m².

The authors identify four drivers: FDA 2024 renal impairment PK guidance favoring eGFR over estimated creatinine clearance, NKF/ASN endorsement of race-free CKD-EPI equations, widespread standardized creatinine assays, and greater cystatin C availability. Their conclusion is a health-system transition recommendation rather than a drug-by-drug efficacy claim.

Why move away from Cockcroft-Gault?

C-G was derived in 1976 using measured creatinine clearance, which overestimates measured GFR because creatinine is secreted by tubules. The equation was developed before serum creatinine standardization, from 249 white men, and the female adjustment was estimated rather than scientifically quantified.

Modern implementation adds variability: total, ideal, adjusted, lean, or dosing body weight may be selected inconsistently; some practitioners and EHR calculators round low serum creatinine to arbitrary values; and US body size has shifted substantially since the equation was developed. These factors make C-G a poor standardization tool across institutions and practitioners.

Recent developments

The paper aligns its recommendation with NKF/ASN race-free eGFR recommendations, KDIGO 2024 CKD practice points, and FDA 2024 renal impairment PK guidance. It emphasizes three race-free CKD-EPI equations: 2021 creatinine, 2012 cystatin C, and 2021 creatinine-cystatin C.

The source distinguishes standardized eGFR used for CKD staging from BSA-adjusted eGFR used for medication decisions. This distinction is essential because laboratory-reported eGFR is typically normalized to 1.73 m², while medication clearance and drug-dosing thresholds are usually expressed in mL/min.

Why eGFR and C-G vary from measured GFR

All estimating equations are population-derived and rely on endogenous biomarkers. Creatinine and cystatin C can change for reasons unrelated to kidney filtration, causing individual estimates to deviate from measured GFR. Accuracy is often discussed by P30, the percentage of estimates within ±30% of measured GFR.

The article highlights that C-G eCrCL using total body weight has worse bias and accuracy than contemporary eGFR equations in comparative datasets. It also emphasizes that non-GFR determinants should drive equation choice and the decision to use cystatin C, measured GFR, measured CrCL, or TDM.

BSA adjustment and terminology

The paper proposes “eGFR” for standardized laboratory results in mL/min/1.73 m² and “eGFRBSAadj” for eGFR adjusted to the individual’s BSA in mL/min. It argues that BSA adjustment should be standard for medication-related decisions because it better approximates measured GFR and medication clearance when body size differs from 1.73 m².

The Titan et al pooled dataset is used to show the direction and magnitude of error: standardized eGFR underestimated measured GFR by about 19.7 mL/min in the highest BSA group and overestimated it by about 9.9 mL/min in the lowest BSA group, while BSA-adjusted eGFR reduced those mean differences to about 2.8 and 2.7 mL/min, respectively.

Cystatin C use

Creatinine remains the practical first-line biomarker, but cystatin C is less influenced by muscle mass, meat consumption, physical activity, age, sex, and nutritional status. The combined creatinine-cystatin C equation generally has better accuracy and lower bias across broad cohorts and is suggested for class 2–3 obesity when available.

Cystatin C is not a universal fix. It can be affected by obesity/adiposity, thyroid disease, malignancy, smoking, high-dose steroids, coronary artery disease, and inflammation. If creatinine and cystatin C estimates differ by more than about 20%, the combined equation is usually more accurate unless a single biomarker has a clear isolated confounder.

Medication labels, compendia, and PK evidence

The workgroup consensus is that eGFRBSAadj can replace C-G eCrCL for medication-related decision-making in adults with stable kidney function, even when older labels still refer to C-G. The rationale is that many legacy labels used older creatinine assays and total-body-weight C-G methods that pharmacists do not consistently reproduce.

Four 2023 drug-clearance studies support the concept for vancomycin, aminoglycosides, meropenem, and cefepime. The signal is directionally favorable, especially for BSA-adjusted CKD-EPI equations, but the evidence is limited by small or single-center cohorts, predominantly white populations in several studies, selected exclusions, and surrogate PK endpoints.

Putting it together, research needs, and conclusion

The paper’s algorithm starts with stable adult GFR, screens for clinical factors affecting creatinine and cystatin C interpretation, then selects the most appropriate equation or measurement method. It explicitly recommends risk-benefit assessment for individual dosing regimens and use of TDM when available.

The research-needs section appropriately limits the recommendation by calling for more data in diverse populations, extremes of body size, frailty, muscle-wasting states, amputations, different kidney-disease etiologies, hospitalized and critically ill patients, and drug-specific dose adjustment validation. The conclusion is best interpreted as a call for structured transition, not a warrant for unmonitored dosing changes.

Best use: policy design, renal dosing governance, pharmacist education, EHR decision-support build, and exception handling. Do not use as stand-alone evidence for unmonitored drug-specific dose changes.

Main strengths

  • Directly addresses a real-world inconsistency in pharmacy practice: multiple C-G weights, arbitrary creatinine floors, and divergent EHR calculations.
  • Separates standardized eGFR for CKD staging from eGFRBSAadj for medication dosing.
  • Provides practical cystatin C guidance instead of treating creatinine as universally reliable.
  • Includes an implementation algorithm and acknowledges non-GFR determinants and high-risk exceptions.
  • Aligns with FDA, KDIGO, and NKF/ASN direction without overstating drug-specific outcome evidence.

Main limitations

  • Consensus/narrative source; it does not report a prospective patient-outcome implementation trial.
  • Drug-clearance evidence is limited to selected drugs and mainly surrogate PK outcomes.
  • Critically ill evidence excludes many of the patients who create the greatest renal dosing uncertainty.
  • Does not resolve how to update every legacy product label or compendium entry.
  • Requires EHR, lab, calculator, pharmacy education, and local governance changes to avoid mixed-method drift.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate-high for interpreting the paper’s practice implications and limitations. Confidence is lower for any drug-specific dose recommendation because the source is not a dosing compendium and does not validate every renal cutoff.

What should and should not change in practice

Should change now

  • Stop empiric rounding of low serum creatinine for renal dosing.
  • Standardize terminology: eGFR for mL/min/1.73 m²; eGFRBSAadj for mL/min.
  • Build or use a BSA-adjusted eGFR calculator for adult medication decisions.
  • Use race-free CKD-EPI equations as the default contemporary estimator in stable adults.
  • Develop cystatin C and measured-clearance exception pathways.
  • Educate staff that C-G and CKD-EPI values are not directly interchangeable without units, BSA status, and method context.

Should not change automatically

  • Do not mass-convert every renal dose category without drug-specific review.
  • Do not use eGFRBSAadj alone for unstable renal function or rapidly changing serum creatinine.
  • Do not discontinue TDM for vancomycin, aminoglycosides, lithium, immunosuppressants, or other measurable high-risk drugs.
  • Do not ignore label, payer, regulatory, or local policy constraints when documenting dose rationale.
  • Do not treat cystatin C as immune to confounding.
  • Do not apply this adult stable-GFR pathway to pediatrics, pregnancy, KRT, or ECMO without a separate policy.
Explicit insufficiency warning: This source is insufficient, by itself, to justify a bedside dose escalation or reduction when a patient is unstable, the drug has severe concentration-dependent toxicity, drug levels are discordant with estimated function, or the only support is a threshold crossing between C-G and eGFRBSAadj. In those situations, use drug levels, measured clearance, consultation, and patient-specific pharmacokinetics.
