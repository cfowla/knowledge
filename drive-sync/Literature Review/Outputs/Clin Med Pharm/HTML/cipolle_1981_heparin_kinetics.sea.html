One-page synthesis

Core thesis: marked interpatient variability in both pharmacodynamic sensitivity to heparin and heparin disposition contributes to variable dose requirements; body weight was the most consistent practical correlate, while smoking, sex, and treatment timing showed additional associations in this small cohort.

The investigators tried to replace empiric heparin dosing with a patient-specific kinetic workflow. Before treatment, each patient’s plasma was exposed to graded heparin concentrations and APTT response was modeled. After an approximately 70 U/kg IV bolus, serial APTTs were converted back to estimated heparin concentrations using the individual sensitivity curve. A one-compartment model then produced elimination rate, half-life, peak concentration, distribution volume, and clearance. Maintenance infusion dose was calculated as clearance × the heparin concentration predicted to achieve the desired APTT, with a target of 1.5–2.5 times baseline.

The study documented a roughly 500% spread in heparin sensitivity, half-lives from 0.4–1.6 h, clearance from 1.2–7.36 L/h, and whole-body-weight-normalized dose requirements averaging 13.9 U/kg/h (range 8.6–19.8). Weight correlated with distribution volume, clearance, and dose requirement. Smokers had shorter half-life and higher elimination rates; men had higher unadjusted clearance and weight-normalized dose requirements; treatment within 24 h of symptoms was associated with higher dose needs. A four-predictor dose model explained 78% of observed variance, but it was derived and assessed in only 20 patients with no external validation.

Principal caution: the paper’s strongest numbers are model-development associations, not validated treatment effects. The same APTT-based sensitivity relationship was used to estimate concentrations and to guide dosing, and clinical outcome events were too few to establish efficacy or safety.

Section condensation

Abstract / summary

The abstract frames the clinical problem as unpredictable anticoagulation with standardized heparin dosing. It summarizes the patient-specific sensitivity and kinetic method, reports large variability, and identifies smoking, sex, body weight, and time from symptom onset as candidate determinants of heparin kinetics or dose.

The abstract’s headline model is the dosage regression using body weight, sex, treatment delay, and smoking, which explained 78% of dose variance. This is a model-development result in the same small cohort, not external validation.

Introduction

The introduction argues that continuous infusion can be effective and that maintaining coagulation times within a therapeutic range is clinically important, yet fixed dosing yields variable anticoagulation. The authors propose that variability could arise from differences in coagulation response, monitoring methodology, or heparin elimination.

The stated study aim is to quantify heparin kinetics in patients with thromboembolic disease and relate kinetic parameters and dose requirements to patient variables.

Methods

Twenty patients were studied: 13 with lung-scan-confirmed pulmonary emboli and seven with venogram-confirmed DVT. Symptom-to-treatment delay ranged 4–120 h (median 24 h). Pretreatment plasma was exposed to 0–1 U/mL beef-lung heparin; APTT was measured in duplicate with a controlled reagent system. Log change in APTT versus log change in heparin concentration was fitted by linear regression for each patient.

After an approximately 70 U/kg IV bolus, APTTs were obtained at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 h and converted to estimated concentrations from the sensitivity curve. A one-compartment nonlinear fit produced Kd, half-life, and peak concentration; Vd = bolus/Cp and clearance = Kd×Vd. The maintenance infusion targeted 1.5–2.5× baseline APTT and was calculated as clearance×desired steady-state concentration, rounded to 50 U/h. The full individualization process took 3–4 h; APTT was reassessed 6–12 h later. Statistical analysis used Pearson correlations and forward stepwise multiple regression.

Results — sensitivity and model fit

Heparin sensitivity varied markedly: the authors report a roughly 500% range in sensitivity curves, and at 0.4 U/mL the APTT increase ranged 11–57 s. Each patient’s fitted-versus-measured pretreatment APTT correlation was ≥0.93. Sensitivity slope correlated with baseline APTT (r=0.46; p<0.05); the paper describes an inverse hematocrit–baseline APTT association while printing r=0.43, an internal sign inconsistency that should not be silently repaired.

The post-bolus concentration-time data were described as monoexponential. Mean measured-minus-fitted residuals stayed within about ±0.01 U/mL, which the authors interpreted as minimal bias for the one-compartment model.

Results — kinetics

Mean half-life was 0.88±0.29 h (range 0.4–1.6); mean elimination rate constant was 0.87±0.31 h⁻¹ (range 0.44–1.72). Smokers had shorter half-life (0.62 vs 0.97 h; p<0.05) and higher Kd (1.19 vs 0.77 h⁻¹; p<0.005). A sex/disease/age/smoking model explained 63% of Kd variance.

Mean distribution volume was 3.27±1.9 L (range 1.53–10.37) and correlated with lean body weight (r=0.60) and whole body weight (r=0.55). Mean clearance was 2.75±1.52 L/h (range 1.2–7.36). Men had higher unadjusted clearance than women (3.36 vs 1.83 L/h), and clearance correlated strongly with lean body weight (r=0.76). Once lean body weight entered the clearance model, sex and smoking did not add predictive information.

Results — dose requirements and follow-up

Maintenance doses ranged 500–2,000 U/h. Whole-body-weight-normalized dose averaged 13.9±3.6 U/kg/h (range 8.6–19.8) and correlated with both lean body weight (r=0.65) and whole body weight (r=0.76). Men had higher weight-normalized requirements than women (15.3 vs 11.8 U/kg/h), and patients treated within 24 h of symptom onset required larger doses than those treated later. The weight/sex/treatment-delay/smoking model explained 78% of dose variance.

Observed follow-up APTTs were 1.3–2.3× baseline (mean 1.7) versus a mean desired value of 1.9× baseline. Seven patients required no dose adjustment; adjustments ranged zero to four, with mean and median time to first adjustment of 4 days. No bleeding or recurrent thromboembolism was reported; one patient developed heparin-associated thrombocytopenia after 2 days and heparin was stopped.

Discussion

The authors interpret the findings as evidence that both pharmacodynamic sensitivity and disposition contribute to heparin dose variability. They emphasize body weight as a consistent determinant and suggest that smoking, sex, and timing after symptom onset may matter. They also use the findings to argue that larger doses may be needed early in treatment.

Importantly, the paper stops short of recommending the kinetic workflow for practice: it was time consuming and expensive. The closing suggestion is narrower—history of symptom timing, body weight, sex, and smoking may help inform initial dose requirements.

Individualization workflow

Inputs:
  baseline APTT + pretreatment plasma + patient variables
Process:
  1. Build individual in-vitro heparin concentration ↔ APTT curve.
  2. Give ~70 U/kg IV heparin bolus.
  3. Measure APTT at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 h.
  4. Convert APTT values to estimated heparin concentrations.
  5. Fit one-compartment model → Kd, t½, Cp.
  6. Calculate Vd = bolus/Cp; clearance = Kd×Vd.
  7. Select desired APTT (1.5–2.5× baseline) → desired steady-state concentration.
  8. Calculate infusion dose = clearance × desired concentration; round to nearest 50 U/h.
  9. Recheck APTT 6–12 h after starting infusion.
Output:
  individualized continuous IV heparin dose.
Operational burden:
  approximately 3–4 h before individualized infusion initiation.

Validity

Strengths: carefully specified assay procedure; duplicate APTT measurements; explicit timed post-bolus sampling; explicit one-compartment calculations; a residual check; separate raw and weight-standardized kinetic parameters; and prospective application of individualized doses with follow-up APTT measurement.

Major limitations: only 20 patients; no comparator arm; no prespecified power calculation reported; numerous pairwise correlations and stepwise regression with several candidate predictors; no correction for multiplicity described; no internal resampling or external validation; no confidence intervals around correlations/model performance; and only one thrombocytopenia event with zero bleeding/recurrent thrombosis, which is far too little event information for clinical safety/efficacy inference.

Measurement limitation: post-bolus “heparin concentrations” were inferred from APTT using each patient’s in-vitro sensitivity curve rather than independently measured by a direct heparin assay. The same biomarker relationship therefore contributes to exposure estimation, dose selection, and subsequent APTT assessment. That design is coherent for the authors’ mechanistic model but weakens independent validation.

Regression limitation: the 78% dose-variance model is especially vulnerable to optimism because four predictors were selected/evaluated in n=20. The paper gives no external test cohort and does not print a complete dose equation, so the R² should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than transportable performance.

Practice translation

The most durable finding is not a specific coefficient; it is the demonstration that UFH response and disposition are heterogeneous and that body size tracks several kinetic and dosing parameters. Smoking, sex, and symptom-to-treatment timing are interesting signals, but this dataset is too small to establish causal or clinically reliable adjustment rules.

The paper itself explicitly says the full kinetic method was time consuming, expensive, and not yet recommended. That source-level caution is consistent with the independent appraisal: use it to understand the historical rationale for individualized/weight-aware heparin dosing and monitoring, not to dose a current patient directly.

Contemporary context — external verification

This section is external to the 1981 paper. It is included only to satisfy the protocol’s currency check for clinical-practice interpretation.

A current U.S. DailyMed heparin sodium label (Pfizer; updated October 2025) describes current product as derived from porcine intestinal tissue and instructs dose adjustment according to coagulation test results. For continuous IV therapy it lists baseline coagulation assessment and continued aPTT monitoring; the label’s adequacy language uses aPTT 1.5–2 times normal. This supports the continuing principle of laboratory-guided individualization, but it does not validate the 1981 beef-lung-heparin sensitivity-curve/PK workflow or its regression coefficients.

Current label: DailyMed — Heparin Sodium Injection (Pfizer).

Implementation / practice takeaways

PreserveThe paper as historical evidence that UFH has substantial interpatient PK/PD variability and that body weight is strongly related to clearance and dose requirement.
Use cautiouslySmoking, sex, and treatment-delay associations as hypotheses/possible explanatory variables, not validated dosing modifiers.
Do not implementThe printed Kd equation or the four-variable dose model as a clinical dosing rule; neither was externally validated.
Do not overread outcomesZero bleeding and zero recurrent thromboembolism in 20 patients do not establish safety or efficacy.
Methodological lessonSeparate pharmacodynamic sensitivity from pharmacokinetic disposition when reasoning about “heparin resistance” or unexpectedly high/low dose requirements.
Research lessonA modern replication would need direct exposure measurement, prespecified modeling, larger multicenter cohorts, calibration/validation, and clinically meaningful comparative outcomes.
