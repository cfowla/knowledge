One-page clinical synthesis

Bottom line: In adults undergoing major noncardiac inpatient surgery with an estimated red-cell transfusion risk of at least 5%, a hospital policy of perioperative IV tranexamic acid reduced in-hospital red-cell transfusion from 9.8% to 7.4% (RR 0.73; 95% CI 0.61-0.86; adjusted absolute difference -2.7 percentage points; NNT 37) and met noninferiority for 90-day VTE (2.1% vs 2.1%; RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.65-1.38; noninferiority margin upper RR 1.46).

The trial is practice-relevant because it tested a hospital policy, not merely a tightly controlled individual dosing experiment. The population included diverse general, gynecologic, urologic, thoracic, vascular, spine, neurosurgical, and other major noncardiac surgeries, with oncologic procedures representing 60.5% of cases. It directly addresses a common reason for inconsistent TXA adoption: concern that antifibrinolysis could increase postoperative thrombosis, especially in cancer or other prothrombotic populations.

The practice implication is not “TXA for everyone without screening.” It is: eligible major noncardiac surgical patients should be considered for a standardized perioperative TXA pathway, with explicit exclusions for active thrombosis and other contraindications, renal-function safeguards for repeat dosing, IV-only medication-safety controls, and preservation of standard VTE prophylaxis. Evidence is insufficient to use this trial alone for active thromboembolic disease, pregnancy, pediatrics, free-flap surgery, trauma patients already treated with TXA, severe renal impairment dosing, or conclusions about rare arterial thrombosis, seizures, anaphylaxis, mortality, or length-of-stay benefit.

Population, exclusions, and applicability

Included population
  • Adults >=18 years.
  • Major noncardiac inpatient surgery.
  • Estimated red-cell transfusion risk >=5%.
  • Open procedures and laparoscopic procedures expected to last >=3 hours.
  • Mostly elective operations: 93% elective; 7% urgent/emergency.
  • Oncologic operations: 60.5% overall.
Major exclusions / non-applicable groups
  • Active thromboembolic disease.
  • Cardiac surgery and hip/knee total arthroplasty, where TXA is already standard in many settings.
  • Surgeries involving a free flap.
  • Trauma surgery if TXA had been given within the prior 3 hours.
  • Pregnancy.
  • Pediatric patients; outpatient/minor procedures; low-transfusion-risk cases were not the target population.
Applicability questionAnswer
Does this apply to major cancer surgery?Reasonably, for included oncologic procedures. Cancer surgery was well represented. However, the oncologic VTE subgroup CI was still wide enough that it should be considered reassuring, not definitive proof for every cancer surgery or very high-risk cancer-associated thrombosis scenario.
Does this apply to vascular surgery?Caution. Vascular surgery represented 7.0%; subgroup transfusion effect did not favor TXA numerically (RR 1.28; 95% CI 0.83-1.95), and VTE subgroup estimates were imprecise. Do not create a vascular-surgery mandate from this trial alone.
Does this apply to low-risk surgery?No direct TRACTION evidence. NICE 2026 is broader for adults in operating theatres with any bleeding risk, but this specific RCT studied high-transfusion-risk inpatient surgery.
Does this apply to patients with active VTE or active intravascular clotting?No. Active thromboembolic disease was excluded, and active intravascular clotting is a labeled contraindication.
Does this apply to renal impairment?Only cautiously. Median creatinine was approximately normal, and the trial report does not establish a renal-adjusted repeat-dose strategy. TXA is predominantly renally eliminated, so repeat dosing should be adjusted or avoided when renal dysfunction raises accumulation risk.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames a policy-level question: whether routine intraoperative TXA for major noncardiac surgery can reduce red-cell transfusion without an unacceptable increase in venous thromboembolism. The investigators randomized hospitals at 4-week intervals to TXA or placebo and used coprimary outcomes of in-hospital red-cell transfusion and 90-day VTE diagnosis, with VTE analyzed for noninferiority using an upper 95% CI relative-risk margin of 1.46.

Among 8273 analyzable patients, TXA reduced transfusion (7.4% vs 9.8%; RR 0.73; 95% CI 0.61-0.86) and met VTE noninferiority (2.1% vs 2.1%; RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.65-1.38). The authors conclude that a TXA hospital policy lowered transfusion and was noninferior to placebo for VTE.

Background

The introduction positions perioperative bleeding as a major driver of red-cell transfusion in the setting of constrained blood supply. TXA is inexpensive, widely available, and already supported in cardiac and orthopedic surgery, but uptake in major noncardiac surgery has been variable because of unresolved thrombotic-safety concerns.

The authors specifically cite POISE-3, which reduced major bleeding but did not establish noninferiority for a composite major cardiovascular safety outcome. TRACTION was designed to test a pragmatic hospital-wide TXA policy in major noncardiac surgery, with VTE safety emphasized over short-term bleeding endpoints alone.

Methods

TRACTION was a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, cluster-crossover trial across 10 Canadian hospitals. Hospitals alternated every 4 weeks between TXA and placebo policies. Eligible patients were adults undergoing major noncardiac inpatient surgery with estimated transfusion risk >=5%; exclusions included active thromboembolic disease, cardiac surgery, hip/knee arthroplasty, free-flap surgery, recent trauma TXA exposure, and pregnancy.

TXA dosing was 1 g IV at the start of surgery, 2 g if weight >100 kg, plus 1 g before skin closure. Cointerventions such as cell salvage, topical TXA, and thromboprophylaxis were documented but not protocolized. Outcomes were centrally and electronically ascertained; mixed-effects models accounted for cluster and period effects. The superiority analysis for transfusion used intention-to-treat; the primary noninferiority analysis for VTE used per-protocol with intention-to-treat as supportive.

Results

Of 8421 randomized patients, 8273 (98.2%) had successful data linkage for coprimary outcomes. Median age was 64 years, 47.1% were men, median weight was 80 kg, and median creatinine was 0.96 mg/dL among those with available creatinine. General surgery, gynecologic surgery, urologic surgery, thoracic surgery, vascular surgery, spine surgery, and neurosurgery were represented; 60.5% of procedures were oncologic.

TXA reduced red-cell transfusion during hospitalization from 9.8% to 7.4% (RR 0.73; adjusted difference -2.7 percentage points; NNT 37). Ninety-day VTE was 2.1% in both groups and met noninferiority. Red-cell units per 100 patients were lower at the patient level (24.7 vs 34.2; mean difference -9.5), while hospital-level unit difference crossed zero. MI, stroke, PE, ICU admission, hospital survival, and 90-day survival appeared similar, but event counts limited reliable comparative inference for rare events.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the trial as extending surgical TXA evidence by testing a hospital-level policy in a diverse noncardiac population with unusually high representation of cancer surgery. They emphasize that VTE within 90 days was not increased overall and that results support hemostatic use of TXA for major noncardiac operations.

Key limitations for practice are that participating hospitals had data-linkage capacity, some patients could not be linked, VTE ascertainment used administrative and imaging data rather than systematic screening, and subclinical events could be missed. Clinically, the safety endpoint was VTE, not a comprehensive rare-harm endpoint; arterial events, seizures, anaphylaxis, renal accumulation, and procedure-specific subgroups remain less certain.

Safety signals, contraindications, interactions, and monitoring

Contraindications / avoid
  • Neuraxial administration: contraindicated; IV use only.
  • Subarachnoid hemorrhage.
  • Active intravascular clotting or active thromboembolic disease.
  • Severe hypersensitivity to TXA or formulation components.
  • Trial-specific non-inclusion: pregnancy, free-flap surgery, cardiac surgery, hip/knee arthroplasty, and trauma cases with recent TXA.
Interactions / risk-amplifiers
  • Prothrombotic products: factor IX complex concentrates and anti-inhibitor coagulant concentrates.
  • Hormonal contraceptives: thrombotic-risk caution in label context.
  • Chlorpromazine: label notes potential increased bleeding risk.
  • Other seizure-threshold and renal-accumulation contexts: not direct interactions, but relevant safety modifiers.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for perioperative blood management and clinical pharmacy/anesthesia protocol design. Best use is as a high-quality implementation-supporting RCT for major noncardiac surgery TXA policy, not as a universal TXA safety license.
Relevance: 9/10Directly relevant to transfusion avoidance, perioperative pharmacotherapy, policy implementation, and medication-safety design. Lower if the institution already has broad TXA policy with outcome surveillance.
Novelty: 8/10Adds hospital-policy-level evidence and high oncologic representation beyond POISE-3 and smaller heterogeneous trials. Lower because TXA efficacy itself is not a new concept.
Method strength: 8.5/10Large double-blind cluster-crossover RCT with prespecified statistical plan and near-complete outcome linkage. Lower for reliance on administrative outcome capture and non-protocolized cointerventions.
Evidence strength: 8/10Strong for transfusion reduction and moderate for VTE noninferiority. Lower for rare harms, arterial thrombosis, severe renal impairment, and subgroup-specific certainty.
External validity: 7.5/10Procedure-diverse Canadian hospital sample with academic/community mix. Lower for settings without linked data, different transfusion thresholds, low-risk surgery, pediatric/obstetric patients, and excluded procedures.
Implementation value: 8.5/10Regimen, policy design, outcomes, and operational barriers are clear enough to inform order sets and governance. Higher with full protocol/supplement renal logic and local cost-effectiveness details.

Main strengths

  • Large sample with 8273 analyzable patients and no missing primary/secondary outcomes in linked datasets.
  • Double-blind cluster-crossover design estimates a real hospital-policy intervention.
  • Coprimary design required both transfusion benefit and VTE safety criterion.
  • High proportion of oncologic surgery increases relevance to prothrombotic surgical populations.
  • Effect size is easy to translate operationally: NNT 37 to prevent one red-cell transfusion during index hospitalization.

Main limitations

  • VTE capture relied on clinical/administrative diagnosis and may miss subclinical events.
  • Subgroup safety estimates are imprecise; some wide confidence intervals cannot exclude harm.
  • Trial excluded several high-concern groups, including active thromboembolic disease, pregnancy, free-flap surgery, and recent trauma TXA exposure.
  • Rare adverse events were too uncommon for reliable inference.
  • Renal impairment and repeat dosing require external label/local protocol translation; the trial did not establish renal-adjusted perioperative dosing.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate-high. Confidence is high for the central conclusion that TRACTION supports transfusion reduction without an overall clinically important VTE increase in eligible patients. Confidence is lower for procedure-specific subgroups, renal dosing, and rare safety outcomes.
