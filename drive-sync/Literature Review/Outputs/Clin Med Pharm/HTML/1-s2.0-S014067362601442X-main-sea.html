One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is pivotal phase 3 evidence for a potentially practice-changing once-weekly oral HIV-1 maintenance regimen. It supports non-inferior virological control through week 48 in a carefully selected suppressed population, but it is not a standalone basis for current prescribing or regulatory conclusions.

Core finding. Among 626 treated participants, week-48 HIV-1 RNA ≥50 copies/mL occurred in 1/314 (0.3%) with once-weekly islatravir–lenacapavir and 4/312 (1.3%) with daily standard of care. The adjusted difference was −1.0 percentage points (95.002% CI −3.0 to 1.1), satisfying the prespecified 4% non-inferiority margin but not superiority. Viral suppression below 50 copies/mL was 95.2% versus 95.5%.

Safety and immunology. Overall adverse-event frequency was similar (79% vs 77%), as were grade ≥3 adverse events (8% vs 9%) and serious adverse events (7% vs 9%). Investigator-attributed treatment-related adverse events were more frequent with the weekly regimen (18% vs <1%), although all were grade 1–2; one serious seizure was considered treatment-related in a participant who had missed antiseizure medication. Grade 3–4 direct bilirubin elevations were more frequent with islatravir–lenacapavir (9% vs 5%), while grade 3–4 decreased creatinine clearance was less frequent (3% vs 12%). The day-1-baseline CD4 analysis showed an LSM difference of −31 cells/µL (95% CI −58 to −4), but a post-hoc baseline averaging screening and day 1 attenuated this to −23 cells/µL (−47 to 2), and category/percentage changes were similar.

Adherence and experience. Median pill-count adherence to the weekly regimen was 100% and 98% of the adherence-analysis set took at least 90% of doses. Within the intervention group, 64% reported their prior daily regimen was more burdensome, 82% reported the weekly regimen was more convenient for their lifestyle, and 78% were more or much more satisfied. These findings are vulnerable to selection and measurement effects because participants were suppressed, had no prior virological failure, self-selected into a switch trial, adherence was measured by pill count, and preference results in this report were limited to the weekly group.

Clinical question / PICO

Population
Adults ≥18 years with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL on daily standard of care for ≥6 months; no prior virological failure; no prior islatravir/lenacapavir; no active/occult HBV infection.

Days 1–2: two tablets each containing islatravir 0.5 mg + lenacapavir 300 mg. From day 8: islatravir 2 mg + lenacapavir 300 mg orally once weekly.

Continue guideline-recommended oral standard of care, generally once daily.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction and research context

The authors frame daily adherence burden, privacy concerns, adherence-related anxiety, and treatment fatigue as reasons to develop longer-interval oral ART. Islatravir and lenacapavir have distinct mechanisms and pharmacokinetics supporting weekly dosing, and prior phase 2/companion phase 3 work supported advancing the combination.

The paper’s added-value claim is specific: ISLEND-2 tests switching from heterogeneous daily standard-of-care regimens to a weekly oral single-tablet regimen in a multinational phase 3 setting, complementing ISLEND-1.

Methods

Participants were randomised 1:1, stratified by geography, ART class, and CD4 category, with no masking. The primary endpoint used the FDA Snapshot algorithm. Efficacy used the full analysis set; safety used the treated safety set. The primary analysis used stratum-adjusted Mantel–Haenszel differences with a 95.002% CI after alpha penalties for planned monitoring. A 4% non-inferiority margin and ≥93% power target were prespecified.

CD4 change used a repeated-measures mixed model. Because of baseline variability, a post-hoc analysis used the average of screening and day-1 CD4 values as baseline. Adherence was estimated by pill count. The sponsors developed the protocol, collected data, monitored conduct, ran statistical analyses, and participated in interpretation, writing, and the publication decision.

Results — efficacy

Primary virological failure at week 48 was rare: 0.3% with weekly islatravir–lenacapavir versus 1.3% with standard of care, with adjusted difference −1.0 percentage points (95.002% CI −3.0 to 1.1). The upper CI bound was below 4%, establishing non-inferiority, but remained above 0%, so superiority was not shown. Per-protocol and sensitivity analyses were concordant.

Suppression below 50 copies/mL was 95.2% versus 95.5%. The single weekly-regimen participant with HIV-1 RNA ≥50 at week 48 had 104 copies/mL and later resuppressed. No interpretable resistance result from the high-viremia standard-of-care case was available because assays failed.

Results — CD4, adherence, and patient experience

Day-1-baseline CD4 changes were −45 versus −8 cells/µL, yielding an LSM difference of −31 (95% CI −58 to −4). The post-hoc averaged-baseline analysis yielded −23 (−47 to 2), and mean percentage changes and CD4 category shifts were similar. Absolute lymphocyte counts remained stable and no participant met discontinuation criteria for CD4 or lymphocyte decline.

Weekly-regimen pill-count adherence was very high (median 100%; 98% took ≥90% of doses). Patient-reported results favored weekly dosing for burden, convenience, and satisfaction, but the paper itself cautions that the trial enrolled people interested in switching and the preference measure reported here was limited to the intervention group.

Results — safety

Any adverse event: 79% vs 77%; grade ≥3 adverse event: 8% vs 9%; serious adverse event: 7% vs 9%. Treatment-related events were reported in 18% vs <1%, all grade 1–2. Two weekly-regimen and one standard-of-care participant discontinued because of adverse events. Three deaths occurred and none was treatment-related.

Grade 3–4 laboratory abnormalities occurred in 21% vs 26%. Direct bilirubin elevations were more frequent with the weekly regimen (9% vs 5%), whereas decreased creatinine clearance was less frequent (3% vs 12%). The report states most bilirubin elevations were small/not clinically significant, with alternative causes identified for more substantial abnormalities.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the trial as confirming non-inferior efficacy and generally acceptable tolerability, while emphasizing potential convenience and patient-centered value. They also treat the CD4 signal cautiously because of baseline imbalance/regression-to-the-mean concerns and call for longer follow-up.

Key limitations are open-label reporting bias, selected suppressed participants without prior failure, exclusion of HBV infection and lack of pregnancy evaluation, pill-count rather than detailed dose-timing adherence, optional reminder-tool use, self-selection for interest in weekly therapy, and lack of real-world preference/effectiveness data. The source only reports primary week-48 results.

Evaluation and appraisal

9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Pivotal phase 3 evidence for a complete once-weekly oral maintenance regimen in HIV-1.

Evidence basis: Direct comparison with contemporary daily oral standard of care in virologically suppressed adults.

Principal limit: Applies to a selected maintenance-treatment population rather than all people with HIV-1.

What would change it: Broader evidence in treatment-naive, viremic, and prior-failure populations or guideline incorporation would increase practical relevance.

9/10

Novelty

Rationale: A complete once-weekly oral HIV-1 regimen would materially change dosing frequency without requiring injections.

Evidence basis: ISLEND-2 tests the weekly single-tablet islatravir–lenacapavir strategy in a multinational phase 3 switch trial.

Principal limit: A companion phase 3 trial and earlier phase 2 work already establish part of the concept.

What would change it: Longer-term and real-world evidence demonstrating durable benefit beyond dosing convenience would raise the score.

External validity

Rationale: Multinational enrollment and demographic diversity improve transportability within suppressed maintenance populations.

Evidence basis: 14 countries/territories; 34% female at birth, 31% Black, 19% Asian, 20% Hispanic or Latine, 14% aged ≥65 years.

Principal limit: Excluded unsuppressed patients, prior virological failure, HBV infection, and study of pregnancy; interest in switching may select for favorable adherence/preference.

What would change it: Evidence in excluded populations and routine-care cohorts with adherence challenges would raise the score.

6/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper provides a clear dosing regimen and clinically useful efficacy/safety signals but is not sufficient by itself for implementation.

Evidence basis: Weekly maintenance dose is islatravir 2 mg plus lenacapavir 300 mg after day-1/day-2 loading; adherence and key safety outcomes are described.

Principal limit: At publication the combination is described as investigational; detailed missed-dose behavior, appendix algorithms, and real-world workflow data are not available in this task.

What would change it: Regulatory/guideline verification, full protocol/appendix review, and operational missed-dose guidance would increase implementation value.

Main risks to interpretation. Non-inferiority is supported for week-48 virological maintenance in the studied population, not for unsuppressed disease, prior virological failure, HIV/HBV coinfection, pregnancy, or adherence-challenged populations. Very low primary event counts make absolute differences imprecise despite the favorable CI. Open-label attribution likely affects treatment-related AE comparisons, and extensive sponsor involvement increases the importance of independent replication and transparent full-protocol review.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What this supports

Strong phase 3 evidence that a weekly oral islatravir–lenacapavir switch strategy can maintain suppression through week 48 in carefully selected, already-suppressed adults.

What to monitor in future use

HBV status/immunity, bilirubin/liver signals, CD4/lymphocyte trends, adherence and missed-dose behavior, and resistance if rebound occurs.

What not to infer

Do not extrapolate efficacy to active viraemia, prior virological failure, HIV/HBV coinfection, or pregnancy from this trial alone.

What would change practice confidence

Week-96 results, external/regulatory/guideline confirmation, full appendix/SAP review, and real-world effectiveness/adherence data.

Bottom line: The efficacy signal is convincing for non-inferiority in the studied maintenance population. The clinically important unanswered question is less “does weekly dosing suppress virus at week 48?” and more “how durable, forgiving, safe, and generalisable is this strategy outside a selected switch-trial population?”
