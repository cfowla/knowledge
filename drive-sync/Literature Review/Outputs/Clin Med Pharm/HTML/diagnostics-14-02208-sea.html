Scope warning. This is a narrative, ICU-focused synthesis rather than a systematic review or primary trial. It is useful for orientation, bedside framing, and locating source studies, but individual dosing thresholds, monitoring targets, and treatment decisions should be checked against current guidelines, drug-specific references, local assays, and the underlying primary evidence.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Pulmonary embolism is harder to prevent, recognize, risk-stratify, and treat in critically ill patients because the ICU itself creates VTE risk while also obscuring classic symptoms, confounding scores and biomarkers, altering anticoagulant pharmacokinetics, and increasing bleeding/procedural constraints.

What the source says. Pharmacologic prophylaxis remains central, with UFH, LMWHs, and fondaparinux represented; the review favors an ICU-aware approach to contraindications, thrombocytopenia, HIT, and anti-Xa monitoring. Diagnosis should not rely on emergency-department symptom patterns or D-dimer in isolation. CTPA remains the diagnostic test of choice, while bedside ultrasound, hemodynamic trends, and lower-extremity compression ultrasound can be especially useful when transport or clinical instability complicates standard workup. Risk stratification should use hemodynamic status, right-ventricular findings, sPESI/PESI context, and biomarkers cautiously because many abnormalities are common in critical illness.

Management frame. Low/intermediate-risk PE is generally managed with therapeutic anticoagulation; high-risk PE requires a reperfusion strategy; intermediate-high-risk patients warrant close monitoring and escalation if they deteriorate. The review emphasizes the reversibility and procedural flexibility of UFH in unstable/high-bleeding-risk patients, anti-Xa monitoring in selected LMWH/UFH scenarios, selective IVC-filter use, and systemic or catheter/surgical reperfusion according to risk and contraindications.

Independent appraisal. The review is highly useful as a clinically organized map of ICU PE problems and provides unusually practical tables for prophylaxis, anticoagulation, biomarkers, and thrombolysis. Its main weakness is evidence methodology: there is no explicit systematic search strategy, study-selection process, risk-of-bias assessment, or evidence grading, and many ICU-specific recommendations are extrapolated from non-ICU populations or lower-level evidence. The article should therefore function as a synthesis and citation hub rather than the final authority for protocol construction.

Section condensation

1. Introduction

The review frames ICU PE as a preventable but frequently missed source of morbidity and mortality. It identifies mechanical ventilation, immobility, inflammation/infection, endothelial/coagulation dysfunction, central venous catheters, sedatives, paralytics, and vasopressors as contributors to thromboembolic risk. It reports substantial diagnostic underrecognition: post-mortem series suggest only about one-third of ICU PE is diagnosed, while one CT-based mechanically ventilated cohort found PE in 18.7% with 60% clinically silent.

The authors emphasize that classical PE symptoms are often unavailable or nonspecific in sedated, mechanically ventilated patients and that ICU populations are underrepresented in the studies underlying major guidelines. This establishes the review's central problem: guideline-based PE care must be adapted to a setting where the disease is both more difficult to recognize and more difficult to treat safely.

2. Thromboembolic Prophylaxis

UFH, LMWHs, and fondaparinux are summarized as pharmacologic prophylaxis options, with the review highlighting a shift toward LMWH and citing PROTECT as demonstrating lower PE rates with LMWH versus UFH without an increase in bleeding complications or bleeding-related death. Table 1 provides prophylactic regimens. The authors then focus on common ICU barriers: active or intracranial bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, heparin allergy, and serious coagulation disorders. They cite a practical platelet threshold above 50,000 for prophylaxis while acknowledging limited supporting data; at 20,000–50,000, they describe cautious individualized prophylaxis with close monitoring. Mechanical prophylaxis, especially intermittent pneumatic compression, is an alternative when pharmacologic prophylaxis cannot be given.

HIT receives a dedicated discussion: ICU differential diagnosis is difficult because sepsis, drugs, surgery, and bleeding also cause thrombocytopenia. Confirmation requires anti-PF4/heparin antibody testing; on suspicion, all heparin should be stopped and an alternative parenteral anticoagulant started, with bivalirudin or argatroban favored in the critically ill. The review also describes anti-Xa monitoring as a tool when organ failure or other ICU pharmacokinetic changes make LMWH exposure uncertain, reporting a prophylactic twice-daily enoxaparin target of 0.2–0.4 IU/mL 3–4 hours after dosing once steady dosing has been established, while explicitly deferring to local assay/agent protocols.

3. Clinical Suspicion, Diagnosis, and Risk Stratification

Clinical suspicion is central because chest pain, dyspnea, and hemoptysis may be absent or masked. Persistent unexplained hypoxemia, tachycardia, and hypotension are emphasized as common triggers. Geneva and Wells scores can structure pre-test probability but were not developed for ICU populations and contain variables that critical illness often distorts. Continuous monitoring can reveal right-ventricular strain patterns, falling cardiac output, elevated CVP, or other changes, but these are not PE-specific.

CTPA remains the preferred diagnostic imaging test; the review cites 83% sensitivity and 96% specificity, while noting transport, contrast, radiation, and renal concerns. Bedside echocardiography and compression ultrasound are useful when instability limits transport; McConnell and 60/60 signs are relatively specific but uncommon. Triple-POCUS has ED evidence but lacks ICU validation. D-dimer is considered poorly discriminatory and not recommended for ICU PE diagnosis. Risk stratification follows hemodynamic instability, sPESI/PESI, right-ventricular dysfunction, and myocardial-injury biomarkers, yet all are susceptible to ICU confounding; trends and baseline echocardiography may be more informative than isolated values.

3.1. Continuous Hemodynamic Monitoring

The authors describe new/persistent hypoxemia, tachypnea, hypocapnia (or hypercapnia during mechanical ventilation), S1Q3T3 and other right-strain ECG changes, new atrial fibrillation, and nonspecific ST changes as clues rather than diagnostic findings. Invasive/advanced monitoring may show elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, CVP >20 mmHg, CVP exceeding wedge pressure, cardiac index <2 L/min/m², stroke-volume index <30 mL/m², or mixed venous oxygen saturation <55% in right-ventricular dysfunction.

Massive PE may produce falling pressure/cardiac output/stroke volume with rising CVP and systemic vascular resistance, while wedge pressure may remain relatively unchanged. The value of these signals is longitudinal: abrupt deviations from the patient's baseline can prompt targeted evaluation even when no single monitor variable is specific for PE.

3.2. Imaging and Other Diagnostic Tests

CTPA is positioned as the diagnostic anchor; lung scintigraphy is rarely practical in the ICU. Echocardiography can show right-heart thrombus, RV dilation/dysfunction, septal flattening, IVC dilation, reduced TAPSE, reduced tricuspid S′ velocity, McConnell sign, and the 60/60 sign. Lower-extremity compression ultrasound is particularly valuable because a proximal DVT in a high-suspicion patient can support immediate therapeutic action.

Lung ultrasound is mainly useful for alternative diagnoses; triple-POCUS integrates cardiac, lung, and leg ultrasound but has not been validated in ICU populations. D-dimer retains negative predictive utility only in low pre-test probability populations and is undermined by the high prevalence of inflammation, malignancy, trauma, surgery, liver disease, and advanced age in the ICU; the authors therefore advise against relying on it in this setting.

3.3. Risk Stratification

The review uses high, intermediate-high, intermediate-low, and low early-mortality risk categories based on hemodynamic instability, sPESI, RV dysfunction, and troponin. It explicitly shows why the ICU can make each criterion noisy: vasopressors, baseline hypoxemia, mechanical ventilation/ARDS or chronic cardiopulmonary disease, and non-PE troponin elevation are common. sPESI is summarized as 1.0% 30-day mortality at 0 points and 10.9% at ≥1 point, but heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation may reflect the underlying critical illness rather than PE severity.

Hemodynamic instability is defined by cardiac arrest, SBP <90 mmHg, vasopressor requirement to maintain SBP ≥90 mmHg despite fluids, or a ≥40 mmHg SBP drop lasting >15 minutes. RV dilation/contrast reflux on CTPA and echo findings support prognostication. Biomarkers such as troponin, BNP/NT-proBNP, lactate, renal markers, sodium, and copeptin can add information, but sepsis, renal failure, arrhythmias, and other comorbidities materially limit specificity.

4. Management of Acute PE in the ICU

Supportive care is organized around preserving right-ventricular function. Hypoxemia should be treated, but positive-pressure ventilation and hypercapnia can increase RV afterload; if intubation is unavoidable, the authors emphasize hemodynamic preparation and low ventilatory pressures. Norepinephrine is presented as the preferred catecholamine for blood-pressure support and dobutamine below 10 µg/kg/min as the preferred inotrope for RV contractility; excessive fluids are discouraged because RV distension can worsen output.

Risk then determines specific therapy: therapeutic anticoagulation for low/intermediate-risk PE; reperfusion for high-risk PE; and close monitoring with immediate reperfusion if intermediate-high-risk patients deteriorate. VA-ECMO is considered an adjunct to reperfusion or salvage option in refractory collapse/cardiac arrest, but randomized efficacy/safety evidence is absent. PERTs may increase use of advanced therapies, although outcome benefit remains unproven.

4.1. Therapeutic Anticoagulation

Parenteral anticoagulants are emphasized for ICU treatment. The authors highlight ICU-specific reasons LMWH may become less predictable: impaired SC absorption, renal dysfunction, fluid/vasopressor-driven changes in volume of distribution, and unexpected invasive procedures. For twice-daily therapeutic LMWH, a peak anti-Xa target of 0.5–1.2 IU/mL around 4 hours after administration is reported, with repeated caution to use drug- and laboratory-specific local standards.

Table 6 lists UFH, dalteparin, enoxaparin, nadroparin, tinzaparin, and fondaparinux regimens. UFH is favored when rapid discontinuation/reversal is valuable or when hemodynamic deterioration may lead to reperfusion. The table reports an aPTT target of 1.5–2.3× control for continuous UFH; the authors also note that anti-Xa monitoring may be useful when aPTT is discordant and high UFH doses are otherwise being escalated.

4.2. Inferior Vena Cava Filters

IVC filters are reserved for selected situations: lower-extremity DVT with an absolute anticoagulation contraindication, recurrent PE despite therapeutic anticoagulation, or a necessary delay before anticoagulation. Routine filter use for PE prevention is discouraged. Filters should be removed once no longer required; complications include venous-wall penetration, filter embolization, thrombosis, and infection concerns in septic or high-risk ICU patients.

4.3. Reperfusion Therapies

Systemic thrombolysis is presented for high-risk PE and selected intermediate-high-risk patients, with major bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage as the main hazards. The review lists absolute and relative contraindications and reconstructs rtPA, streptokinase, and urokinase regimens. For intermediate-high-risk patients, the source favors observation and rescue escalation rather than routine lysis because bleeding can offset hemodynamic benefit.

If systemic thrombolysis is contraindicated or fails, surgical embolectomy and catheter-directed interventions are alternatives. The authors stress major evidence gaps: no randomized comparison of surgery versus systemic thrombolysis and insufficiently powered high-risk studies defining the comparative effectiveness of catheter-based approaches.

5. Conclusions

The authors conclude that ICU PE requires individualized decisions because thrombocytopenia, bleeding risk, renal impairment, altered distribution, procedural needs, and thrombolysis contraindications disrupt simple guideline pathways. Diagnostic uncertainty is similarly amplified by sepsis, delirium, acute coronary syndromes, ventilator dyssynchrony, hypovolemia, and non-PE shock.

They explicitly identify the exclusion of critically ill patients from many randomized trials as a central evidence limitation, making prospective observational studies and case series unusually important in this population. This is a defensible clinical observation but also explains why many recommendations in the review remain lower-certainty or extrapolative.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first — as an ICU-specific orientation and citation map, not as a sole protocol authority.
9/10
Relevance

Directly addresses ICU PE, heparin/LMWH selection, HIT, anti-Xa monitoring, bleeding constraints, and reperfusion.

Evidence basis: the entire review is organized around ICU-specific prevention/diagnosis/management. Limiter: some content extends beyond heparin. Would change: lower if the task were general ambulatory PE; higher is not needed for this cluster.

6/10
Novelty

Its added value is the ICU lens and integration of monitoring/logistical problems more than novel evidence.

Evidence basis: practical cross-walk of guideline concepts to critical illness. Limiter: most underlying evidence predates the review. Would rise: new prospective ICU-specific comparative data or a validated ICU algorithm.

4/10
Method strength

The review does not report a reproducible systematic search, study-selection process, risk-of-bias method, or evidence-grading framework.

Evidence basis: mapped source contains no methods section for evidence synthesis. Limiter: narrative selection can introduce citation/selection bias. Would rise: systematic methods, duplicate selection/extraction, and formal certainty assessment.

5/10
Evidence strength

Useful claims are supported by guidelines, trials, observational studies, and reviews, but ICU-specific evidence is often sparse and heterogeneous.

Evidence basis: source itself repeatedly notes lack of ICU validation/RCT data. Limiter: secondary synthesis without formal certainty grading. Would rise: high-quality ICU RCTs or prospective validation.

6/10
External validity

The target population is explicitly ICU patients, improving face applicability, but the evidence base frequently comes from non-ICU or mixed populations.

Evidence basis: authors emphasize critical-care confounding and trial underrepresentation. Limiter: trauma, neurocritical, septic, renal-failure, and postoperative patients differ substantially. Would rise: multi-center ICU-specific validation across these subgroups.

8/10
Implementation value

High practical utility from dosing tables, anti-Xa targets, HIT management framing, diagnostic caveats, and risk-to-treatment mapping.

Evidence basis: 7 tables plus a supportive-care figure. Limiter: some doses/thresholds require local and current verification. Would rise: explicit algorithm, evidence grades, and validated dosing/monitoring pathways.

Main appraisal constraint. Several statements look protocol-ready because they contain specific doses or thresholds, but the article is not a dosing guideline and does not independently establish those values. Treat such entries as leads to the cited primary studies/guidelines and verify drug/assay/local-protocol details before implementation.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Useful nowUse the paper as a map for ICU-specific failure modes: occult PE, score/biomarker confounding, renal/volume-distribution effects on anticoagulants, HIT differential diagnosis, and procedural bleeding constraints.
Heparin-specific valueHigh-yield source nodes include prophylactic/therapeutic UFH and LMWH regimens, reasons to favor UFH when reversibility matters, aPTT/anti-Xa discordance, and anti-Xa monitoring windows/targets reported by the review.
Buildable CDS conceptsSeparate risk stratification from ICU confounders; surface renal function, platelet count, active/recent bleeding, HIT suspicion, invasive-procedure plans, and hemodynamic instability before recommending anticoagulant strategy.
Do not overclaimDo not encode the review's platelet or anti-Xa thresholds as universal truth without tracing the cited sources and confirming assay-, drug-, population-, and institution-specific applicability.

Currency verification (external; 12 Aug 2026)

The European Society of Cardiology's current clinical-practice page still identifies the 2019 Acute Pulmonary Embolism guideline as the active ESC PE guideline. The ESC publication schedule lists a new Pulmonary embolism guideline planned for 2027. This means the 2024 review's major ESC guideline frame has not yet been superseded, but a guideline refresh is formally scheduled; protocol-level reuse should be rechecked when the 2027 document appears.
