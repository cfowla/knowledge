One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This paper is unusually close to real clinical-pharmacy use. Its strongest lesson is not which model ranked first. The important result is that polished, relevant, context-aware drug-information answers can still fail source verification.

The study submitted 102 real drug-information questions from a Thai university-hospital DIC to seven general-purpose LLM configurations. Each model answered every question, yielding 714 responses. The evaluation separated answer concordance from four other properties: clarity in Thai, relevance, context awareness, and citation credibility. A three-rater pilot on 10 separate questions showed agreement coefficients of 0.79-0.86. The main 102-question set was then split among three assessors, so each response received one main-study rating.

Performance looked strong if judged by presentation and task fit. Clarity ranged from 85.25% to 92.75% of the scale maximum. Relevance ranged from 0.97 to 1.00, and context awareness from 0.90 to 0.99. Concordance with pharmacist responses was weaker and more variable at 0.70-0.86. Citation credibility was poor for every model at 0.03-0.36. The omnibus concordance comparison was significant, but after Holm correction only ChatGPT-5.2 Thinking versus Copilot Think Deeper remained significant. Those model labels and their relative ordering should be treated as a time-bound November 2025 snapshot, not a lasting product hierarchy.

Main safety finding. Citation quality breaks the link between a plausible answer and an auditable answer. The source reports non-specific citations, inaccessible links, hallucinated or irrelevant sources, and some non-academic sources across the evaluated systems. A pharmacist cannot safely treat fluent prose as evidence.

Section condensation

Abstract and background

The paper frames general-purpose LLMs as possible assistants for hospital drug-information work, especially preliminary retrieval, drafting, and organization. It also treats source verification as a separate requirement because DIC answers can influence medication decisions. The study question is practical: can widely available LLMs answer historical Thai-language DIC questions clearly and contextually while agreeing with pharmacist answers and supplying credible citations?

The background cites prior medical and pharmacy chatbot evaluations but identifies a gap in multi-model, non-English, real-world DIC comparisons. The paper does not claim that an LLM can replace drug-information resources or pharmacist review.

Methods

The investigators screened DIC records from 2024-2025 and included 102 questions covering dosage, administration, special populations, stability or compatibility, interactions, adverse effects, vaccines, and therapeutic use. They excluded product availability, cost, drug identification, and records missing patient information required for an answer. Original questions and pharmacist answers were in Thai. The study received an ethics exemption identified in the paper as REC.68-41-19-9.

Seven source-reported model configurations answered each question in a new chat during November 2025. A standardized prompt asked for an answer suited to Thai medication use and treatment guidance plus references and accessible links. The rubric used five domains. Four were binary; clarity used a 5-point Likert scale. Three experts reviewed rubric content using IOC. A separate 10-question pilot produced 70 responses and 210 three-rater rating instances. After that calibration, each of the 714 main responses was rated by one assessor. Binary model comparisons used Cochran Q, followed by Holm-adjusted McNemar tests when appropriate.

Results

Pilot agreement was substantial to almost perfect for the binary domains and good for clarity. Concordance ranged from 0.70 to 0.86. Four configurations met the study's 0.80 acceptability threshold: ChatGPT-5.2 Thinking, Gemini 3 Thinking, Perplexity Pro, and Gemini 3 Fast. The concordance omnibus test was significant at Q=18.47, df=6, p=0.005, but only one pair remained significant after correction.

Clarity, relevance, and context awareness were uniformly high, while citation credibility was uniformly low. The mismatch is the main result: the systems could write clear, focused, context-sensitive answers without supplying sources that met the study's traceability and credibility rule. Table 7 shows different citation failure patterns by model, including non-specific links, inaccessible links, hallucinated or irrelevant sources, and non-academic sources.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors argue for a human-in-the-loop role. They view LLMs as possible tools for preliminary information work and drafting, not replacements for pharmacist-led evidence verification. They also caution that model comparisons depend on version, prompt, language, and question type. The paper separates citation credibility from answer plausibility and notes that a clinically plausible answer can still rest on weak or unverifiable sources.

The paper's own limitations are substantial. It is single-center, the question set may not cover broader practice, model versions will drift, one prompt was used, four domains used binary scoring, and every main response had one assessor. The original pharmacist source mix was not systematically classified, and specialized medical or institution-specific retrieval systems were not tested. The conclusion therefore supports supervised use and further implementation study, not autonomous deployment.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance9/10

Rationale. The paper directly tests pharmacist-facing LLM use on real drug-information questions and reports source-verification failures that matter to clinical workflows.

Evidence basis. Real hospital DIC questions, seven model configurations, source-verification domain, full question supplement.

Limiting factor. One Thai university hospital and a November 2025 model snapshot.

What would raise the score. Replication in several health systems and languages with current model builds.

Novelty6/10

Rationale. The study adds a contemporary multi-model, Thai-language DIC comparison with a distinct citation-credibility analysis, but the core question has prior precedent.

Evidence basis. Seven-model comparison, real-world historical questions, citation taxonomy and supplement.

Limiting factor. It extends rather than replaces prior drug-information chatbot evaluations.

What would raise the score. Prospective workflow testing, repeated runs, and retrieval-enabled clinical systems would add more.

Method strength6/10

Rationale. The paired question set, predefined rubric, content-validity step, pilot reliability testing, and multiplicity correction are useful, but the main dataset uses one rater per response.

Evidence basis. 102 paired questions, 714 responses, 10-question three-rater pilot, Cochran Q and Holm-adjusted McNemar tests.

Limiting factor. Single main rater, binary scoring for four domains, no repeated generations, and no independent clinical gold-standard adjudication.

What would raise the score. Duplicate blinded main ratings, expert adjudication against primary evidence, repeated runs, and prespecified error severity.

Evidence strength5/10

Rationale. The data support descriptive claims about this benchmark and show a clear source-verification problem, but they do not establish autonomous clinical safety or current model rankings.

Evidence basis. Concordance 0.70-0.86, citation credibility 0.03-0.36, full citation-error table, significant concordance omnibus test.

Limiting factor. No patient outcomes, single center, source-reported model snapshots, and pharmacist answers used as the concordance reference.

What would raise the score. Independent replication with current models, clinical-outcome or decision-impact endpoints, and source-level correctness adjudication.

External validity4/10

Rationale. The question set is clinically plausible, but language, local practice, model versions, and site-specific question mix constrain transfer.

Evidence basis. Historical DIC records cover eight drug-information categories and preserve original Thai wording.

Limiting factor. One Thai tertiary center; administration questions make up 36 of 102; specialized and local RAG systems were excluded.

What would raise the score. Multicenter studies across languages, institutions, question mixes, and clinical AI architectures.

Implementation value8/10

Rationale. The study gives a usable safety lesson: a clear, relevant answer still needs evidence checking, and citation traceability should be measured separately from answer quality.

Evidence basis. Five-domain rubric, citation-error taxonomy, 102-question supplement, model-level profiles.

Limiting factor. It does not test turnaround time, pharmacist workload, downstream decisions, or an actual human-in-the-loop deployment.

What would raise the score. Prospective DIC pilots with time savings, correction burden, source verification, and patient-safety measures.

Strengths

  • Uses real historical DIC questions rather than exam questions alone.
  • Separates linguistic quality, task relevance, context handling, answer concordance, and citation credibility.
  • Applies the same questions and prompt across seven configurations.
  • Includes a content-validity step and three-rater pilot before the main evaluation.
  • Reports a full citation-failure taxonomy and model-level error profiles.
  • Supplies the complete 102-question dataset in the supplement.

Critical limitations

  • The main study does not retain duplicate independent ratings. One assessor rated each response after the pilot.
  • Pharmacist-provided answers are a pragmatic concordance standard, not an independently adjudicated evidence standard.
  • Binary scoring compresses partial correctness and clinical severity into the same 0 or 1 outcome.
  • The study does not measure reproducibility across repeated generations or prompt variants.
  • The single-center Thai setting and question mix limit generalization.
  • The model labels are time-bound snapshots. Product behavior can change after the November 2025 response period.
  • No patient outcomes, downstream clinical decisions, pharmacist correction burden, or time savings were measured.

Bottom line. Use this paper to design pharmacist-facing evaluation and governance. Do not use it to select an autonomous drug-information model or to treat a model's fluent answer as verified evidence.

Implementation takeaways

Keep citation verification separate.
Score answer correctness and source traceability as different properties. A model can do well on one and fail the other.
Require pharmacist review.
The study supports drafting assistance, not unsupervised clinical use.
Use the 102-question supplement as a test seed.
It is a useful real-world benchmark set for building local evaluation cases, but a local system should add institution-specific questions and adjudicated answers.
Measure correction burden.
A deployment study should record how often pharmacists rewrite answers, replace citations, retrieve restricted sources, or reject the output.
Freeze model and prompt metadata.
Store the model configuration, date, prompt, tool access, and retrieval settings with every evaluation run. Product names alone are not enough for reproducibility.
Add source-level evidence checks.
For clinical use, verify that each cited source exists, is accessible through the intended workflow, and actually supports the claim it is attached to.
