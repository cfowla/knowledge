One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In this institution's anti-Xa–monitored VTE UFH protocol, initiating UFH by adjusted body weight was associated with a shorter median time to first therapeutic anti-Xa than total-body-weight dosing. The signal was concentrated in patients aged ≥65 years and in the initial-bolus subgroup; the higher-weight-only subgroup did not show a statistically significant difference.

The study compared a 2019 TBW protocol era with a 2021 AdjBW protocol era. UFH was initiated at 18 units/kg/hour, with optional bolus dosing, and anti-Xa was targeted to 0.3–0.7 units/mL. The final sample was deliberately balanced so each 40-patient cohort contained the same age/weight strata. Median time to first therapeutic anti-Xa was 20.9 hours with TBW versus 13.6 hours with AdjBW; the Hodges-Lehmann point estimate for the difference was 5.3 hours (95% CI 0.2–9.9; p=0.02).

Clinical interpretation is narrower than the headline result. The age-only subgroup showed a statistically significant difference (21.9 vs 13.6 hours; p=0.026), but the weight-only subgroup did not (13.9 vs 11.2 hours; p=0.47). Major bleeding was 10% vs 5% (p=0.68) and mortality 7.5% vs 10% (p=1), leaving clinical safety and efficacy unresolved. The source also contains an internal numerical inconsistency for the “therapeutic anti-Xa values within 24 hours” endpoint: narrative text reports 40% vs 53%, p=0.02, while Table 2 reports 38.8% vs 56.2%, p=0.01.

Section condensation

Abstract / Highlights

The article frames optimal UFH initiation in older and higher-weight adults as uncertain because of pharmacokinetic differences and over-anticoagulation concerns. It reports a shorter time to therapeutic anti-Xa with AdjBW and explicitly states that larger prospective studies are needed for clinical safety and efficacy outcomes.

Background

The rationale is that UFH distribution and dose requirement may not scale linearly with total body weight, while aging may alter heparin response. The institution changed its protocol from TBW to AdjBW for adults age ≥65 years or weight ≥100 kg, creating the natural pre-post comparison. Prior literature cited by the authors included obesity-focused dosing studies and age-adjusted heparin work, but the paper positions itself as a direct anti-Xa comparison in older adults.

Methods — design and patients

Adults with suspected/confirmed VTE treated on a standardized UFH infusion for at least 24 hours were screened. UFH started at 18 units/kg/hour with or without bolus doses; anti-Xa was checked about six hours after initiation/rate changes and titrated to 0.3–0.7 units/mL. Eligibility required age ≥65 years and/or weight ≥100 kg. Protocol deviations, pregnancy, incarceration, ECMO, recent oral factor-Xa inhibitor exposure, missing height, and height ≤152.4 cm were exclusion criteria.

The TBW cohort came from July–September 2019 and the AdjBW cohort from July–September 2021. The main PDF does not state the formula used to calculate AdjBW and refers to Online Resource 1 for detailed titration/bolus protocol.

Methods — endpoints and analysis

The primary endpoint was median time to first therapeutic anti-Xa. Secondary endpoints were first anti-Xa in range and therapeutic anti-Xa measurements in the first 24 hours. Safety endpoints were in-hospital mortality and ISTH major bleeding. The primary endpoint used Mann-Whitney U testing with a Hodges-Lehmann median-difference estimate; categorical endpoints used chi-square or Fisher exact tests. Age, weight, and combined strata were planned; bolus/no-bolus analysis was post hoc.

Results

Among 571 screened patients, 90 met eligibility criteria and 10 were randomly excluded to balance strata, leaving 40 per cohort. Each cohort had 27 age-only patients, 11 weight-only patients, and 2 meeting both criteria. Concomitant antithrombotic medication use differed at baseline (62.5% TBW vs 85% AdjBW; p=0.02), while other listed baseline characteristics were not statistically different.

The primary endpoint favored AdjBW (20.9 vs 13.6 hours; point estimate 5.3 hours, 95% CI 0.2–9.9; p=0.02). The age-only and initial-bolus subgroups favored AdjBW, while the weight-only and no-bolus subgroups did not reach statistical significance. First therapeutic anti-Xa proportion was not significantly different (30% vs 43%; p=0.24). Figure 2 shows fewer supratherapeutic first levels with AdjBW (p=0.04). Mortality and major bleeding were not statistically different.

Discussion / Conclusion

The authors interpret the result as support for age-sensitive UFH dosing and suggest AdjBW may also be reasonable for higher-weight patients despite the nonsignificant weight-only subgroup. They also propose that AdjBW-based bolus dosing may help reduce over-anticoagulation. These latter interpretations extend beyond the direct strength of the clinical outcome data because bleeding and VTE treatment outcomes were not powered to show benefit.

Limitations include retrospective single-center design, institution-specific protocol, delayed/hemolyzed anti-Xa sampling, a laboratory primary endpoint, small subgroup sizes, exclusion of recent oral factor-Xa inhibitor exposure, and restricted COVID-era generalizability. The authors appropriately call for a larger prospective randomized trial.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin deep dive; do not use alone for practice change. The paper is highly relevant and offers a directly usable hypothesis for older adults, but the evidence remains small, retrospective, single-center, surrogate-endpoint–driven, and internally inconsistent on one secondary endpoint.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses UFH dosing, anti-Xa monitoring, older adults, higher weight, and VTE. Limiting factor: narrow single-center protocol. Raise: multi-center replication in comparable anti-Xa protocols.

Novelty
7/10

Direct comparison of AdjBW vs TBW using anti-Xa in older adults adds value beyond obesity-only/aPTT literature. Limiting factor: observational pre-post design. Raise: prospective randomized evaluation.

Method strength
5/10

Clear protocol, prespecified primary outcome, nonparametric analysis, and balanced strata. Limiting factors: n=80, era confounding, random exclusion of 10 eligible patients, post-hoc bolus analysis, and baseline antithrombotic imbalance. Raise: prospective allocation, larger sample, adjusted analysis.

Evidence strength
5/10

The primary laboratory endpoint has a quantified effect and CI, but clinical outcomes are sparse and nonsignificant. Limiting factors: surrogate endpoint, small safety event counts, secondary-endpoint discrepancy. Raise: patient-centered efficacy/safety endpoints with adequate power.

External validity
4/10

Applicability is constrained by one institution, one anti-Xa protocol, exclusion of recent factor-Xa inhibitors, height restriction, and selected historical windows. Raise: multi-center pragmatic validation across diverse nomograms.

Implementation value
5/10

Provides age/weight triggers, 18 units/kg/hr initiation, anti-Xa target, and monitoring cadence. Limiting factor: the main PDF omits the AdjBW calculation formula and detailed titration/bolus nomogram contained in supplementary material. Raise: validated supplement plus reproducible protocol and outcome replication.

Validity and bias

Selection: 10 otherwise eligible patients were randomly excluded to equalize age/weight strata. This improves stratum balance but makes the analyzed sample less purely consecutive. Temporal confounding: cohorts are separated by two years, so unmeasured changes in staffing, testing, VTE case mix, or supportive care could contribute. Measurement: delayed anti-Xa draws and hemolysis can extend measured time-to-target independently of pharmacodynamic response. Multiplicity/power: subgroup analyses are small, with the bolus comparison post hoc; the weight-only subgroup is explicitly underpowered.

Clinical pharmacotherapy lens

The mechanistic premise is plausible and the anti-Xa target is operationally clear, but this paper tests a dosing strategy within one institutional framework rather than a universal pharmacokinetic rule. The source does not establish the exact AdjBW formula in the main article. Safety interpretation is especially limited: six total major bleeds and seven deaths are insufficient to infer meaningful clinical superiority, and concomitant antithrombotic use differed between cohorts.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use as a high-priority evidence node when evaluating whether older adults on anti-Xa–monitored UFH may benefit from an age-sensitive or AdjBW-based initiation strategy.

Do not overclaim

Do not claim proven reduction in bleeding, mortality, recurrent VTE, or superiority in higher-weight adults under age 65. Those outcomes were not established.

Protocol design implication

If testing locally, preserve the study's separation of age-only and weight-only effects and define the exact AdjBW formula, bolus logic, monitoring timing, and anti-Xa target before implementation.

Validation need

Prospective or pragmatic multi-center evaluation should measure time-to-target plus bleeding, recurrent/progressive VTE, transfusion, length of stay, and protocol deviations.

Bottom line: The study provides a credible signal that AdjBW initiation can shorten anti-Xa time-to-target in older adults, but it is best treated as protocol-development evidence rather than practice-defining evidence.
