The update converts selected 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA heart failure guideline recommendations into operational measures. Performance measures are intended for national quality programs, public reporting, or pay-for-performance when evidence and measure maturity are sufficient. Quality measures are intended for quality improvement when evidence or implementation maturity is not yet adequate for those uses.

The new performance measures cover four-class HFrEF guideline-directed medical therapy at discharge, blood pressure control in HFpEF, and SGLT2 inhibitor treatment in symptomatic HFrEF. The six quality measures cover SGLT2 inhibitor treatment in HFmrEF and HFpEF, social-needs screening with action, pregnancy counseling, continuation of therapy in HFimpEF, optimization of therapy before TEER for secondary mitral regurgitation, and monoclonal protein screening when bone scintigraphy is used for suspected cardiac amyloidosis.

Bottom line. This is a high-implementation-value specification document rather than a new efficacy guideline. Its main contribution is translating established or emerging heart failure recommendations into measurable care processes with explicit denominators, exclusions, exceptions, data sources, settings, and measurement periods.

Development method and evidence basis

The Joint Committee convened the update in 2023 and compared the 2020 measure set with the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA heart failure guideline and other public measure sets. The committee kept every 2020 measure unchanged and added nine new measures.

Performance measures were generally drawn from Class 1 or Class 3 recommendations. Quality measures could remain below performance-measure status when the guideline recommendation, evidence base, or implementation testing was less mature. The clearest example is SGLT2 inhibitor treatment in HFmrEF and HFpEF. The document notes that the 2022 guideline recommendation was Class 2a and says an AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline update would be needed before the measure could be promoted.

The document underwent a 14-day peer-review period, then ACC, AHA, and HFSA approval processes. The work was supported by ACC and AHA without commercial support. Author and reviewer relationships with industry are disclosed in Appendices B and C.

Evaluation

What the document does well

The strongest feature is operational specificity. Each measure states who enters the denominator, what counts in the numerator, the measurement window, care setting, attribution level, data sources, exclusions, and exceptions. That structure makes the document more useful for quality-program design than a recommendation table alone.

The update also broadens quality measurement beyond drug prescribing. QM-2 requires action after social-needs screening rather than screening alone. QM-3 treats pregnancy planning as a recurring cardiovascular care process. QM-6 addresses a diagnostic-safety failure mode by linking bone scintigraphy to monoclonal protein testing.

The denominator-exception framework is clinically necessary. It allows documented intolerance, contraindication, patient preference, and access barriers to be represented rather than forcing a one-size-fits-all treatment rate.

Where the evidence is weaker

The publication does not present a formal field-validation study of the nine new measures. It does not quantify inter-rater reliability, electronic extraction accuracy, documentation burden, gaming risk, baseline performance distribution, or the effect of public reporting on outcomes. The authors explicitly state that the measure set has not been tested for all potential applications.

The evidence basis also differs across measures. The HFrEF medication measures map closely to strong treatment recommendations and randomized trial evidence summarized by the guideline. Several quality measures instead test implementation concepts, care processes, or evidence that was still evolving when the 2022 guideline was written.

Appraisal

Implementation value: high. The document gives health systems concrete numerator and denominator definitions that can be translated into EHR logic, registry rules, audit tools, and quality dashboards.

Evidence maturity: mixed by measure. PM-1 and PM-3 sit closest to mature HFrEF treatment evidence. PM-2 uses a guideline-linked blood pressure target but depends on measurement quality and the meaning of the most recent reading. The quality measures are deliberately more developmental.

External validity: the measures are written for adult heart failure care across inpatient and outpatient settings, but actual reliability will depend on local data quality, medication reconciliation, laboratory interfaces, documentation practices, and access to specialty services.

Risk of unintended behavior: several measures can degrade into documentation targets if implementation focuses on checkbox completion rather than clinical effect. QM-2 is the clearest example. A documented action after a social-needs screen does not prove that the identified barrier was resolved. Prescription-based measures also record prescribing, not medication acquisition or adherence.

SEA scoring: no protocol-specific numeric score is assigned. The exact project SEA v4 scoring rubric was not directly retrievable in this run, so assigning a numeric score would imply precision that was not available.

Source integrity findings

QM-6 classification conflict. The abstract and Appendix A classify monoclonal protein screening as quality measure QM-6. Top 10 Take-Home Message 10 calls it a performance measure. This appraisal treats Appendix A as the operational specification and preserves the conflict rather than silently correcting the source.
Transplant and LVAD exclusion conflict. Top 10 Take-Home Message 5 says patients after heart transplant or LVAD placement are excluded from all measures. Appendix A does not list those exclusions for QM-2, QM-3, or QM-6. Implementers should use the individual Appendix A specification unless the issuing organizations provide clarification.
