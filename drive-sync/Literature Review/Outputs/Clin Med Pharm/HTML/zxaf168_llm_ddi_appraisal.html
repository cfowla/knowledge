One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The evaluated LLMs usually identified intentionally embedded drug-drug interactions in simplified cases, but they overcalled clinical relevance and sometimes generated nonoptimal or potentially harmful management recommendations.

The study tests a clinically important distinction: recognizing that two drugs interact is not the same as deciding whether the interaction matters for this patient or how therapy should be modified. Across 15 simplified cases, Claude 3 identified all index DDIs in phase 1, followed by GPT-4 at 14/15 and Gemini 1.5 at 12/15. In phase 2, Gemini 1.5 and Claude 3 identified all index DDIs and GPT-4 identified 13/15. Rationale generation was reported as accurate for all identified index DDIs.

The safety signal is in management, not recognition. All models were more likely than clinical experts to classify DDIs as clinically relevant, raising an alert-fatigue analogue for LLM-mediated medication review. Recommendation quality varied: optimal recommendations ranged from 61.5% with GPT-4 to 86.7% with Claude 3. GPT-4 produced 2 potentially harmful recommendations, and Gemini 1.5 produced 1. The paper's most useful contribution is therefore negative and practical: LLMs may be useful as a DDI-recognition layer or drafting aid, but unverified management recommendations remain unsafe.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames LLMs as promising but under-evaluated tools for medication regimen assessment. The authors created 15 patient cases with medication regimens containing common DDIs and compared GPT-4, Gemini 1.5, and Claude 3 on DDI identification, clinical relevance assignment, and recommendation quality.

Key reported results: Claude 3 identified all DDIs in phase 1; GPT-4 identified 14/15; Gemini 1.5 identified 12/15. All LLMs were significantly more likely than clinical experts to categorize DDIs as clinically relevant. GPT-4 recommendations were optimal in 8/13 cases where it identified the DDI, and 2 GPT-4 recommendations plus 1 Gemini 1.5 recommendation were deemed potentially harmful.

Background / rationale

The introduction distinguishes structured clinical AI tasks, such as diagnosis or report interpretation, from comprehensive medication management. CMM requires judging appropriateness, effectiveness, safety, and feasibility for a patient-specific regimen, often with multiple reasonable paths rather than one clean ground truth.

The DDI use case is clinically plausible because DDIs contribute to adverse drug events and are targeted by EHR/CDSS alerts, but alert fatigue and clinical irrelevance limit current systems. The authors argue that LLMs might improve medication management if they can incorporate clinical context and generate individualized recommendations, but this requires evaluation beyond interaction-pair recognition.

Methods - data source and case construction

Three board-certified critical care pharmacists created 15 patient cases. Each case included one intentionally embedded index DDI documented in LexiDrug, plus distractor medications. Cases were simplified to include chief complaint, past medical history, relevant laboratory values, and vital signs.

The expert panel determined whether each index DDI was clinically relevant, defined as requiring immediate actionable intervention such as dose change or alternate therapy. The patient cases and DDI rationales were stated to be available in an eAppendix, but that supplement was not present in the uploaded PDF.

Methods - study design and prompts

The study used two separate phases. Phase 1 asked each LLM to identify any DDIs, determine whether identified DDIs were clinically relevant, and provide a rationale. Phase 2 started in a separate LLM session and asked each model to identify the most clinically relevant DDI, justify it, and provide a management recommendation: continue as-is, continue with monitoring, or modify therapy.

A single investigator entered all cases and prompts using a consistent process. The authors used standardized zero-shot prompts and the same version of each LLM for all cases. Important reproducibility details remain limited: the article does not provide repeated runs, sampling parameters, precise product build/version identifiers, or prompt-sensitivity analysis.

Methods - outcomes and analysis

The primary outcome was correct identification of the index DDI. Secondary outcomes were correct rationale, correct assignment of clinical relevance, and quality of DDI management recommendation. Recommendation quality was first categorized as potentially harmful versus acceptable, and acceptable recommendations were then evaluated for whether they optimized therapy.

Ground truth came from LexiDrug for DDI identification and rationale, and expert consensus for clinical relevance and recommendation assessment. Chi-square testing was used for primary and most secondary outcomes, descriptive statistics for rationale accuracy, R 4.3.0 for analysis, and no sample-size calculation because the work was hypothesis-generating.

Results

Phase 1 showed high DDI recognition but overclassification of clinical relevance. Claude 3 identified 15/15 DDIs, GPT-4 14/15, and Gemini 1.5 12/15. Clinical experts classified 8/15 DDIs as clinically relevant, while the LLMs classified 85.7% to 100% of identified DDIs as clinically relevant, a statistically significant difference versus ground truth.

Phase 2 showed that recognition did not guarantee safe or optimal management. GPT-4 correctly identified 13/15 index DDIs, while Gemini 1.5 and Claude 3 identified all 15. Potentially harmful recommendations occurred with GPT-4 in 2/13 cases and Gemini 1.5 in 1/15 case. Optimal recommendations were 8/13 for GPT-4, 12/15 for Gemini 1.5, and 13/15 for Claude 3.

Discussion and limitations

The discussion emphasizes the gap between recognizing DDIs and applying clinical reasoning to manage them. The authors connect LLM overcalling of clinical relevance to the known CDSS problem of excessive alerts. They also highlight two harmful GPT-4 examples: recommending piperacillin/tazobactam instead of meropenem to avoid a valproate-meropenem interaction in ESBL E. coli sepsis, and suggesting monitoring or switching apixaban to rivaroxaban despite phenobarbital induction affecting rivaroxaban as well.

Limitations are material. The case count was small; cases were simplified; no prompt engineering, RAG, domain-specific model adaptation, subscription-tier comparisons, repeated runs, explainability, citation behavior, reproducibility, or prompt-sensitivity metrics were evaluated. The study is therefore best read as a controlled warning signal and evaluation template, not as a definitive benchmark or deployment validation.

Conclusion, data availability, disclosures

The conclusion is appropriately cautious: LLMs show promise for DDI identification but have significant limitations when asked to assign clinical relevance or produce management recommendations. The authors suggest the findings may inform future development of LLMs for DDI-related clinical decision support.

The article states that underlying data are available in the article and online supplementary material. Disclosures report AHRQ funding for Dr. Sikora and Dr. Smith through R21HS028485 and R01HS029009, and no declared potential conflicts of interest.

Workflow extraction - two prompt phases

WORKFLOW 1 - DDI identification and clinical relevance
Inputs: Simplified patient case plus medication regimen.
Process: Ask LLM to identify DDIs, determine clinical relevance, and provide rationale.
Outputs: DDI list, relevance label, rationale.
Ground truth: LexiDrug for index DDI and rationale; expert panel for clinical relevance.
Failure modes: Missed index DDI; extra nonindex DDI; overcalling relevance; accurate mechanism without useful clinical prioritization.
Human review: Required to adjudicate relevance and avoid alert-fatigue style overclassification.
Governance needs: Versioned prompts, model build logging, repeated-run stability checks, and expert review of false positives/false negatives.
WORKFLOW 2 - Most clinically relevant DDI plus management recommendation
Inputs: Simplified patient case plus medication regimen in a separate LLM session.
Process: Ask LLM to identify the most clinically relevant DDI, provide rationale, and recommend continue-as-is, continue with monitoring, or therapy modification.
Outputs: Single prioritized DDI, rationale, management plan.
Ground truth: LexiDrug for identification/rationale; expert panel for acceptability, potential harm, and therapy optimization.
Failure modes: Correct interaction with incorrect substitution; unsafe therapy modification; underweighting infectious diseases or anticoagulation context; incomplete monitoring plan.
Human review: Mandatory before use in care.
Governance needs: Hard stops for high-risk drug classes, deterministic drug-reference retrieval, local antimicrobial stewardship logic, anticoagulation interaction checks, and audit trail.

Main strengths

  • Clinically relevant task selection: DDI recognition plus management is closer to pharmacist work than simple drug-pair classification.
  • Ground truth is separated by domain: LexiDrug for interaction identity/rationale and expert consensus for relevance/recommendation quality.
  • The study highlights the clinically important failure mode of correct mechanism with unsafe management.
  • Tables preserve denominator-based outcomes and potential-harm endpoints.

Main limitations

  • Only 15 cases; no sample-size calculation; limited precision and no meaningful subgroup analysis.
  • Cases were simplified and synthetic, not real-world medication profiles with messy EHR context, incomplete histories, active orders, formulary constraints, allergies, microbiology, or local stewardship rules.
  • No repeated runs, temperature control, model build/version pinning, prompt sensitivity, or output reproducibility analysis.
  • Supplement and raw model outputs were not present in the uploaded PDF, limiting independent adjudication of specific errors.
  • The evaluated models and interfaces are time-sensitive; results should not be assumed to represent current or future versions.
Safety interpretation. The harmful GPT-4 examples are not minor formatting errors. They represent inappropriate therapy selection despite correct DDI recognition, which is the central hazard for LLM use in medication management.

Implementation and practice takeaways

For clinical pharmacistsUse LLM DDI outputs as untrusted drafts. Verify interaction identity, clinical relevance, and management plan independently.
For stewardshipAntibiotic substitutions to avoid DDIs must be constrained by syndrome, severity, organism, resistance mechanism, source control, and local guidance.
For anticoagulationEnzyme induction interactions need class-level and agent-specific safeguards; switching within the same vulnerable pathway can preserve the hazard.
For tool buildersUse deterministic DDI databases and local rules as the retrieval/control layer; use LLMs only for summarization or explanation unless prospectively validated.
For evaluationScore false positives, false negatives, clinical relevance calibration, rationale correctness, recommendation acceptability, and potential harm separately.
For governanceLog model version, prompt, retrieved references, patient context used, output, reviewer action, and downstream override/adverse-event signals.

What this should change

  • When reviewing LLM-in-pharmacy papers, require recommendation-quality endpoints, not only answer accuracy or DDI recognition.
  • When designing local pilots, build a case bank with high-risk interaction classes and require pharmacist adjudication before any recommendation reaches prescribers.
  • When using LLMs informally, ask for uncertainty and citeable references, but do not treat references or fluent rationales as proof of correct management.

What this should not change

  • Do not relax existing pharmacist verification of DDIs or high-risk therapy changes.
  • Do not replace CDSS or tertiary drug references with a general-purpose chatbot.
  • Do not infer that the best-performing model in this small 2026 article is best for current use without current-version validation.
