One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. High-value comparative map of acute antimanic monotherapy efficacy and tolerability, but individual network comparisons often have low/very-low confidence and the search is historical.

The review synthesized 72 double-blind RCTs (n=16,442) spanning 23 drugs plus placebo. Thirteen agents beat placebo for response and mania symptom improvement. Four second-generation antipsychotics - aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone - combined efficacy with lower all-cause discontinuation versus placebo. Tamoxifen produced the largest efficacy estimates but was supported by two small studies, creating major imprecision and generalizability concerns.

Safety tradeoffs were prominent. Haloperidol showed strong efficacy but high akathisia/EPS signals; olanzapine had the strongest reported weight-gain RR; many antipsychotics increased somnolence, EPS/akathisia, anticholinergic use, constipation, or nausea. Lithium and valproate remained effective but ranked below many antipsychotics for acute efficacy. CINeMA confidence was generally low or very low because of within-study bias concerns, suspected publication bias, indirect evidence, sparse comparisons, and localized inconsistency.

Section condensation

Introduction

The authors framed acute bipolar mania as a setting with multiple recommended pharmacologic options but an evidence base that had evolved beyond earlier network meta-analyses. They aimed to update comparative efficacy and acceptability while expanding to remission, psychotic symptoms, and individual adverse events.

Methods

Eligible studies were published or unpublished randomized trials of oral monotherapy lasting at least 10 days in adults with acute mania. Open-label trials, high selection-bias studies, pediatric studies, dual-diagnosis populations, trials allowing antipsychotic rescue, and trials ending early without efficacy analysis were excluded. PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase were searched through March 14, 2021; the supplement documents registry and reference-list searches.

Random-effects pairwise and frequentist network meta-analysis used RRs for dichotomous outcomes and SMDs for continuous outcomes. The review evaluated heterogeneity, global/local inconsistency, transitivity, P-scores, risk of bias, meta-regression, multiple sensitivity analyses, time-course response, funnel plots, and CINeMA. Analyses targeted 3-4 week outcomes, using the nearest available timepoint from >10 days to 12 weeks when needed.

Results - evidence base

Seventy-two double-blind RCTs contributed data (n=16,442; mean age 39.55 years; 50.93% male; mean study duration 3.96 ± 2.39 weeks). Fifty-six studies were industry sponsored. Twenty-one studies were low overall ROB and the remainder moderate; the paper later summarizes 70.8% as moderate overall ROB.

Results - efficacy and acceptability

Thirteen drugs improved treatment response versus placebo (N=56; n=14,503), with significant RRs ranging from 7.461 for tamoxifen to 1.281 for asenapine. The same 13 improved mania rating scores (N=61; n=15,466), with SMDs from -1.806 for tamoxifen to -0.216 for valproate. Twelve drugs reduced discontinuation due to inefficacy (N=50; n=14,284). Ten drugs improved clinical remission and eight improved psychotic symptoms.

For all-cause discontinuation (N=70; n=16,324), aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone were superior to placebo; olanzapine RR 0.647 (95% CI 0.552-0.758) and aripiprazole RR 0.840 (0.719-0.980) bracketed the significant range. Topiramate was worse than placebo, RR 1.335 (1.032-1.728). This network had significant global inconsistency despite low global heterogeneity.

Results - tolerability and safety

Asenapine, haloperidol, and lithium increased discontinuation due to adverse events. Anticholinergic use, akathisia, EPS, somnolence, dizziness, dry mouth, constipation, nausea, and weight gain varied substantially by agent. Notable signals included haloperidol akathisia RR 5.579 and EPS RR 5.337, olanzapine weight gain RR 8.180, and cariprazine somnolence RR 5.158. Headache and diarrhea did not differ significantly from placebo across evaluated drugs.

Global heterogeneity was mostly low or low-moderate, but discontinuation due to adverse events and diarrhea were moderate-high and depression was high. Significant global inconsistency appeared for all-cause discontinuation, psychotic symptoms, adverse-event discontinuation, and depression. Overall CINeMA confidence was generally low or very low.

Discussion and conclusions

The authors emphasized aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone as the most balanced acute options because they were favorable across primary/secondary efficacy and acceptability outcomes. Tamoxifen's large efficacy estimates were explicitly tempered by the tiny evidence base. Haloperidol and carbamazepine had strong efficacy but important tolerability/safety limitations; lithium and valproate were effective but ranked lower than many antipsychotics on acute efficacy.

The authors concluded that multiple antipsychotics, carbamazepine, lithium, tamoxifen, and valproate were efficacious for acute mania, but only four agents had better acceptability than placebo. They stressed health monitoring because of adverse-event risks.

Limitations

Key limitations were moderate overall ROB in 70.8% of studies, mixed-episode terminology not aligned with DSM-5 mixed-features framing, short trial durations of 1-12 weeks, lack of explicit modeling of placebo-response magnitude, and omission of combination/nonpharmacologic strategies and cost-effectiveness. Sparse direct evidence and many comparisons with fewer than 10 studies also constrained publication-bias assessment and confidence.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance - 9/10

Directly addresses comparative acute-mania pharmacotherapy across efficacy and safety outcomes.

Evidence basis: 72 double-blind RCTs; 23 drugs plus placebo; 21 outcomes.

Principal limit: Search closed March 2021 and the artifact is not current-practice guidance.

Would raise/lower: Current external guideline alignment and newer trials would increase practice relevance.

Novelty - 7/10

Expanded prior acute-mania networks with newer agents and a broader adverse-event set.

Evidence basis: Included brexpiprazole, endoxifen, eslicarbazepine and 21 outcomes.

Principal limit: Several drug classes and core comparisons were already established.

Would raise/lower: More modern agents and patient-level effect-modifier analyses would increase novelty.

Method strength - 8/10

Registered, structured network meta-analysis with double-blind trial focus, random effects, inconsistency checks, sensitivity analyses, meta-regression, and CINeMA.

Evidence basis: PRISMA-NMA process, OSF registration, transitivity review, SIDE/design-by-treatment tests, multiple sensitivity analyses.

Principal limit: 70.8% of included studies were moderate overall risk of bias; many comparisons were sparse.

Would raise/lower: Lower-risk trials and denser head-to-head networks would raise the score.

Evidence strength - 6/10

The network is large, but confidence in individual comparisons was generally low or very low.

Evidence basis: 16,442 participants across 72 double-blind RCTs; CINeMA grading; uncertainty intervals reported.

Principal limit: Within-study bias concerns, suspected publication bias, indirect-only evidence, local heterogeneity/inconsistency.

Would raise/lower: High-confidence direct comparisons with lower bias and more trials per contrast would raise the score.

External validity - 6/10

Adult acute-mania RCTs span many drugs and settings but remain short-duration trial populations.

Evidence basis: Mean age 39.55 years; 1-12 week durations; mixed/rapid-cycling/psychosis representation varied.

Principal limit: Long-term outcomes, combination strategies, cost-effectiveness, and routine-care context were not addressed.

Would raise/lower: Longer pragmatic trials and broader real-world populations would raise the score.

Implementation value - 7/10

Useful for comparing acute monotherapy tradeoffs and anticipating adverse-event patterns.

Evidence basis: Response, discontinuation, symptom, remission, psychosis and multiple adverse-event networks.

Principal limit: Does not establish a treatment algorithm, patient-specific ranking, combination strategy, or current guideline position.

Would raise/lower: Current guideline/label reconciliation and patient-stratified decision rules would raise the score.

Central appraisal constraint: High relevance does not imply high certainty. CINeMA judged the comparison-level evidence generally low or very low, so rankings should not be treated as deterministic treatment order.
