One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Continuous IV heparin produced a more reliable early anticoagulant response than the tested intermittent SC regimen, and recurrent VTE clustered strongly among patients whose APTT remained below the trial's lower therapeutic limit.

The investigators randomized 115 consenting patients with proximal DVT to continuous intravenous or intermittent subcutaneous UFH under a double-blind design. All received a 5,000-unit IV bolus. Initial daily heparin exposure was similar by nominal dose (about 30,000 units/day), and both arms were adjusted using the same APTT target of 55-75 seconds. Despite this, a subtherapeutic response for at least 24 hours occurred in 63.2% of the SC group versus 29.3% of the IV group. Recurrent VTE occurred in 11/57 (19.3%) SC-treated patients versus 3/58 (5.2%) IV-treated patients (P=0.024).

The most influential finding is not simply a route comparison. Across both groups, recurrent VTE occurred in 13/53 (24.5%) patients with APTT below the lower therapeutic limit for at least 24 hours versus 1/62 (1.6%) patients at or above that limit (reported relative risk 15; P<0.001). The authors therefore interpreted the SC regimen as inadequately intense for initial treatment and argued that effective initial heparinization depends on promptly achieving an adequate anticoagulant response.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract / Introduction

The paper frames UFH as standard initial therapy for acute venous thrombosis but notes that the required intensity of initial heparinization was incompletely established. Subcutaneous heparin had practical appeal for early mobilization, yet there were concerns about an inadequate or unpredictable early anticoagulant response. The trial was designed to compare continuous IV heparin with an adjusted-dose SC regimen for initial proximal DVT treatment.

The abstract reports the central contrast: SC heparin produced an initial anticoagulant response below the target in most patients and was associated with 11/57 recurrent VTE events, whereas continuous IV heparin produced therapeutic responses in most patients and 3/58 recurrent events. The authors proposed that achieved anticoagulant intensity, rather than nominal heparin administration alone, explained the observed difference.

Methods

Consecutive patients at Chedoke-McMaster Hospitals with venographically demonstrated proximal DVT were considered. Exclusions included active bleeding contraindicating heparin, already receiving IV heparin at referral, and inability to complete outpatient follow-up because of distance; 22 otherwise eligible patients were unwilling or unable to consent. Before randomization, patients were grouped by prior VTE history and assigned by a computer-generated randomization arrangement. Masked pre-labeled IV and SC preparations maintained blinding. All patients received a 5,000-unit IV bolus. The IV arm then received a continuous infusion initially equivalent to 29,760 units/day; the SC arm received 15,000 units every 12 hours (30,000 units/day) plus IV placebo. Both arms were adjusted daily to a mid-interval APTT of 55-75 seconds using the same dosing schedule.

Warfarin started on heparin day 6 or 7 at 10 mg/day, heparin stopped on day 10, and warfarin continued for 12 weeks. Patients were followed for three months with daily clinical assessment during heparin therapy and scheduled objective testing at 3, 6, and 12 weeks. Recurrent DVT and PE required prespecified objective criteria; major bleeding required overt bleeding with a hemoglobin fall ≥2 g/dL, ≥2-unit transfusion, retroperitoneal bleeding, major prosthetic-joint bleeding, or intracranial bleeding. Diagnostic test results were interpreted independently without knowledge of other tests, clinical state, or treatment assignment. Fisher exact tests compared event frequencies; repeated-measures ANOVA evaluated APTT, heparin levels, and dose.

Results

Fifty-eight patients were randomized to continuous IV heparin and 57 to SC heparin; all completed primary therapy and three-month follow-up. Baseline clinical characteristics were similar. Recurrent VTE occurred in 11/57 SC patients (19.3%) versus 3/58 IV patients (5.2%; P=0.024). One SC recurrence followed inability to receive long-term warfarin because of major bleeding; excluding that patient left 10/57 (17.5%) versus 3/58 (5.2%; P=0.043). A subtherapeutic APTT response for at least 24 hours occurred in 36/57 SC patients (63.2%) versus 17/58 IV patients (29.3%; P<0.001). Ten of the 11 SC recurrences and all 3 IV recurrences occurred in patients with an initially subtherapeutic response.

Across treatment groups, Table 4 showed recurrence in 13/53 patients (24.5%) whose APTT was below the lower therapeutic limit for at least 24 hours versus 1/62 (1.6%) at or above the limit (reported relative risk 15; P<0.001). Heparin-associated bleeding occurred in 3/57 SC patients (5.3%) and 4/58 IV patients (6.9%); two patients in each group had major bleeding. Six SC patients and three IV patients died (10.5% vs 5.2%; P=0.322). At 18 hours, mean APTT was 54.8 seconds with SC heparin and 71.8 seconds with IV heparin (P<0.001), with corresponding heparin levels 0.226 and 0.309 unit/mL.

Discussion

The authors regarded the trial as firm evidence for the effectiveness of continuous IV heparin in initial proximal DVT treatment and emphasized the strong relation between early anticoagulant intensity and recurrence. They explicitly stated that, in retrospect, the SC regimen used in this trial was inadequate for initial treatment. The SC regimen had been based on prior experience in long-term therapy after a 14-day IV heparin course, which the authors recognized did not establish its adequacy as initial treatment.

They further argued that maintaining APTT above the prespecified lower limit during initial heparinization was clinically important and consistent with prior monitoring practice. The trial therefore supports a pharmacodynamic principle—rapid, adequate anticoagulation matters—but its specific dosing schedule and APTT target are assay- and era-specific.

Validity and interpretation

  • Strength: randomized double-blind comparison with identical monitoring logic and objective diagnostic criteria.
  • Strength: no loss to follow-up through primary therapy and three months.
  • Strength: diagnostic studies were interpreted independently and without treatment-group knowledge.
  • Key limitation: the trial does not isolate route as the causal factor; SC administration produced substantially weaker early anticoagulant responses despite similar nominal daily dose.
  • Key limitation: the authors themselves concluded the tested SC regimen was inadequate for initial therapy; therefore the result should not be generalized to all possible SC UFH regimens.
  • Key limitation: the therapeutic APTT range (55-75 s using Dade Actin-Fs) is reagent-specific and historical.
  • Key limitation: few recurrent events make route-specific effect estimates imprecise even though the observed difference reached statistical significance.
Do not overclaim: This paper does not establish that subcutaneous UFH is intrinsically inferior to continuous IV UFH under every dosing strategy. It establishes that the particular SC regimen tested frequently failed to produce adequate early anticoagulation and had more recurrent VTE, while recurrence strongly tracked with subtherapeutic APTT.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use nowUse the paper as foundational evidence that initial UFH effectiveness depends on promptly achieving an adequate anticoagulant response, not merely prescribing a nominal dose.
Use for protocol historySupports the historical move toward monitored, rapidly titrated IV UFH and later weight-based nomograms designed to reduce time spent subtherapeutic.
Do not copyDo not transplant the 55-75-second APTT target or the 1986 SC/IV doses into a modern institution without assay-specific calibration and contemporary evidence.

Clinical bottom line

Use now? Yes, as historical/mechanistic evidence; no, as a stand-alone current protocol. The trial supports aggressive prevention of prolonged subtherapeutic UFH exposure and provides a clear empirical bridge between laboratory response and recurrent thrombosis. Modern route selection, assay targets, and dosing should be sourced from contemporary evidence and local assay calibration.
