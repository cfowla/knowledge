One-page synthesis

The study tested an early-pandemic repurposing hypothesis: whether montelukast, a leukotriene receptor antagonist with anti-inflammatory rationale, was associated with less short-term respiratory deterioration in hospitalized COVID-19. Of 256 screened patients, 92 were analyzed. Thirty received montelukast 10 mg orally once daily starting on hospital Day 1 at clinician discretion; 62 did not receive montelukast. The primary endpoint was any increase in COVID-19 Ordinal Scale score from Day 1 to Day 3.

The crude outcome favored montelukast: 3/30 (10.0%) versus 20/62 (32.2%) deteriorated (p=0.022), with unadjusted OR 0.23 (95% CI 0.063-0.86). After adjustment only for age >=60 years, the OR was 0.28 (95% CI 0.072-1.04; p=0.058), crossing the null. Baseline groups differed materially in age, asthma prevalence, azithromycin exposure, and LDH. Treatment was nonrandom and clinician-selected, the cohort was small, and the authors explicitly reported inability to control multiple confounders. The signal is therefore compatible with benefit but is not reliable evidence of causal efficacy.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract states the mechanistic premise that leukotriene-receptor antagonism might reduce inflammatory lung injury and defines the objective as reducing clinical deterioration on the COVID-19 Ordinal Scale. It reports the retrospective 30-versus-62 comparison and the crude deterioration rates of 10% versus 32% (p=0.022).

The authors frame the findings as possible clinical activity rather than proof and explicitly call for prospective confirmation.

Background

The rationale draws on early COVID-19 hyperinflammation concepts and preclinical influenza/ARDS data suggesting leukotriene signaling can contribute to alveolar injury. Prior asthma and airway-inflammation literature is used to support biologic plausibility for montelukast.

These background data are secondary evidence cited by the paper; they are not results generated by the hospitalized COVID-19 cohort.

Materials and methods

The study was retrospective and IRB-approved. Montelukast exposure was clinician-selected, not protocol-mandated; treated patients began 10 mg orally once daily on Day 1 and were not prior outpatient montelukast users. Controls were consecutively selected from confirmed COVID-19 patients who survived to at least Day 4. In total, 256 patients were screened and 92 analyzed.

Care occurred during a rapidly changing March 2020 institutional regimen. Hydroxychloroquine 400 mg twice daily for 5 days plus azithromycin was standard through March 27, after which hydroxychloroquine was removed; all but one included patient received hydroxychloroquine. The Ordinal Scale was used to classify oxygen escalation, with Day 1-to-Day 3 chosen as the most consistently available comparison.

Statistical analysis

Baseline categorical data used Fisher exact testing; continuous variables used two-sample t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Laboratory baselines and changes were assessed with Wilcoxon methods. The primary outcome was tested with Fisher exact testing and logistic regression.

Because the number of primary events was limited, the multivariable model contained only treatment and age. Three sensitivity analyses excluded asthma, azithromycin, and steroid subgroups separately. Two-sided p<0.05 was considered significant; p<0.10 was labeled a trend.

Results — baseline characteristics

The montelukast group was older (median 67 vs 59 years, p=0.011), had much more baseline asthma (36.7% vs 6.5%, p=0.0005), and received less azithromycin (13.3% vs 40.3%, Table 2 p=0.01). Baseline LDH was also lower (344.5 vs 439, p=0.04).

Other measured baseline characteristics were reported as not significantly different, including Day 1 Ordinal Scale distribution and steroid use from Days 1-3. These imbalances still leave substantial potential for treatment-selection bias and residual confounding.

Results — laboratory values

Aside from lower baseline LDH in the montelukast group, the authors found no baseline laboratory differences and no significant between-group differences in Day 1-to-Day 3 changes for WBC, D-dimer, ferritin, LDH, CRP, creatinine, or pulse oximetry.

The Discussion notes the absence of cytokine measurements and serial viral loads, so the proposed anti-inflammatory/antiviral pathway was not directly tested in this cohort.

Results — clinical deterioration

Clinical deterioration occurred in 3/30 (10.0%) montelukast patients and 20/62 (32.2%) controls (p=0.022). The unadjusted OR was 0.23 (95% CI 0.063-0.86; p=0.029). The age-adjusted OR was 0.28 (95% CI 0.072-1.04; p=0.058), which is imprecise and includes no effect.

Among patients without asthma, rates were 11% versus 33% (p=0.077); among those not receiving azithromycin, 8% versus 32% (p=0.030). A steroid-receiving sensitivity analysis was reported as showing no difference. Median length of stay did not differ (7 vs 8 days, p=0.26).

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the lower oxygen-escalation rate as a possible montelukast signal and discuss leukotriene/NF-kB inflammatory pathways and prior asthma evidence. They also acknowledge that conventional inflammatory laboratory trajectories did not distinguish groups and that length of stay was not different.

Load-bearing limitations are explicit: small sample, retrospective design, selection bias, clinician-discretion treatment, limited observations, no cytokine/viral-load measurements, and inability to adjust for multiple confounders. The study also provides no cohort-specific adverse-event analysis. The authors recommend confirmatory trials rather than practice adoption.

Conclusions, disclosures, and data

The conclusion restates fewer deterioration events with montelukast. The data were stated to be available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The study reported no funding.

Disclosures include a Certa Dose executive role, industry funding/advisory relationships for one author, a Merck scientific-affairs role for another author, and Merck research funding/personal fees for the senior author stated to be unrelated to this work. Merck manufactures Singulair/montelukast.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Do not use for practice.
Best use: historical and hypothesis-generating evidence about an early montelukast signal in hospitalized COVID-19. Do not use for: causal efficacy claims, treatment selection, dosing escalation, or current COVID-19 standard-of-care decisions.
4/10
Relevance

Historically relevant to early COVID-19 repurposing and montelukast pharmacology, but limited current practice relevance.

Evidence basis: The paper directly evaluates a clinically meaningful deterioration endpoint in hospitalized COVID-19.

Principal limit: The therapeutic context is early 2020 and current major guideline pages do not identify montelukast as a recommended COVID-19 therapy.

Would raise confidence: A modern randomized or high-quality comparative study showing benefit in a defined current population.

5/10
Novelty

At publication, this was an early clinical observational signal for repurposed montelukast in COVID-19.

Evidence basis: It moved beyond mechanistic/preclinical rationale to a hospitalized clinical cohort.

Principal limit: Small retrospective signals are intrinsically fragile and the mechanistic rationale was not validated with cytokine or viral-load measurements.

Would raise confidence: Prospective mechanistic and clinical replication.

3/10
Method strength

The endpoint and analytic plan are understandable, but nonrandom treatment assignment and sparse-event adjustment leave substantial bias risk.

Evidence basis: Clear Day 1-to-Day 3 endpoint, Fisher testing, logistic regression, and stated sensitivity analyses.

Principal limit: Physician-discretion assignment; 23 primary events; only age included with treatment in the adjusted model; substantial baseline imbalances.

Would raise confidence: Randomization or robust propensity/target-trial methods with adequate events and prespecified confounder adjustment.

3/10
Evidence strength

The unadjusted association is sizable, but the age-adjusted confidence interval crosses 1 and residual confounding is a major threat.

Evidence basis: 3/30 vs 20/62 deteriorated; unadjusted OR 0.23 (95% CI 0.063-0.86); adjusted OR 0.28 (0.072-1.04).

Principal limit: Observational design, small sample, selection bias, physician-discretion treatment, no safety analysis, and source table inconsistencies.

Would raise confidence: Consistent replicated randomized evidence with patient-centered outcomes and safety data.

2/10
External validity

Generalizability is poor because this was one hospital during a narrow early-pandemic treatment era.

Evidence basis: The cohort reflects real hospitalized patients.

Principal limit: Single center, March-April 2020, near-universal hydroxychloroquine exposure, changing institutional treatment, and highly selected control construction.

Would raise confidence: Multicenter contemporary cohorts or trials across current variants, immunity states, and standards of care.

1/10
Implementation value

The paper should not drive current treatment decisions.

Evidence basis: Dose and timing are described and the endpoint is operationalized.

Principal limit: No confirmatory causal evidence; no study-specific adverse-event evaluation; outdated care context; current guideline pages do not mention montelukast.

Would raise confidence: Guideline-supported randomized evidence with defined patient selection, monitoring, and benefit-risk criteria.

Validity

The crude effect is large, but the design does not support causal attribution. Treatment was clinician-selected; asthma, age, azithromycin, and LDH differed at baseline; only age was included in the adjusted outcome model. With 23 deterioration events, the adjusted estimate remained wide and crossed the null. The separate sensitivity analyses do not replace comprehensive confounder control and may themselves be unstable in small subgroups.

Safety and benefit-risk

The article discusses known montelukast tolerability and neuropsychiatric warnings from external literature but does not report treatment-emergent adverse events for this cohort. Therefore this paper cannot establish a COVID-19-specific benefit-risk balance.

Confidence in appraisal

High for the conclusion that the study is hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing, because the major causal limitations are directly visible in the design, baseline imbalances, sparse adjustment, and confidence interval. Moderate for fine-grained effect interpretation because the cohort is small and some source table percentages are internally inconsistent.

Current practice context — external verification

This section is external to the primary article. The current IDSA COVID-19 guideline page (published/updated 14 Oct 2025) addresses recommended antivirals and other therapies; a text search of that page found no montelukast mention. WHO’s August 2025 Therapeutics and COVID-19: living guideline landing page likewise contains no montelukast mention. These checks support treating this 2021 observational signal as historical rather than current guideline-based therapy.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What the study supportsPreserve as an early observational signal and a useful example of why crude treatment associations can attenuate after even limited adjustment.
What it does not supportDo not infer that montelukast prevents deterioration in current hospitalized COVID-19 care, and do not convert the calculated 22.3-percentage-point crude difference into a causal NNT.
What to monitor in stronger evidenceRandomization, contemporary standard-of-care, prespecified confounder handling, patient-centered outcomes, safety, subgroup denominators, and mechanistic biomarkers if a leukotriene pathway is claimed.
Source-quality cautionRetain the Table 5 percentage inconsistencies and the Table 2/narrative azithromycin p-value difference as provenance-level source issues rather than silently correcting them.
