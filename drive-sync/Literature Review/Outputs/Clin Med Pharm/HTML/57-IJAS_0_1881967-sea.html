One-page synthesis

The study asked whether montelukast, initiated at hospital admission, was associated with less short-term respiratory deterioration in confirmed COVID-19. Investigators screened 256 patients and analyzed 92 who were hospitalized at least three days. Thirty received montelukast 10 mg orally once daily from Day 1 at physician discretion; 62 did not receive montelukast. The primary endpoint was any increase in the COVID-19 Ordinal Scale from Day 1 to Day 3.

Clinical deterioration occurred in 3/30 (10%) montelukast patients and 20/62 (32.2%) controls (p=0.022). Univariate logistic regression yielded OR 0.23 (95% CI 0.063-0.86; p=0.029). After adjustment for age ≥60 years, the association weakened to OR 0.28 (95% CI 0.072-1.04; p=0.058). The groups were imbalanced: montelukast patients were older, had more asthma, and received less azithromycin. The authors acknowledged small sample size, retrospective treatment assignment, selection bias, and inability to control multiple confounders. Their appropriate forward-looking recommendation was confirmatory study rather than immediate incorporation into practice.

Robert Wood Johnson University Hospital/Rutgers conducted the IRB-approved retrospective study during late March to early April 2020. Treatment was not randomized: clinicians prescribed montelukast at their discretion. Nearly all included patients received hydroxychloroquine, and contemporaneous institutional therapy changed during the study period. Data included demographics, comorbidities, laboratory values, medications, supplemental oxygen, physician assessments, and radiology.

Fisher exact tests compared deterioration rates. Logistic regression assessed the montelukast association. Because events were limited, the multivariable model contained only montelukast receipt and age. Separate sensitivity analyses excluded patients with asthma, azithromycin exposure, and steroid exposure. Two-sided p<0.05 was considered significant and p<0.10 a trend.

Results

Baseline balance

Montelukast patients were older (median 67 vs 59 years; Table 2 p=0.011), more often had asthma (36.7% vs 6.5%; p=0.0005), and less often received azithromycin (13.3% vs 40.3%; Table 2 p=0.01). Day 1 ordinal-scale distributions did not differ significantly (p=0.61), and steroid use from Days 1-3 was 16.7% vs 22.6% (p=0.51). Baseline LDH was lower with montelukast (344.5 vs 439; p=0.04); other baseline laboratory measures did not differ significantly.

Primary and sensitivity outcomes

The observed primary event rate favored montelukast: 10% vs 32.2% (p=0.022). The unadjusted OR was 0.23 (95% CI 0.063-0.86; p=0.029), whereas the age-adjusted OR was 0.28 (95% CI 0.072-1.04; p=0.058). Among patients without asthma, deterioration was 11% vs 33% (p=0.077). Among those not receiving azithromycin, it was 8% vs 32% (p=0.030). The steroid sensitivity analysis did not show a difference. Day 1-to-3 laboratory changes were not significantly different. Median length of stay was 7 vs 8 days (p=0.26).

Interpretive boundary. The adjusted confidence interval includes the null, and treatment assignment, control selection, concomitant therapy, and baseline imbalances leave substantial residual confounding. The study is hypothesis-generating.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Do not use for practice. Best used as a hypothesis-generating early-pandemic observational signal and as an example of how treatment-selection and baseline imbalance can affect retrospective comparative-effectiveness findings.

Relevance5/10

Directly concerns pharmacotherapy in hospitalized COVID-19, but the clinical question is historically bounded.

Evidence: A 92-patient hospitalized COVID-19 cohort with medication exposure and clinical outcome data.

Limit: Early-pandemic treatment context and narrow present-day applicability.

Would raise: A clinically current question or evidence linked to current practice would raise relevance.

Novelty6/10

At publication, it provided an early clinical signal for repurposed montelukast.

Evidence: Observed deterioration rates and regression analyses in an early hospitalized cohort.

Limit: Retrospective signal generation rather than a controlled efficacy test.

Would raise: Prospective randomized confirmation would raise novelty as actionable evidence.

Method strength3/10

The design is vulnerable to treatment-selection bias and residual confounding.

Evidence: Single-center retrospective cohort; physician-discretion treatment; limited events; age-only adjusted model.

Limit: Small sample and nonrandom exposure with important baseline imbalances.

Would raise: Randomization, larger sample, prespecified multivariable adjustment, and robust missing-data handling would raise the score.

Evidence strength3/10

The unadjusted association was significant, but age adjustment crossed the null and event counts were small.

Evidence: 3/30 vs 20/62 deterioration; univariate OR 0.23; age-adjusted OR 0.28 (95% CI 0.072-1.04).

Limit: No causal identification and limited ability to adjust multiple confounders.

Would raise: A sufficiently powered randomized trial with patient-centered outcomes would raise the score.

External validity2/10

The cohort reflects one center during a highly specific early-pandemic treatment era.

Evidence: Late March/early April 2020; almost all patients received hydroxychloroquine; control selection required survival to Day 4.

Limit: Single-center setting, selected survivors, and obsolete concurrent-care context.

Would raise: Multicenter prospective enrollment under contemporary care would raise the score.

Implementation value1/10

The source does not support clinical implementation of montelukast for COVID-19.

Evidence: Authors themselves call for confirmatory trials rather than incorporation into practice.

Limit: Observational design and uncertain causal effect.

Would raise: Consistent randomized evidence plus current guideline/label support would be required.

Main strengths

  • Clinically interpretable oxygen-support endpoint with an explicit Day 1-to-Day 3 definition.
  • Reports raw event counts, unadjusted and age-adjusted regression results, and several sensitivity analyses.
  • Authors explicitly acknowledge major confounding and selection limitations and call for confirmation.

Main limitations

  • Small single-center retrospective cohort with only 23 primary events.
  • Montelukast assignment depended on physician discretion; control eligibility required survival to at least Day 4.
  • Important baseline differences in age, asthma, azithromycin use, and LDH.
  • Age-only multivariable adjustment despite multiple plausible confounders.
  • Historical concurrent care included near-universal hydroxychloroquine use.
  • No mechanistic cytokine or serial viral-load measurements, and length of stay was not standardized.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use this source for
Historical hypothesis generation, observational-method critique, and understanding the reported early montelukast signal.
Do not use this source for
Establishing causal efficacy, selecting COVID-19 therapy, or supporting montelukast dosing beyond the studied 10 mg daily exposure.
Most important quantitative finding
3/30 vs 20/62 deterioration; unadjusted OR 0.23, but age-adjusted OR 0.28 with 95% CI 0.072-1.04.
Critical design lesson
Large observed differences in a small nonrandom cohort can weaken after minimal adjustment and remain highly sensitive to unmeasured confounding.
