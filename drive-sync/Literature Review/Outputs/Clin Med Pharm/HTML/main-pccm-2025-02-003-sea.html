One-page synthesis

Core thesis: The authors argue that PE prevention and treatment remain overly tied to convenient historical dosing patterns and insufficiently attentive to anticoagulant pharmacokinetics. Their central proposals are more individualized LMWH prophylaxis, greater use of weight-based and more frequent or continuous dosing in high-risk/ICU settings, and initially monitored intravenous anticoagulation for acute PE when perfusion is abnormal.

The review’s most substantive contribution is its pharmacokinetic challenge to fixed-dose, once-daily subcutaneous LMWH. It assembles older and newer anti-Xa observations showing weight dependence, troughs or low levels late in the dosing interval, and substantial variability in critically ill patients with and without vasopressors. It uses these data to argue that anti-Xa exposure should be treated as a patient-level dosing problem rather than judged from group averages. The paper also revisits historical UFH data emphasizing rapid attainment of therapeutic anticoagulation and contrasts those principles with contemporary subcutaneous treatment strategies.

The paper then broadens into system-level and future-facing claims: skepticism about mandatory risk scores and PERTs, criticism of advanced-therapy trials whose control anticoagulation may be inadequate, and interest in intravenous LMWH, suction thrombectomy, alpha-2-antiplasmin inhibition, and factor XI/XIa inhibition. These sections are useful for generating questions, but the article often moves from pharmacokinetic or mechanistic observations to clinical recommendations without systematic evidence synthesis. That matters because the paper’s most forceful acute-PE treatment and PERT positions are not aligned with the subsequently published 2026 multidisciplinary AHA/ACC acute PE guideline.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract argues that current PE prophylaxis and treatment underuse pharmacokinetic knowledge. It proposes abandoning once-daily fixed-dose LMWH prophylaxis in favor of weight- or weight-category dosing, at least twice-daily dosing, or continuous infusion in critically ill patients. For acute PE, it claims reduced perfusion makes subcutaneous anticoagulant absorption unpredictable and recommends intravenous anticoagulation until hemodynamics and cutaneous perfusion normalize.

It also frames several research directions: intravenous LMWH with anti-Xa targeting, renewed use of monitored UFH, suction thrombectomy and catheter-directed lysis, factor XI/XIa inhibition, and alpha-2-antiplasmin inhibition. The abstract presents these positions assertively, but it does not signal a systematic evidence-selection method or grade the certainty behind the recommendations.

Introduction

The authors use continuous-infusion beta-lactam therapy as an analogy for pharmacokinetic reasoning, arguing that achieving a pharmacologic target should matter more than preserving historically convenient dosing schedules. They extend this principle to anticoagulation and argue that trials should measure target-specific effects rather than rely heavily on mortality outcomes influenced by many non-treatment factors.

The stated purpose is to identify what is going well, what is going poorly, and what future PE care may look like. The framing is intentionally argumentative: historical protocols are characterized as sometimes marketing-influenced, and the authors position individualized pharmacology as a corrective.

What’s going right with pulmonary embolism

The review credits increasing VTE awareness, prophylaxis quality-improvement efforts, broader specialty engagement in PE recognition, more standardized treatment pathways, longer-term attention to incomplete recovery and CTEPH, and an expanding pipeline of therapies. It cites a Chinese quality-improvement example in which prophylaxis use increased and inpatient VTE was numerically lower with a computerized decision-support intervention.

The authors also see value in protocolization when it prevents dosing errors, while warning that rigid protocols can suppress individualized expertise. They note newer CTEPH hemodynamic thresholds and describe multiple emerging treatment approaches, including intravenous LMWH, factor XI/XIa inhibitors, novel fibrinolytic mechanisms, and catheter-based clot-removal strategies.

What’s going wrong with pulmonary embolism

This is the review’s central section. It criticizes diagnostic and prophylaxis algorithms when they become mandatory rather than supportive, questions the value of large PERT structures in hospitals that already have PE expertise, and argues that modern practice has drifted from the older principle of rapidly achieving and monitoring therapeutic anticoagulation. The authors repeatedly emphasize that group-average anticoagulant levels are inadequate for patient-specific treatment decisions.

The evidence assembled is predominantly pharmacokinetic. The review reports a prophylactic peak anti-Xa target of roughly 0.20–0.40 U/mL around four hours after LMWH dosing, describes weight-related variability after fixed dosing, notes anti-Xa decline well before 24 hours with once-daily regimens, and highlights marked variability in ICU patients. It cites trauma practice using weight-category enoxaparin every 12 hours with anti-Xa checks. From this, the authors infer that fixed once-daily prophylaxis is often inadequate and that acute PE should generally begin with monitored intravenous anticoagulation until hemodynamic stability is convincingly restored.

What’s new with pulmonary embolism

The authors describe research on intravenous LMWH prophylaxis and treatment, particularly in ICU patients, and emphasize the possible advantage of lower HIT risk relative to UFH. They discuss suction thrombectomy as a short catheter-based aspiration procedure that avoids fibrinolytic exposure, contrast it with catheter-directed alteplase infusions, and criticize advanced-therapy trials if control-arm anticoagulation is not measured or optimized.

Other investigational strategies include alpha-2-antiplasmin inhibition to promote fibrinolysis with a hypothesized lower distant-bleeding risk and factor XI/XIa inhibition to reduce thrombosis while limiting bleeding. For post-PE complications, the paper notes the relative rarity of CTEPH, specialized surgical and catheter therapies, and uncertainty about whether highly detailed follow-up or exercise prescriptions improve outcomes.

Conclusions

The authors conclude that PE prevention and treatment have meaningful successes but also important shortcomings. They call for clinicians to incorporate pharmacokinetic evidence into anticoagulant dosing and for investigators to design trials with stronger explanatory value and practice-changing integrity.

Validity

The article is not a systematic review. No database search, eligibility criteria, evidence hierarchy, risk-of-bias assessment, certainty grading, or quantitative synthesis is reported. Citation selection is therefore vulnerable to confirmation bias, especially because the prose is explicitly advocacy-oriented. The strongest evidence presented often consists of anti-Xa pharmacokinetic distributions, historical heparin studies, or selected clinical examples rather than a comprehensive comparison of patient-centered outcomes.

A second validity issue is inferential distance. Demonstrating variable anti-Xa exposure after a dosing regimen supports a pharmacokinetic concern; it does not by itself establish that a different regimen reduces symptomatic VTE, mortality, or major bleeding across all patient groups. The paper sometimes treats this bridge as largely self-evident. The critique of advanced-therapy trials likewise raises a legitimate control-arm question, but the review does not provide a formal trial-level bias analysis.

Pharmacotherapy strengths

The review is unusually attentive to dose, route, frequency, weight dependence, renal function, timing of anti-Xa measurements, and the consequences of impaired perfusion. It usefully distinguishes prophylaxis from treatment and highlights that subcutaneous absorption can become an important variable in critical illness. It also directs readers to specific primary studies on UFH target attainment, LMWH anti-Xa profiles, trauma prophylaxis, ICU pharmacokinetics, and catheter therapies.

Pharmacotherapy limitations

The review uses anti-Xa target ranges and pharmacokinetic exposure as proxies for clinical effectiveness more strongly than the cited narrative evidence can securely support. It also makes broad treatment recommendations that are not graded by clinical severity, bleeding risk, renal function, planned procedure, or contemporary guideline category. Statements such as halving LMWH in marked renal insufficiency are presented generically rather than as drug-specific, renal-function-specific dosing guidance.

Practice translation: Treat the article as a structured challenge to dosing assumptions, not as a replacement for current PE guidelines, product labeling, or local anticoagulation protocols.

Current-practice verification — external to the source

Important temporal conflict. The review was published in March 2025. A new multidisciplinary AHA/ACC acute PE guideline was published in 2026, after this review, and materially changes the current-practice context for two of the review’s strongest positions.
  • Initial parenteral anticoagulation: the 2026 AHA/ACC/ACCP/ACEP/CHEST/SCAI/SHM/SIR/SVM/SVN guideline recommends LMWH over UFH for acute PE patients who require initial parenteral anticoagulation. This does not support using UFH as the default standard merely because it is easier to monitor.
  • PERT: the same 2026 guideline recommends PE response teams to improve timeliness of care. That conflicts with the review’s generally skeptical framing of PERTs in centers with existing PE expertise, although implementation value may still be institution-dependent.
  • Advanced therapy: the 2026 guideline considers systemic thrombolysis, catheter-based thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, and surgical embolectomy reasonable or considerable in selected higher-risk PE categories rather than treating them simply as unproven innovations.
