One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. This is a large, methodologically thoughtful observational study that reinforces inpatient oseltamivir use in children, especially the rationale for not withholding treatment solely because more than 48 hours have elapsed. It is confirmatory rather than practice-changing.

Core thesis. In FluSurv-NET pediatric influenza hospitalizations, oseltamivir modeled as a time-dependent exposure was associated with a lower hazard of ICU admission and a modestly faster hospital discharge. The primary ICU model estimated an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.69 (95% CI, 0.60-0.80). The LOS model estimated an adjusted discharge hazard ratio of 1.13 (95% CI, 1.06-1.21), corresponding to a model-estimated 9.4-hour shorter LOS (95% bootstrap CI, 4.3-14.6 hours).

Why it matters. The analysis directly addresses several recurrent problems in antiviral effectiveness studies: treatment started before admission, symptom-onset timing, and immortal time bias. However, treatment was not randomized. The treated group was clearly different at baseline—more comorbidity, more ICU admission, and more mechanical ventilation—so the adjusted benefit depends heavily on adequate control of confounding. The study strengthens confidence in the direction of benefit but should not be read as a causal effect estimate equivalent to an RCT.

Section condensation

Abstract and Key Points

The authors frame declining antiviral use in hospitalized children as a clinical problem and identify weaknesses in prior pediatric oseltamivir effectiveness studies, including influenza misclassification, uncertain symptom-onset timing, and incomplete capture of preadmission antiviral use. They ask whether oseltamivir is associated with lower ICU admission and shorter hospital LOS in children hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza.

After exclusions, 6,044 cases contributed to the ICU analysis and 7,103 to the LOS analysis. The abstract reports the same principal adjusted estimates as the main results: ICU aHR 0.69 (0.60-0.80) and discharge aHR 1.13 (1.06-1.21). The abstract contains an internal demographic-count inconsistency; Table 1 is more coherent and is used for baseline counts in this appraisal.

Introduction

The introduction summarizes prior outpatient and observational inpatient evidence, the source-stated recommendations from CDC, IDSA, and AAP to treat hospitalized children with suspected or confirmed influenza, and recent declines in antiviral use. It also describes adverse-effect concerns and variation in clinician prescribing as potential contributors to underuse.

The stated hypothesis is that oseltamivir is associated with both reduced ICU admission and shorter inpatient LOS. The article positions FluSurv-NET as an opportunity to improve on prior database studies by using laboratory confirmation, symptom-onset dates, and information on antiviral receipt before hospitalization.

Methods

FluSurv-NET provided population-based surveillance data from 13 states across 8 influenza seasons. Eligible patients were younger than 18 years, hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza, and had a respiratory symptom-onset date. Major exclusions removed missing timing data, transfers, observation encounters, LOS ≤1 day or >30 days, implausible testing/treatment timing, and antivirals other than oseltamivir. Same-day oseltamivir initiation and ICU admission was excluded from the primary ICU analysis because day-level dates could not establish sequence.

Oseltamivir was modeled as a time-dependent exposure anchored at respiratory symptom onset. Multivariable Cox models estimated ICU-admission and discharge hazards and were intended to mitigate immortal time bias. Covariates were selected a priori plus a liberal bivariate screen; sensitivity analyses changed same-day classification, included short stays, and excluded prehospital treatment. Early treatment was ≤2 days from symptom onset; late treatment was ≥3 days. LOS estimates came from model-based survival curves with bootstrap CIs.

Results

From 13,127 reported pediatric hospitalizations, 7,103 remained in the LOS cohort (5,746 treated) and 6,044 in the ICU cohort (4,240 treated before ICU). In Table 1, the LOS cohort had median age 3 years (IQR 1-7), 56% male, 50% with medical comorbidity, and 27% with asthma. Oseltamivir recipients differed materially from nonrecipients: they were treated/admitted earlier in illness, had more comorbidity and asthma, and had higher raw ICU admission (31% vs 15%) and mechanical ventilation (6% vs 3%).

Adjusted oseltamivir exposure was associated with reduced ICU-admission hazard (aHR 0.69; 0.60-0.80) and increased discharge hazard (aHR 1.13; 1.06-1.21). The model-estimated LOS difference was 9.4 hours shorter with treatment. ICU associations persisted across reported sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of prehospital treatment. LOS benefit was clearest with early treatment; late treatment versus no treatment was not statistically significant for LOS (aHR 1.08; 0.98-1.20; P=.13). No tested treatment interaction by age, comorbidity, or ICU status was statistically significant in the main-text summaries.

Discussion and Limitations

The authors interpret the findings as evidence of benefit across hospitalized pediatric patients, including otherwise healthy children and patients treated beyond 2 days after respiratory symptom onset. They emphasize laboratory confirmation, standardized abstraction, symptom-onset anchoring, capture of prehospital oseltamivir, and time-to-event methods as improvements over earlier retrospective database studies.

They also acknowledge retrospective multi-site data, clinician-dependent influenza testing, incomplete prehospital treatment capture, day-level rather than timestamp-level timing, lack of complex survey adjustment for the 2022-2023 sampling season, FluSurv-NET coverage of roughly 9% of the US population, and residual confounding. These limitations are material because treatment assignment and illness severity were strongly nonrandom.

Conclusions

The authors conclude that oseltamivir was associated with lower likelihood of ICU admission and shorter LOS and state that their findings support treating hospitalized children as soon as possible regardless of symptom duration or underlying risk factors.

This source supports that practice direction but does not independently establish causality, show that late treatment is superior to early treatment, or quantify an individual patient’s absolute ICU-risk reduction. The timing analyses should be interpreted as observational associations, not a strategy trial.

Clinical-practice appraisal

PICO

P: Children <18 years hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza in FluSurv-NET.
I/Exposure: Oseltamivir, time-varying from symptom onset.
C: No oseltamivir.
O: ICU admission; hospital discharge/LOS.

Validity

Strong observational design choices address immortal time and symptom timing. The major unresolved threat is residual confounding because oseltamivir receipt was strongly associated with baseline severity and timing of care.

Practice translation

Best used to reinforce prompt inpatient oseltamivir and to counter a rigid “48-hour cutoff” for hospitalized children. The study does not supply dosing, monitoring, adverse-effect, resistance, or drug-interaction guidance.

Bottom line

Use now? As supportive evidence, yes. As a stand-alone causal basis for changing practice, no. It reinforces rather than replaces guideline-based treatment decisions.

Important interpretive boundaries

  • No NNT/NNH: an NNT from the raw 31% vs 15% ICU proportions would be misleading because treatment was time-varying and the groups were confounded. Safety was not evaluated as a primary outcome, so NNH is not available.
  • Mortality: death was too infrequent for meaningful effectiveness analysis and was handled as censoring in the time-to-event models; this paper should not be used to quantify mortality benefit.
  • Timing: the evidence supports not withholding treatment solely because symptoms began more than 48 hours earlier, but it does not show that delayed initiation is preferable to early initiation.
  • Guideline currency: this appraisal summarizes the article’s statements about national recommendations. No external guideline verification was performed in this run.

Principal limiting factor: FluSurv-NET covers about 9% of the US population, testing was clinician/facility dependent, and short stays were excluded from the main cohort.

Main strengths

  • Population-based, multistate surveillance over 8 influenza seasons.
  • Laboratory-confirmed influenza rather than diagnosis-code-only case identification.
  • Respiratory symptom onset used as the analytic anchor.
  • Oseltamivir treated as a time-varying exposure, directly addressing immortal time bias.
  • Prehospital treatment captured and tested in sensitivity analysis.
  • Multiple sensitivity and interaction analyses with consistent direction for the main outcomes.

Main limitations

  • Retrospective observational treatment assignment with substantial baseline differences.
  • Whole-day timing prevents precise sequencing of same-day treatment and ICU admission/discharge.
  • Potential incomplete capture of prehospital oseltamivir and clinician-dependent influenza testing.
  • FluSurv-NET catchment represents about 9% of the US population.
  • Complex survey design for 2022-2023 abstraction was not incorporated.
  • Supplement 1 was unavailable in the retrieved PDF, preventing independent inspection of eTables/eFigures that contain additional sensitivity/subgroup details.
  • The abstract contains an internal sex-count inconsistency.
  • Funder was involved in multiple stages of study conduct and publication; this is disclosed and should be considered in appraisal, not treated as evidence of bias by itself.
