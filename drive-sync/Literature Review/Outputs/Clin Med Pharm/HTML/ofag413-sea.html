One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In selected adults with susceptible Gram-positive bacteremia, often after about 5 inpatient IV days, cefadroxil step-down produced similar observed 90-day treatment success to IV-only and other oral regimens, with no severe adverse events observed in the cefadroxil cohort.

The final cohort included 323 episodes: 58 cefadroxil, 72 IV-only, and 193 alternative oral therapy. Treatment success was 91%, 94%, and 93%, respectively; pairwise comparisons versus cefadroxil were not statistically significant. Ninety-day mortality, bacteremia recurrence, and readmission were also similar. Cefadroxil recipients had shorter hospital stay than IV-only recipients (median 6 vs 8.5 days, P<.001) and shorter total antibiotic duration (16 vs 28.5 days, P=.004). More than 80% of cefadroxil recipients received 1 g twice daily or a renally adjusted equivalent.

The evidence is supportive but not definitive. Cefadroxil patients differed meaningfully from IV patients: skin/soft-tissue sources were more common with cefadroxil, while MSSA was more common in the IV group; hospital assignment and social characteristics also differed. Only 58 patients received cefadroxil, the analysis was retrospective, and the primary multivariable model adjusted only for treatment arm and pathogen. Both oral cohorts had a median 5-day IV lead-in, leaving very early oral transition unanswered.

Best use: supports cefadroxil as a pragmatic oral step-down candidate in carefully selected lower-risk susceptible MSSA/streptococcal bacteremia, including some nonvertebral osteomyelitis cases, when source control and follow-up are adequate.
Do not use this study to justify: cefadroxil for endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, prosthetic/CNS infection, hemodialysis populations, or immediate/very early oral switch; those questions were not tested.

Section condensation

Abstract and background

The authors position cefadroxil as a narrow-spectrum first-generation oral cephalosporin with >90% bioavailability and an approximately 1.5-2 hour half-life, enabling twice-daily dosing. They note increasing support for oral transition in bloodstream and bone/joint infections but limited cefadroxil-specific clinical data for invasive Gram-positive infection.

The study question was whether clinical outcomes with cefadroxil oral transition were comparable to IV-only or alternative oral therapy in susceptible Gram-positive bacteremia, including nonvertebral osteomyelitis. The rationale combines spectrum, tolerability, oral bioavailability, dosing convenience, and avoidance of long-term IV access.

Methods

Investigators retrospectively identified adults across four LAC DHS hospitals with MSSA, S. lugdunensis, or susceptible streptococcal bacteremia. Records were manually reviewed. Comparator patients received IV-only or alternative oral therapy active against the infecting organism. Exclusions included contaminants, multiple overlapping discharge agents, ineligible discharge therapy, completion before discharge, hemodialysis, inadequate follow-up, trial enrollment, certain polymicrobial cases, transfer, and other prespecified conditions. After review, high-risk sources not represented among cefadroxil recipients — including endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, prosthetic and CNS infections — were also removed from comparator cohorts.

Primary outcome was treatment success: 90-day survival without infection progression or relapse. Prioritized secondary outcomes were 90-day mortality, recurrent bacteremia, and readmission; LOS and selected grade 3/4 adverse events were also collected. Group comparisons used chi-square/Fisher tests and nonparametric continuous-variable tests. A Firth penalized logistic regression assessed treatment arm and S. aureus versus other pathogens as predictors of treatment failure.

Results — population and treatment

From 1,604 queried bacteremia episodes, 323 were ultimately analyzed: 58 cefadroxil, 72 IV-only, and 193 alternative oral. Cefadroxil patients had more SSTI sources than IV or alternative oral patients (52% vs 28% and 31%), while IV patients more often had MSSA (60% vs 36% cefadroxil). Housing instability and polysubstance use were also more common in cefadroxil than IV patients, and hospital distribution differed substantially. Case-mix index, expected LOS, and expected mortality were similar.

Median inpatient IV exposure was 5 days for cefadroxil, 8 days for IV-only, and 5 days for alternative oral. More than 80% of cefadroxil recipients received 1 g twice daily or a renally adjusted equivalent. Median total antibiotic duration was 16 days with cefadroxil, 28.5 days with IV-only therapy, and 15 days with alternative oral therapy.

Results — clinical outcomes

Treatment success was 53/58 (91%) with cefadroxil, 68/72 (94%) with IV-only therapy (P=.51), and 180/193 (93%) with alternative oral therapy (P=.63). Ninety-day mortality was 2%, 1%, and 0.5%; 90-day bacteremia recurrence was 3%, 0%, and 3%; and 90-day readmission was 41%, 32%, and 33%. No grade 3/4 adverse events were observed in cefadroxil patients versus 3% IV and 2% alternative oral.

Median LOS was 6 days with cefadroxil, 8.5 days with IV-only therapy (P<.001), and 6 days with alternative oral therapy (P=.06). In the Firth model, treatment arm was not an independent predictor of failure; adjusted ORs were 0.52 (95% CI 0.14-1.93) for IV-only and 0.79 (0.28-2.26) for alternative oral versus cefadroxil. S. aureus was associated with higher failure odds than non-S. aureus infection (adjusted OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.01-5.88). Main-text descriptions of SAB and bone/joint subgroups reported no observed outcome differences, but their numerical detail was in unavailable supplementary tables.

Discussion, limitations, and future directions

The authors interpret cefadroxil as a favorable oral option because observed efficacy was similar while avoiding IV access and because beta-lactams generally have favorable tolerability and interaction profiles. They emphasize that the cohort included osteomyelitis but excluded selected higher-risk syndromes. They also identify source control as an important contributor to clinical failures.

The central weaknesses are retrospective design, unmeasured confounding, selection bias, baseline and hospital-level practice imbalances, small cefadroxil sample size, and limited generalizability. The higher SSTI prevalence in cefadroxil recipients and greater SAB prevalence in IV recipients may bias comparisons in favor of cefadroxil. Both oral cohorts received a median 5-day IV lead-in, so earlier transition remains untested. The authors call for randomized evaluation and additional dosing data, especially for invasive infection, obesity, and altered pharmacokinetics.

Conclusion and disclosures

The authors conclude that cefadroxil transitional therapy was associated with similar treatment success, mortality, and recurrence compared with IV and alternative oral therapy for selected MSSA and streptococcal bacteremia, and that it was well tolerated with no severe adverse events observed. They frame cefadroxil as a pragmatic option for lower-risk infection.

No study funding was required; no potential conflicts were reported. Data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. High relevance and immediate stewardship/pharmacotherapy utility, but the evidence should be treated as supportive observational data rather than definitive comparative efficacy evidence.
Relevance — 9/10Directly addresses oral beta-lactam transition for bacteremia and nonvertebral osteomyelitis. Basis: clinically actionable regimen/outcomes. Limit: selected population. Higher score: broader syndromes and prospective practice data.
Novelty — 7/10Adds cefadroxil-specific invasive-infection data where the paper describes limited prior clinical evidence. Basis: three-cohort comparative study. Limit: oral-switch concept itself is not novel. Higher score: randomized cefadroxil-specific evidence.
Method strength — 5/10Multicenter design and explicit definitions are strengths. Basis: manual review, prespecified exclusions, Firth model. Limit: retrospective selection, strong group/site imbalances, limited adjustment. Higher score: prospective randomization or robust propensity/causal adjustment.
Evidence strength — 5/10Observed outcomes are reassuring but event counts are small and no equivalence/noninferiority framework was used. Basis: 58 cefadroxil patients; 91% success. Limit: wide adjusted intervals and confounding. Higher score: adequately powered randomized or well-adjusted prospective confirmation.
External validity — 4/10Four hospitals improve site breadth, but all are within one county safety-net system and high-risk syndromes were excluded. Limit: generalization to endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, prosthetic/CNS infection, hemodialysis, and earlier switch is unsupported. Higher score: diverse systems and broader infection syndromes.
Implementation value — 7/10Provides a concrete twice-daily cefadroxil strategy, comparator regimens, IV lead-in exposure, and outcome definitions. Limit: optimal dosing in obesity/altered PK and earliest safe switch are unresolved. Higher score: dosing/PK targets and prospective implementation protocol.

Clinical validity

PICO: Adults with selected susceptible Gram-positive bacteremia, with or without nonvertebral osteomyelitis; cefadroxil oral transition; versus IV-only or alternative oral therapy; primary outcome 90-day survival without progression/relapse. The source supports association, not causal equivalence.

Main threats: confounding by indication, site-specific prescribing, imbalance in SSTI and MSSA prevalence, small cefadroxil arm, limited covariate adjustment, and post hoc removal of comparator patients with syndromes absent from the cefadroxil group. This source-matching decision is understandable but creates a selected population and can make the treatment groups appear more comparable than the real-world decision set.

Practice translation

The paper supports considering cefadroxil after initial stabilization in lower-risk susceptible MSSA/streptococcal bacteremia when there is no excluded high-risk source and when source control is adequate. The most commonly used study regimen was 1 g twice daily or a renally adjusted equivalent. The study does not establish a minimum IV lead-in, because oral groups received a median of 5 inpatient IV days.

Use now? Maybe, as supportive regimen-selection evidence in appropriately selected patients and within local stewardship/ID practice. It is not sufficient alone to override higher-quality evidence, guideline restrictions, or patient-specific concerns.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Candidate regimenCefadroxil 1 g twice daily was used in >80% of cefadroxil patients, with renal adjustment when needed.
Selection boundaryDo not extrapolate to endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, prosthetic/CNS infection, or other excluded high-risk settings.
Source controlSeveral cefadroxil failures were attributed to inadequate source control; source-control adequacy remains central.
Interpret LOS carefullyShorter LOS versus IV-only therapy is operationally plausible but is vulnerable to treatment-selection and discharge-planning confounding.
Earlier switch unknownMedian inpatient IV lead-in was 5 days in oral cohorts; the paper does not answer immediate or very early switch.
Research priorityProspective randomized comparison with stronger dosing/PK characterization, particularly in obesity and altered pharmacokinetics.
