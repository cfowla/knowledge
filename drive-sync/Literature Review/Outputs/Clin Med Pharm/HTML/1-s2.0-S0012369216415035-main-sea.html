One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The article organizes the then-available evidence for prevention and treatment of venous thromboembolism around heparin for initial anticoagulation, coumarins (chiefly warfarin) for longer-term therapy, and selected mechanical or pharmacologic prophylaxis strategies. It explicitly grades recommendations and emphasizes that adequate anticoagulant intensity, treatment duration, and bleeding surveillance determine benefit-risk balance.

The most reusable historical content is the transition from empiric anticoagulation toward protocolized management: a bolus-plus-infusion heparin regimen, APTT-guided titration, early warfarin initiation with several days of overlap, and a long-term INR-equivalent target of roughly 2.0-3.0. The authors also distinguish proximal DVT/PE from isolated calf-vein thrombosis and treat persistent risk factors as a reason for longer therapy. These ideas anticipate later guideline architecture, even though the specific agents, diagnostics, evidence-grading system, and many dosing details are dated.

Evidence quality is heterogeneous. The review cites controlled trials for several core claims, but many recommendations rest on lower-level evidence, observational reports, animal data, or expert consensus; one indefinite-anticoagulation recommendation explicitly states that it is not based on published data. The article has no modern systematic-review methods, GRADE certainty framework, formal evidence tables, or contemporary direct oral anticoagulant evidence.

Section condensation

Scope, evidence grading, and risk

The authors frame antithrombotic therapy as prevention of thrombus progression rather than active thrombus dissolution. They distinguish treatment-dose heparin/coumarins from prophylactic strategies and state that efficacy judgments rely chiefly on controlled trials; a positive controlled trial or a sufficiently large negative trial is treated as level I evidence, with weaker studies assigned lower levels.

Risk is organized around stasis/immobility, trauma and surgery, age, estrogen exposure, prior VTE, and malignancy. Orthopedic surgery is emphasized as especially high risk: the text cites about 40% DVT incidence after total hip or knee replacement, and Table 2 gives 40-70% venous thrombosis rates for hip fracture, total hip replacement, and total knee replacement.

Heparin

Heparin is described as an antithrombin-III-dependent inhibitor of thrombin, IXa, and Xa, administered parenterally and prescribed in units. The review treats pulmonary embolism as an established indication and extrapolates treatment logic to proximal DVT. Historical bleeding data are variable: a 5-20% hemorrhagic-complication range is cited, and randomized comparisons generally favored continuous infusion over intermittent injection for bleeding, although dose differences confounded administration-method comparisons.

The review argues that a minimum anticoagulant effect is required and uses APTT prolongation as the practical surrogate. It recommends intensive early monitoring, later daily APTT after stabilization, platelet counts every 3-4 days, and a 7-10 day initial heparin course. Toxicities discussed include thrombocytopenia, rare disseminated thrombosis, and osteopenia with long-term high-dose therapy.

Coumarin derivatives / warfarin

Coumarins are described mechanistically through inhibition of vitamin-K-dependent factors II, VII, IX, and X. The authors explicitly connect the delayed fall of clotting factors and early protein C suppression to the need for overlapping heparin and warfarin. PT monitoring is presented as reagent-sensitive, and the article highlights the difficulty of comparing thromboplastin systems across laboratories.

The review moves toward a lower-intensity VKA target than older practice, equating rabbit-brain PT prolongation of about 1.2-1.5 times baseline with INR roughly 2.0-3.0. Table 4 catalogs factors that increase or decrease warfarin effect. Hemorrhage, skin necrosis, pregnancy-related fetal harms, and multiple drug/diet interactions are emphasized.

Duration, cost-effectiveness, and prevention

Duration is individualized: at least three months is recommended for many patients, while malignancy, antithrombin III deficiency, protein C deficiency, or recurrent VTE are used as reasons for indefinite therapy. A cited controlled trial is interpreted as showing that two weeks of anticoagulation is inadequate. Table 5 favors less-intense warfarin over conventional-intensity warfarin, adjusted-dose heparin, and low-dose heparin in a historical cost-effectiveness model.

For prophylaxis, low-dose subcutaneous heparin is favored in many moderate-risk patients, while pneumatic compression is emphasized for selected surgical groups where bleeding is undesirable. Dextran, aspirin, hydroxychloroquine, heparin-dihydroergotamine, ancrod, and electrical stimulation are discussed as alternatives or historical approaches with important limitations.

Summary and recommendations

The explicit recommendations cover prophylaxis for moderate-risk surgical/medical patients and high-risk orthopedic/neurosurgical/urologic groups, plus treatment of proximal DVT and PE. The core treatment recommendation is therapeutic IV or adjusted-dose subcutaneous heparin, followed by overlapping oral anticoagulation and a roughly three-month minimum course.

The article labels several prophylaxis and treatment recommendations grade A, but heparin duration/overlap and indefinite therapy for continuing risk factors are grade C; the indefinite-treatment recommendation explicitly acknowledges no published-data basis. Calf-vein thrombosis is treated as controversial, with observation conditioned on serial impedance plethysmography when available.

External current-practice verification external information

Why this matters: The source is from 1986. Current-practice comparison is required to prevent a faithful historical summary from being mistaken for contemporary treatment guidance.

CHEST 2021 second update. The contemporary CHEST VTE guideline uses GRADE-based recommendations and includes 29 guidance statements. For isolated distal DVT, it recommends risk- and symptom-stratified management: serial deep-vein imaging for two weeks in lower-risk patients or anticoagulation when severe symptoms/risk factors for extension are present; anticoagulation is started if extension occurs. This modernizes the 1986 article's impedance-plethysmography-based calf-DVT approach.

ASH 2020 treatment guideline (reviewed by ASH in 2022 and kept active at that review). ASH conditionally prefers direct oral anticoagulants over vitamin K antagonists for primary VTE treatment in most patients, recommends INR 2.0-3.0 when a VKA is used for secondary prevention, and frames primary treatment as generally 3-6 months with extended/indefinite therapy selected according to recurrence pattern and persistent risk. This confirms that the 1986 source's heparin-warfarin-centered regimen is historically informative but no longer a complete treatment framework.

Current-reference identifiers: CHEST 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.chest.2021.07.055; ASH 2020 VTE Treatment Guidelines, Blood Advances 4(19):4693-4738. The evaluated 1986 source remains the basis for all source-derived findings above; these current references are used only for currency appraisal.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply — historical foundation; do not use for current practice.
Best use: tracing the evolution of UFH/warfarin VTE protocols, evidence grading, early APTT/INR targets, and prophylaxis logic. Do not use as a contemporary dosing, agent-selection, reversal, prophylaxis, or distal-DVT management guideline.

Rationale: Core VTE principles remain recognizable, but the practice environment, diagnostics, agents, and monitoring technology are substantially dated.

Evidence basis: Proximal DVT/PE anticoagulation, VKA INR 2-3, and longer therapy for persistent risk remain conceptually durable.

Principal limit: No DOAC era, historical thromboplastin reagents, IPG surveillance, dextran/ancrod emphasis, and old surgical prophylaxis evidence.

Rationale: Useful for reconstructing historical protocol logic, but unsafe as a current implementation specification.

Evidence basis: Table 3 supplies a clear stepwise heparin-warfarin protocol and monitoring cadence.

Principal limit: Specific bolus/infusion doses, laboratory targets, reversal, prophylaxis choices, and agent selection are not current standards.

Would change with: Mapping each historical rule to a current CHEST/ASH recommendation and local policy would make it implementation-useful.

Main strengths

  • Clear separation of treatment-dose anticoagulation from prophylaxis.
  • Explicit evidence-level framing and graded recommendations.
  • Detailed heparin monitoring, administration, bleeding, and transition-to-warfarin discussion.
  • All five tables are clinically interpretable and tightly linked to the narrative.
  • The paper openly identifies uncertainty and some recommendations with weak/no published evidence.

Main limitations

  • Secondary review/consensus source rather than primary evidence.
  • No modern systematic-search, risk-of-bias, certainty, or meta-analytic methods.
  • Multiple recommendations depend on low-level evidence, historical laboratory assays, or expert inference.
  • Current core therapies and diagnostics (DOACs, modern ultrasound-based distal-DVT algorithms, contemporary reversal strategies) are absent.
  • Cost data and prophylaxis options are era-specific and not transportable to current practice.

Confidence in appraisal: High for characterization of this source because the full PDF and all main-text tables were inspected; moderate for current-practice comparison because the external verification is intentionally narrow and guideline-focused rather than a full modern VTE guideline review.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Preserve

  • Adequate anticoagulant intensity matters.
  • Transition to VKA requires overlap with parenteral anticoagulation.
  • Bleeding surveillance and platelet monitoring are integral to UFH use.
  • Duration should reflect recurrence risk and persistence of provoking factors.

Do not port forward

  • Fixed 1986 UFH bolus/infusion instructions.
  • Historical APTT seconds-above-baseline targets without assay calibration.
  • Dextran/ancrod-era prophylaxis algorithms.
  • Impedance plethysmography calf-DVT surveillance.
  • 1986 reversal and cost-effectiveness assumptions.

Best CDS/research use

  • Historical provenance layer for heparin protocol evolution.
  • Crosswalk old monitoring targets to current anti-Xa/APTT institutional practice.
  • Track which recommendations persisted, changed, or disappeared across CHEST editions.
  • Use cited primary trials as targets for separate primary-source ATOM extraction.
