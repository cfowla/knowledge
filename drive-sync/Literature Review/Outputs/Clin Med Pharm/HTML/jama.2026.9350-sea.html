One-page synthesis

Core thesis
In the SOLARIS phase 3 trial, raising vitamin D exposure substantially above a standard 400-IU/day comparator did not improve progression-free survival when added to first-line mFOLFOX6 or FOLFIRI plus bevacizumab in unselected patients with previously untreated metastatic colorectal cancer.
Primary efficacy
Median PFS 11.8 vs 10.3 months; HR 0.92 (95% CI, 0.73–1.16); 1-sided P=.25. Adjusted HR 0.92 (95% CI, 0.72–1.17).
Secondary efficacy
ORR 50.9% vs 43.6% (P=.12). Median OS 25.6 vs 27.0 months; HR 1.05 (95% CI, 0.81–1.36); 1-sided P=.66.
Target engagement
High-dose vitamin D3 rapidly and durably increased plasma 25(OH)D: 38.3 vs 23.5 ng/mL at first restaging and 45.6 vs 27.3 ng/mL at treatment discontinuation.
Safety
No clinically meaningful overall toxicity difference was identified. Grade 3/4/5 event rates were 54.2%/16.0%/2.4% vs 52.2%/14.8%/2.9%.
Important nuance
Prespecified tumor-location and ECOG interactions suggested possible heterogeneity, particularly for left-sided tumors, but the article itself describes these subgroup findings as exploratory and requiring further investigation. They should not override the null primary randomized comparison.

PICO & trial design

Population
Adults ≥18 years with histologically confirmed unresectable locally advanced/metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma, measurable RECIST 1.1 disease, ECOG 0–1, and adequate organ function. dMMR/MSI-high disease and prior systemic therapy for advanced/metastatic disease were excluded.
Intervention
High-dose vitamin D3: 8000 IU/day during cycle 1, then 4000 IU/day, plus investigator-selected mFOLFOX6 or FOLFIRI + bevacizumab.
Comparator
Standard-dose vitamin D3: 400 IU/day, with matching placebo in cycle 1 to preserve blinding, plus the same chemotherapy/bevacizumab options.
Outcomes
Primary: PFS by RECIST 1.1 progression or death. Secondary: ORR, OS, baseline vitamin D deficiency, serial 25(OH)D, and safety.

Methods

Design, randomization, and masking

SOLARIS was a multicenter double-blind phase 3 trial. Participants were randomized 1:1 using the Pocock-Simon dynamic allocation method, with balancing by chemotherapy backbone (mFOLFOX6 vs FOLFIRI), primary tumor location (right vs left), and ECOG performance status (0 vs 1). The research pharmacist was unblinded; participants and the remaining study team were blinded.

The common oncologic backbone was mFOLFOX6 or FOLFIRI plus bevacizumab 5 mg/kg IV on day 1 every 14 days. Treatment continued until progression, unacceptable toxicity, or a discontinuation decision. Efficacy analyses used intention-to-treat; safety analyses included patients receiving at least one protocol dose.

Vitamin D intervention and biomarker ascertainment

The high-dose regimen used an 8000-IU/day loading phase during cycle 1 followed by 4000 IU/day; the standard-dose arm received 400 IU/day. Plasma 25(OH)D was sampled at baseline, after cycles 4 and 8, and at discontinuation, measured by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry in a single batch. Ten percent blinded duplicates yielded a reported coefficient of variation of 5.96% (95% CI, 5.17%–6.75%).

Statistical plan

The original sample-size plan was 400 participants; the target was increased to 445 after higher-than-expected nondifferential dropout. The protocol targeted 273 PFS events for 90% power to detect HR 0.70, assuming median PFS 14.3 vs 10 months, using a 1-sided log-rank α=.05. One futility analysis was planned at approximately 50% of PFS events; the trial continued because interim P=.186 did not cross the prespecified futility boundary of .568.

The final primary analysis used an unstratified 1-sided log-rank test and Cox model. Supporting multivariable Cox analyses adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, number of metastatic sites, ECOG status, RAS status, and BRAF status. Interaction testing used α=.10 for subgroup heterogeneity.

Results

Participants and treatment exposure

455 participants were randomized: 228 to high-dose and 227 to standard-dose vitamin D3. At least one protocol dose was received by 212 (93.0%) and 209 (92.1%), respectively. Median follow-up was 20 months (IQR, 7–35). Baseline characteristics were broadly balanced; median age was 59 years, 39.8% were female, 52.3% had ECOG 0, and most received mFOLFOX6.

Treatment exposure was similar. Median chemotherapy cycles were 12 in both groups, and mean vitamin D adherence was 96% in each arm. This reduces concern that a null clinical effect resulted simply from poor exposure to assigned supplementation.

Primary and secondary efficacy

There were 286 PFS events. Median PFS was 11.8 months (95% CI, 10.3–13.3) vs 10.3 months (95% CI, 9.4–12.2), HR 0.92 (95% CI, 0.73–1.16), 1-sided log-rank P=.25. The adjusted HR was 0.92 (95% CI, 0.72–1.17; P=.48). The trial therefore did not meet its primary efficacy objective.

ORR was 50.9% vs 43.6% (P=.12). There were 232 deaths; median OS was 25.6 vs 27.0 months, HR 1.05 (95% CI, 0.81–1.36), 1-sided P=.66; adjusted HR 1.03 (95% CI, 0.78–1.35; P=.84). Neither secondary efficacy outcome showed a statistically significant advantage.

Biologic effect and safety

High-dose supplementation clearly changed vitamin D status. At first restaging, median 25(OH)D was 38.3 vs 23.5 ng/mL; at second restaging, 38.2 vs 23.2 ng/mL; at discontinuation, 45.6 vs 27.3 ng/mL (all Supplement 2 P<.0001). Thus, failure of the primary outcome occurred despite strong pharmacodynamic/biomarker separation.

Overall grade 3–5 toxicity was similar between groups. Any-grade vitamin D-associated events were uncommon: hypercalcemia 3.3% vs 1.4%, hyperphosphatemia 3.3% vs 4.8%, and renal calculi 1.4% vs 0.

Discussion & limitations

Authors’ interpretation

The investigators contrast the negative phase 3 result with the earlier SUNSHINE phase 2 signal. They note a higher baseline 25(OH)D level in SOLARIS and a measurable rise even in the standard-dose arm, potentially reducing exposure contrast. They also emphasize that the high-dose regimen did what it was biologically intended to do—correct vitamin D insufficiency rapidly and durably—while remaining tolerable and adherent.

The authors highlight tumor-location and ECOG interactions as hypothesis-generating. They explicitly state that the subgroup observations remain exploratory and require further investigation.

Principal limitations
  • Generalizability is restricted by the allowed oncologic backbones: mFOLFOX6 or FOLFIRI with bevacizumab; other commonly used regimens were not tested.
  • Outside vitamin D supplementation could not be completely excluded, although serial biomarker separation argues against major contamination.
  • The primary test used a 1-sided α=.05 design. That was prespecified and powered for a directional benefit, but it is a design feature to keep in view when comparing to conventional 2-sided frameworks.
  • Subgroup interaction findings arise within an overall null trial and involve multiple prespecified subgroup comparisons; they are not independent confirmation of benefit.
  • Data-sharing terms cannot be appraised because Supplement 3 was referenced but not supplied.
Strengths
Randomized double-blind design; phase 3 sample size; 151 academic/community US sites; intention-to-treat efficacy analysis; high adherence; comparable chemotherapy exposure; direct serial biomarker confirmation of target engagement; detailed protocol/statistical supplement; and transparent reporting of a negative primary outcome.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a definitive phase 3 test of a clinically simple adjunct that had encouraging earlier randomized evidence. Its main value is the reliable negative primary result and the contrast between strong biomarker target engagement and absent clinical benefit.
8/10
Relevance

Rationale: directly informs adjunctive treatment decisions in metastatic CRC. Evidence basis: phase 3 randomized efficacy and safety outcomes. Limit: applies to a specific first-line backbone/population. Would change: broader regimen coverage or guideline integration would raise practical relevance.

7/10
Novelty

Rationale: advances a positive phase 2 hypothesis to a definitive phase 3 answer. Evidence basis: SOLARIS directly tests high-dose vs standard-dose D3. Limit: concept/mechanistic rationale predates this trial. Would change: validated predictive subgroup biology would raise novelty.

9/10
Method strength

Rationale: multicenter double-blind randomized phase 3 design with prespecified endpoints and ITT analysis. Evidence basis: protocol, randomization, masking, 286 PFS events, biomarker QA. Limit: one-sided primary test, dropout-driven sample amendment, and regimen restriction. Would change: independent replication/broader backbones would strengthen further.

9/10
Evidence strength

Rationale: the null primary conclusion is directly supported by randomized event data and consistent secondary survival analysis. Evidence basis: PFS HR 0.92 (0.73–1.16), ORR P=.12, OS HR 1.05 (0.81–1.36). Limit: subgroup signals are much weaker evidence than the global result. Would change: a replicated subgroup interaction would alter interpretation for selected patients.

7/10
External validity

Rationale: 151 US academic/community sites and diverse participants support real-world relevance. Evidence basis: broad multicenter recruitment. Limit: ECOG 0–1, dMMR/MSI-high exclusion, and mFOLFOX6/FOLFIRI + bevacizumab restriction. Would change: testing contemporary alternative backbones/biologics and less-selected patients would raise this score.

8/10
Implementation value

Rationale: supports avoiding routine high-dose D3 addition to this regimen for unselected patients. Evidence basis: negative primary endpoint despite high adherence and biomarker separation. Limit: this SEA did not externally verify current guideline positioning. Would change: guideline/label comparison or cost/workflow data would raise implementation completeness.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the global efficacy conclusion; moderate for practice extrapolation outside the studied backbones; low-to-moderate for any treatment implication from subgroup interactions.

Practice translation

Use now
Use the trial as evidence against adding high-dose vitamin D3 solely to improve PFS in unselected, previously untreated mCRC patients receiving mFOLFOX6/FOLFIRI + bevacizumab.
Do not overclaim
Do not conclude vitamin D status is clinically irrelevant, that high-dose D3 is harmful, or that left-sided tumors have a validated treatment indication for high-dose D3.
What the biomarker shows
The high-dose regimen reliably raises 25(OH)D during chemotherapy. The negative clinical outcome is therefore not well explained by failure to increase the biomarker.
What to watch next
Replication or prospective enrichment based on tumor sidedness/ECOG or mechanistic markers would be needed before subgroup signals should influence therapy selection.
Clinical boundary: This artifact appraises the publication; it does not replace patient-specific oncology care, contemporary guideline review, or medication/supplement assessment for non-oncologic vitamin D indications.
