Executive summary

The document updates the 2021 universal definition of heart failure and tries to make the syndrome easier to describe consistently across clinical care, research, registries, and policy. It is a terminology and classification document. It explicitly says it is not a clinical practice guideline and not a clinical decision support document.

The most consequential change is the decision to stop treating fixed LVEF cutoffs as the defining structure of heart failure phenotypes. The panel retains LVEF as clinically important, but groups patients as heart failure with reduced, preserved, or improved ejection fraction and asks clinicians to interpret LVEF in context. The document also gives heart failure causes a common taxonomy, formalizes improvement, remission, and recovery as distinct trajectories, separates worsening heart failure from decompensated heart failure, and places social and geographic variation inside the disease framework rather than outside it.

Practice boundary. Statements about SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, finerenone, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, beta blockers, diuretics, devices, transplant, or other therapies summarize evidence or describe management context. They are not recommendations issued by this document. Treatment decisions still need the applicable heart failure guideline, drug labeling, local policy, and patient-specific assessment.

Definition and staging

The syndrome definition on page 3 requires a structural or functional cardiac abnormality that explains typical symptoms, signs, and laboratory or imaging evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion or altered cardiac output. The document lists exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, jugular venous distention, rales, an S3 gallop, and abdominal or leg swelling as typical clinical findings.

BNP or NT-BNP elevation and imaging evidence of congestion or elevated filling pressures increase diagnostic confidence. They are confirmatory evidence, not standalone gates. The source gives HFpEF with normal natriuretic peptide levels and elevated invasive filling pressures as the main example of why a single-test rule fails.

A diagnosis of symptomatic HF is generally retained permanently once established, even if the patient later improves. That statement matters when the trajectory terms are used. "Recovery" describes a favorable state after HF, not proof that the historical diagnosis never existed.

Risk reduction and pre-HF

The update keeps prevention close to the definition. It emphasizes hypertension and ischemic heart disease, then gives added attention to obesity, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes because these conditions are common and now have therapies associated with lower incident HF risk. It also discusses natriuretic peptide screening, troponin, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and artificial intelligence-enabled ECG as ways to identify higher-risk people before symptomatic HF develops.

Source limitation attached to AI screening. The panel says AI-enabled ECG needs more evidence for patient outcomes, performance across population groups, and real-world feasibility, particularly in resource-limited settings. The paper does not establish an AI-ECG screening standard.

LVEF phenotype classification

LVEF remains useful for trial interpretation, therapy selection, device decisions, and longitudinal follow-up, but the panel argues that universal numeric cutoffs overstate the precision of the measurement. It cites variability across imaging methods and possible differences by sex, age, and ethnicity. The paper reports approximate lower limits of normal of 53% for women and 52% for men and says an LVEF below 50% is unlikely to be normal.

Appraisal of an apparent tension. The paper moves away from rigid LVEF cutoffs for the overall phenotype framework, but later describes the conventional HF with improved EF criterion of at least a 10-point increase to an LVEF above 40%. These statements can coexist. The first concerns the universal classification scheme. The second describes a commonly used trajectory definition that the authors themselves say is incomplete.

Universal classification by cause

The panel rejects a simple ischemic versus nonischemic split. It argues that a named cause can change testing, prognosis, and disease-specific treatment. Cardiac amyloidosis is used as the clearest example of why a cause label matters beyond standard HF therapy.

The table is intentionally nonexhaustive. The paper also says categories can overlap and can change when deeper phenotyping or genetic testing identifies a better explanation. This means a clinical data model should permit multiple causes and later revision rather than forcing one immutable label.

Geography, social determinants, and generalizability

The global section is more than background. It argues that the cause mix for HF changes by region, income level, migration, and industrialization. The document summarizes prior evidence showing much higher contributions from ischemic heart disease in many high-income regions than in sub-Saharan Africa, larger roles for rheumatic disease in parts of Asia and Africa, and region-specific patterns such as Chagas disease in Latin America and peripartum or hypertensive disease in Africa.

A cited 195-country analysis is summarized with global 2017 age-standardized prevalence contributions of 26.5% from ischemic heart disease, 26.2% from hypertensive heart disease, and 23.4% from COPD. The paper then warns that high-quality prevalence, cause, and outcome data remain sparse in many low- and middle-income countries. It also points to near absence of sub-Saharan African participants in HF trials.

Evidence gap. A "universal" definition is being applied to a literature base that is not universally representative. The authors state this problem directly. Generalization of trial-derived thresholds, phenotypes, and management assumptions should preserve geography and access-to-care context.

Figure 1 on page 7 depicts HF as a longitudinal course rather than a one-way ladder. Stage A can progress to stage B and then symptomatic stage C. The authors explicitly reject "stable HF" as a reassuring endpoint because residual risk of worsening, hospitalization, and sudden cardiac death remains. They also state that patients with improved EF remain susceptible to recurrent left ventricular dysfunction and should continue GDMT and longitudinal surveillance.

ConceptDefinitionWhat is not required
Worsening HFProgressive deterioration in HF symptoms, signs, and quality of life in a person who already has HF.A clinic visit, emergency visit, or hospitalization is not required. New-onset HF is excluded.
Decompensated HFA HF state defined by the need for treatment escalation or rescue therapy.It does not have to be an abrupt event and is not limited to patients already classified as having worsening HF.

Worsening HF may include more dyspnea or edema, malignant arrhythmias, reduced exercise capacity, higher pulmonary pressures, impaired ventricular function, or rising BNP, NT-BNP, or troponin. Decompensation is tied to a management response such as increased or combined diuretic therapy, GDMT implementation, device or valvular intervention, mechanical circulatory support, transplant, or palliative treatment. The source acknowledges that the threshold for escalation depends on local practice and resource availability.

Mode of presentation and HF mimics

Acute HF can follow myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndrome, hypertensive emergency, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, or acute noncardiac disease such as kidney, liver, or respiratory failure. Some acute syndromes resolve after the precipitating cause is treated and do not become chronic HF.

Chronic HF may develop gradually over weeks or months with fatigue, exertional dyspnea, or fluid-related weight gain. The source notes that these symptoms can be dismissed as deconditioning or aging.

Evaluation of the evidence architecture

The source is strongest as a consensus taxonomy. It collects recent terminology, identifies where old categories fail, and gives clinicians and researchers a common vocabulary. The three main tables and Figure 1 do real work. They are not decorative additions.

The supplied document does not present a systematic-review protocol, formal evidence-to-decision framework, recommendation grading scheme, or detailed voting method. It cites 71 sources that include guidelines, expert consensus documents, systematic reviews, observational studies, randomized trials, and preprints. That is adequate for a consensus definition, but it is not the same evidence process as a graded clinical practice guideline.

The decision to remove fixed LVEF cutoffs is conceptually coherent but creates an implementation problem. Registries, trials, billing systems, and software often need exact machine-readable boundaries. The paper does not supply a replacement algorithm for that problem. A system that stores only "reduced" or "preserved" would discard information the document says remains important. The safer representation is the raw LVEF value, measurement method, date, trajectory, and the contextual phenotype label.

Remission and recovery are useful distinctions, but the source does not give one universal duration, biomarker threshold set, imaging schedule, or adjudication rule that would turn them into fully automated endpoints. Those terms are better treated as clinician-adjudicated states unless a separate protocol supplies operational criteria.

Conflict-of-interest context

The main article devotes pages 14 through 16 to writing-group and reviewer disclosures. It distinguishes modest and significant relationships using a financial threshold and ownership criteria. The separate 26-page ESC national reviewer report adds 2024 and 2025 declarations for national society reviewers, including speaker or consulting payments, institutional payments, travel support, research funding, leadership roles, and entries stating that nothing was declared.

Many disclosed relationships involve companies active in heart failure, cardiometabolic disease, devices, amyloidosis, or related cardiovascular fields. The disclosures are detailed and visible. They do not establish that any definition was improperly influenced. They do mean that conflict transparency should remain attached to the document when it is reused as a terminology authority.

Implementation notes for structured clinical systems

The following points are appraisal-derived implementation guidance, not statements issued by the consensus panel.

  • Store raw LVEF, modality, date, and measurement context. Do not replace the measured value with a single phenotype label.
  • Model stage A, B, C, and D separately from LVEF phenotype. The source treats them as different dimensions.
  • Allow multiple concurrent HF causes and later revision. Table 3 is explicitly nonexhaustive and overlapping.
  • Keep trajectory as a longitudinal field. Improvement, remission, and recovery should not erase a prior stage C diagnosis.
  • Represent worsening HF and decompensated HF separately. One describes deterioration. The other depends on escalation or rescue treatment.
  • Keep acute versus chronic presentation separate from long-term stage.
  • Add a mimic or competing-diagnosis layer for coronary disease, CKD, pregnancy, obesity, and deconditioning when appropriate.
  • Preserve social, geographic, and access-to-care context when interpreting cause prevalence or trial evidence.
  • Do not hardcode a new universal HFrEF LVEF threshold from this document. It intentionally does not provide one.
