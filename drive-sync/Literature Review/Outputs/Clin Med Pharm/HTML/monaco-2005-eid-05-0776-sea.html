One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best used as a historically important case in the emergence and molecular epidemiology of PVL-positive community-acquired MRSA in Europe. Do not use it as standalone treatment guidance.

The letter describes a previously healthy 37-year-old woman hospitalized in Rome in April 2005 with fever, cough, headache, multilobar pulmonary infiltrates, hypoxemia, leukocytosis, and subsequently demonstrated cavitary necrotizing pneumonia. Initial clarithromycin plus ceftriaxone was followed by no clinical improvement. Admission sputum grew MRSA. Therapy was changed to linezolid on hospital day 3, after which fever resolved and the patient improved; she was discharged after 14 days of linezolid treatment with leukocyte count 6,040 cells/µL and PaO2 88 mm Hg.

The isolate was susceptible to the tested non-beta-lactam agents and harbored SCCmec IV, lukS/lukF (PVL), and hlg. MLST assigned ST30 and spa typing assigned type 755. The authors interpreted the spa/MLST pattern as a descendant or local variant of the southwest Pacific clone and argued that fusidic-acid resistance could not be relied on as a screening marker for PVL-producing CA-MRSA in Italy. They characterized the case as the first Italian report of PVL-positive CA-MRSA necrotizing pneumonia and the first SWP-clone isolate reported in southern Europe.

Published correction: the supplied print PDF states linezolid 600 mg three times daily. An official Emerging Infectious Diseases erratum in November 2005 corrected this to 600 mg twice daily. The corrected dose is used only as an external verification note; the ATOM set deliberately omits the dose to avoid propagating the known print error.

Section condensation

Background and rationale

The authors frame the case against rising MRSA prevalence in Italy and uncertainty about whether typical PVL-positive CA-MRSA clones were present locally. Those epidemiologic statements are citations to prior literature rather than data generated by this case report.

Case presentation and treatment

The patient was a 37-year-old woman with no notable medical history or recent travel. Admission findings included 39°C temperature, heart rate 108/min, respiratory rate 32/min, PaO2 73 mm Hg on room air, leukocytes 24,360 cells/µL, and multilobar infiltrates. Clarithromycin plus ceftriaxone did not produce clinical improvement; sputum obtained at admission grew MRSA and CT demonstrated multiple cavitary lesions consistent with necrotizing pneumonia.

Linezolid was started on day 3. Fever resolved and the clinical condition improved rapidly. Discharge followed 14 days of linezolid, with leukocytes 6,040 cells/µL and PaO2 88 mm Hg. Because the report is uncontrolled, this temporal sequence does not isolate linezolid as the cause of recovery.

Microbiology and molecular characterization

The MRSA isolate was susceptible to erythromycin, clindamycin, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, kanamycin, and fusidic acid. Molecular testing identified SCCmec IV, PVL genes lukS/lukF, and hlg. MLST assigned ST30; spa typing assigned a novel type 755.

The spa pattern contained seven repeats (XJ4AKAOM) and differed minimally from the classical SWP pattern. The authors interpreted the isolate as a descendant or local variant of the SWP clone.

Interpretation and limitations

The authors argued that fusidic-acid resistance, associated with the more resistant European ST80 clone, could not be relied on as a screening marker for PVL-positive CA-MRSA in Italy because this ST30 isolate was fusidic-acid susceptible. They also emphasized the apparent geographic novelty of the case.

The report cannot determine whether the strain was circulating in Italy or imported, how the patient acquired it, or whether the observed recovery was attributable specifically to linezolid. There is no surveillance denominator, comparator group, or treatment-effect estimate.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance
7/10

Directly relevant to severe MRSA pneumonia and antimicrobial therapy, but mainly of historical value.

Evidence basis: Single severe CA-MRSA pneumonia case with microbiologic and molecular characterization.

Principal limiting factor: A 2005 case report cannot represent current treatment standards.

What would raise/lower: Higher if paired with contemporary comparative evidence and guideline alignment.

Novelty
8/10

At publication, the authors reported the first Italian PVL-positive CA-MRSA necrotizing pneumonia and first SWP-clone isolate in southern Europe.

Evidence basis: Explicit first-report claims plus ST30/spa 755 characterization.

Principal limiting factor: Novelty is historical and geography-specific.

What would raise/lower: Lower if contemporaneous southern European cases predated publication; higher with independent surveillance confirmation.

Method strength
3/10

Microbiologic and molecular typing are concrete, but the clinical evidence is a single uncontrolled case.

Evidence basis: Culture, susceptibility testing, SCCmec typing, PVL gene detection, MLST, and spa typing were reported.

Principal limiting factor: No comparator, protocolized outcomes, or causal design.

What would raise/lower: Higher with prospective surveillance, replicate isolates, standardized susceptibility methods, and comparative cohorts.

Evidence strength
3/10

The case directly documents one isolate and one clinical course but cannot establish treatment efficacy or population-level epidemiology.

Evidence basis: Patient course, imaging, culture, molecular typing, and discharge measures are directly reported.

Principal limiting factor: n=1; no counterfactual; treatment response is temporally associated rather than causal.

What would raise/lower: Higher with replicated cases, controlled treatment data, and denominators for epidemiologic claims.

External validity
2/10

Generalizability from one patient and one isolate is very limited.

Evidence basis: Previously healthy adult with a PVL-positive ST30 isolate in Rome.

Principal limiting factor: Single center, single patient, no surveillance denominator.

What would raise/lower: Higher with multicenter population-based surveillance and diverse patient isolates.

Implementation value
4/10

Useful for historical recognition of PVL-positive CA-MRSA and molecular epidemiology, but not sufficient to direct present-day therapy.

Evidence basis: Links necrotizing pneumonia phenotype with PVL-positive ST30/SWP lineage and reports susceptibility data.

Principal limiting factor: Old evidence, single case, and a published dosing erratum in the print version.

What would raise/lower: Higher with current resistance surveillance, contemporary treatment comparisons, and guideline-concordant recommendations.

Clinical-practice lens

PICO: single previously healthy adult with severe PVL-positive CA-MRSA necrotizing pneumonia; no randomized comparator; treatment sequence included initial clarithromycin/ceftriaxone followed by linezolid; outcomes were clinical recovery, laboratory improvement, and discharge.

Validity: microbiologic identification and molecular typing provide reasonably direct isolate-level evidence, but clinical treatment inference is extremely weak because the report is uncontrolled. No standardized outcome assessment, susceptibility methodology detail, or causal comparison is provided in the letter.

Practice translation: the source supports historical recognition of this phenotype and lineage, not current regimen selection. Current clinical action should rely on contemporary guidelines, susceptibility data, and higher-level evidence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use

Historical molecular epidemiology of PVL-positive CA-MRSA; recognition that severe necrotizing pneumonia may occur in previously healthy adults; provenance for ST30/SWP-clone discussion.

Do not use

Do not use the report to estimate linezolid efficacy, choose current dosing, infer population prevalence, or define a modern screening rule.

Read next
