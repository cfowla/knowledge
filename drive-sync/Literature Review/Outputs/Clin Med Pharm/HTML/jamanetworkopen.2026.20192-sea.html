Interpretive boundary: this is an observational prognostic association study. The results quantify risk gradients associated with preexisting CKD severity; they do not establish that CKD itself causally produces the observed ICU outcomes.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: CKD was common in this ICU population (25%), and progressively worse baseline kidney function was associated with higher mortality, much greater likelihood of needing acute KRT and remaining dialysis-dependent, and slower ICU/hospital discharge. The dose-response pattern was strongest through non-dialysis stage 5 CKD. Maintenance-dialysis patients were an important exception: their mortality risk was lower than non-dialysis stage 5 and roughly comparable to stage 4.

The study's main strength is scale combined with a more clinically meaningful CKD exposure definition than diagnosis-code-only studies. Baseline eGFR was calculated from an outpatient creatinine 7–365 days before admission, and the analysis adjusted for demographics, comorbidity burden, prior health-care use, sepsis, vasopressors, ventilation, and a severity score with the renal component removed. Sensitivity analyses changed the kidney-function definition, added patients missing baseline creatinine through imputation, used time-to-event modeling for 90-day death, and evaluated albuminuria; the overall risk gradient persisted.

The strongest clinical signal concerns kidney recovery after critical illness. Among day-90 survivors who had started KRT in the ICU, ongoing KRT dependence rose from 7.2% without CKD to 50.3% with stage 4 CKD and 83.8% with previously non-dialysis stage 5 CKD. These are directly useful prognostic numbers, but they should be framed as population-level estimates rather than patient-specific predictions.

Methods

Population and exposure

Adults residing in Ontario and admitted to an ICU were identified through linked ICES data. Only the first ICU admission was used. Prior kidney transplant, absent baseline eGFR, and lack of OHIP eligibility were among the exclusions. Baseline kidney function used the most recent outpatient creatinine 7–365 days before the index ICU admission and the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. CKD categories were ≥60, 45–59, 30–44, 15–29, and <15 mL/min/1.73 m² without dialysis, plus maintenance dialysis.

Outcomes and modeling

Mortality was measured during ICU stay, during hospitalization, and through 90 days. Secondary endpoints included KRT initiation during ICU stay, KRT dependence at 90 days, and ICU/hospital length of stay. Logistic regression was used for mortality and ICU KRT, and Fine–Gray models treated death as a competing event for discharge. The authors removed the renal component from MODS in adjustment to reduce direct mathematical overlap between CKD exposure and illness-severity adjustment.

Adjustment breadth: the multivariable models incorporated age, sex, rural residence, neighborhood income, major comorbidities, recent health-care use, vasopressors, ventilation, MODS without its renal component, sepsis, and severe sepsis/septic shock.

Primary results

Cohort

Of 848,202 ICU admissions identified, 531,090 adults met eligibility criteria. Mean age was 67 years (SD 15), and 57% were men. Baseline eGFR was ≥60 in 75%, 45–59 in 12%, 30–44 in 7%, 15–29 in 3%, and <15 without dialysis in 1%; 2% were receiving maintenance dialysis.

The Cox analysis preserved the monotonic risk gradient through non-dialysis stage 5 and again showed a lower relative estimate in maintenance dialysis.

Missing baseline creatinine

The primary analysis excluded 293,861 people without an outpatient baseline creatinine; they were younger on average (59 years) and had fewer comorbidities. Assigning those patients an imputed eGFR of 75 mL/min/1.73 m² expanded the cohort to 824,951, and the principal mortality/KRT relationships remained directionally similar. For 90-day mortality, adjusted ORs in the imputed analysis ranged from 1.05 for eGFR 45–59 to 2.17 for non-dialysis eGFR <15; maintenance dialysis was 1.95.

Discussion and limitations

The authors emphasize the overrepresentation of CKD in ICU patients and interpret the findings as useful for prognostication. They also note an unusual pattern in which maintenance-dialysis patients fare better than non-dialysis stage 5 CKD; suggested explanations include dialysis-related physiologic control, differences in ICU transfer threshold, survivor selection, and reversible dialysis-related indications. These are plausible hypotheses presented by the authors, not mechanisms established by the analysis.

  • Observational design permits association, not causal inference; residual confounding remains possible.
  • A single most recent outpatient eGFR may not prove chronicity and may misclassify baseline kidney function.
  • Albuminuria was absent from the primary CKD definition and available in only 39.3% for secondary analysis.
  • AKI present at ICU admission was not directly incorporated.
  • Administrative data lack some clinical granularity, including exact reason for ICU admission.
  • Excluding 303,195 admissions without baseline creatinine may select a sicker/more medically observed primary cohort, although the imputed analysis helps address this concern.

Model appraisal

Exposure measurement
4/5
Outpatient creatinine-based eGFR is stronger than diagnosis-code CKD classification; chronicity and albuminuria remain imperfect.
Outcome ascertainment
4/5
Mortality and dialysis are hard endpoints in linked provincial databases; some clinical context is absent.
Confounding control
4/5
Broad adjustment and removal of the renal MODS component are thoughtful, but residual confounding and goals-of-care/selection effects cannot be excluded.
Robustness
5/5
Multiple sensitivity analyses—Cox, median creatinine, imputation, albuminuria—support the direction and dose-response pattern.
Causal inference
2/5
The study was not designed to estimate a causal effect of CKD; interpretation should remain prognostic.
Clinical usefulness
4/5
The stage-specific KRT-dependence estimates are especially useful for counseling, while individual prediction remains uncertain.
Overall appraisal: strong evidence for a graded prognostic association between preexisting CKD severity and adverse ICU outcomes in a large health-system cohort, with credible robustness checks. The paper is less informative about causal mechanisms, individual-level prediction, and why maintenance-dialysis patients differ from non-dialysis stage 5 patients.

Practice takeaways

Risk stratification
Baseline CKD stage adds meaningful prognostic context after ICU admission; advanced CKD signals substantially higher death and KRT risk even after adjustment.
Kidney recovery counseling
For patients who start KRT in ICU and survive 90 days, ongoing dialysis dependence is common in stage 4 and extremely common in previously non-dialysis stage 5 CKD.
Dialysis nuance
Do not assume maintenance dialysis is equivalent to non-dialysis stage 5 for mortality prognosis; the observed relationship was nonmonotonic at this boundary.
Bedside limitation
These estimates are population averages and should complement—not replace—acute diagnosis, organ failure trajectory, frailty, goals of care, and patient-specific recovery markers.
