One-page synthesis

The chapter organizes pharmacologic care around diabetes type, comorbid disease, treatment burden, safety, weight, access, and individual preference rather than around A1C alone. For type 1 diabetes, insulin is essential. The chapter favors physiologic basal-prandial replacement, insulin analogs, education that links dosing to food and glucose patterns, and broad use of CGM and automated insulin delivery when safe and acceptable. It also addresses concentrated and inhaled insulin, noninsulin adjuncts, severe hypoglycemia prevention, and selected beta-cell replacement strategies.

For type 2 diabetes, the dominant 2026 framework is comorbidity-first treatment. ASCVD, heart failure, CKD, obesity, and MASLD/MASH can determine medication selection irrespective of A1C. GLP-1–based therapy is preferred to insulin when severe hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis is absent; if insulin is needed, the chapter recommends retaining useful noninsulin agents and combining insulin with GLP-1–based therapy when appropriate. The chapter also gives practical pathways for insulin initiation and intensification, overbasalization, CGM/AID, glucagon, cost barriers, shortages and compounding, childbearing potential, cancer-treatment hyperglycemia, glucocorticoids, transplantation, monogenic diabetes, and SGLT-associated ketoacidosis.

Bottom line. Read first. This is a high-value 2026 pharmacotherapy reference because the formal recommendations, medication-comparison table, insulin algorithms, and special-population guidance are directly usable. The most important constraint is heterogeneity of evidence maturity: many central recommendations are grade A, while several special-population and operational recommendations remain C or E.

Section condensation

Pharmacologic therapy for adults with type 1 diabetes

The chapter treats insulin replacement as essential and recommends CSII or multiple daily basal-prandial insulin for most adults. Insulin analogs or inhaled insulin are preferred over injectable human insulin when minimizing hypoglycemia is the priority. Dosing is individualized to food, glucose patterns, activity, illness, and treatment goals. Typical total daily dose estimates are 0.4–1 unit/kg/day, with lower initial requirements in newly diagnosed adults and roughly 30–50% supplied as basal insulin. CGM and AID are integrated into routine management, while pump users require explicit backup planning because interruption of rapid-acting insulin delivery can quickly cause metabolic decompensation.

The section also compares injection and pump regimens, concentrated insulin, inhaled insulin, pramlintide and other adjunctive approaches, and beta-cell replacement. Inhaled insulin offers rapid kinetics but requires pulmonary screening and is contraindicated in chronic lung disease. Pancreas or islet replacement can restore endogenous insulin secretion but introduces procedural and lifelong immunosuppression risks, limiting use to selected people with severe metabolic complications or transplantation-related indications.

Pharmacologic therapy for adults with type 2 diabetes

Medication selection starts with person-centered goals and comorbidity. Established or high-risk ASCVD, heart failure, and CKD move agents with demonstrated outcome benefit to the front of the plan irrespective of A1C. Obesity and symptomatic HFpEF expand the role of dual GIP/GLP-1 or GLP-1 therapy; MASLD/MASH adds liver-specific treatment priorities. Metformin remains a common effective and low-cost option when no comorbidity-driven choice supersedes it, but kidney function, gastrointestinal tolerance, and vitamin B12 status matter.

The chapter recommends timely treatment modification and discourages combinations that add burden without benefit, such as DPP-4 inhibitors with GLP-1–based therapy. GLP-1–based therapy is preferred to insulin for initial or add-on therapy when severe hyperglycemia or crisis is absent. When insulin is necessary, the chapter recommends preserving useful background therapies, combining insulin with GLP-1–based therapy when appropriate, reassessing doses, and watching for overbasalization rather than simply escalating basal insulin.

Additional recommendations for all people with diabetes

CGM, AID, glucagon access, medication affordability, diabetes self-management education and support, healthy behaviors, and attention to social determinants are treated as pharmacotherapy infrastructure rather than optional extras. CGM recommendations extend beyond insulin to noninsulin therapies that can cause hypoglycemia and to other treatment situations where CGM materially helps management.

The cost section gives U.S. acquisition-price comparisons for noninsulin agents and insulin products. These tables are useful for relative cost awareness but are dated to 15 July 2025 and do not estimate any one person's out-of-pocket cost. Lower-cost agents remain reasonable when access barriers dominate, but the chapter requires clinicians to weigh their hypoglycemia, weight, cardiovascular, kidney, and other adverse-effect profiles.

Special circumstances and populations

The chapter addresses non-FDA-approved compounded products, drug shortages, childbearing potential, immunotherapy- and targeted-therapy-associated hyperglycemia, glucocorticoids, posttransplant diabetes, and monogenic diabetes. Several recommendations in these areas carry C or E evidence grades, reflecting a thinner prospective evidence base than the major cardiorenal and glucose-lowering recommendations.

SGLT-associated ketoacidosis receives a dedicated safety discussion. Risk is highest in type 1 or insulin-deficient diabetes and can present with lower-than-expected glucose. The chapter therefore emphasizes risk-factor education, ketone monitoring, avoidance of ketogenic eating patterns, and discontinuation of SGLT inhibition in people with prior DKA.

Inputs: Current basal/prandial doses, CGM or SMBG patterns, hypoglycemia timing, meal intake, activity, and illness.

Figure 9.4 — Glucose-lowering medication algorithm for adults with type 2 diabetes

Primary split: (1) reduce cardiorenal risk and (2) achieve individualized weight and glycemic goals.
Cardiorenal branch: ASCVD/high risk, heart failure, and CKD drive selection of GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, or dual GIP/GLP-1 therapy according to demonstrated outcomes, irrespective of A1C when specified.
Metabolic branch: Choose agents by glucose-lowering efficacy, weight effect, hypoglycemia, adverse effects, access, and comorbidities including MASLD/MASH.
Key interpretation: The branches overlap; one medication plan can address several goals at once.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: current diabetes pharmacotherapy selection, insulin strategy, cardiorenal and liver comorbidity alignment, CGM/AID decisions, cost-aware treatment, and special-population planning. Do not use it as the only source for local formulary rules, individual product labeling, pregnancy management, or the complete ADA evidence-development method.
Relevance10/10

Directly governs contemporary pharmacologic treatment of adults with diabetes across insulin, cardiorenal disease, weight, liver disease, cost, technology, and special populations.

Evidence basis. The chapter contains 47 formal recommendations plus operational algorithms and medication tables.

Limiting factor. The chapter depends on other Standards sections for full disease-management context.

What would change the score. Would fall if used outside diabetes pharmacotherapy or without the companion Standards sections.

Novelty8/10

The chapter integrates newer 2026-era outcome evidence, GLP-1/GIP positioning, HFpEF/obesity, MASH, CGM/AID, shortages, oncology, and transplant considerations into one treatment framework.

Evidence basis. Several recommendations and algorithms reflect recent trial and implementation evidence.

Limiting factor. Many foundational insulin and metformin principles are established rather than novel.

What would change the score. Would rise with more genuinely new comparative evidence or formal evidence-to-decision detail within this section.

Method strength8/10

The Standards use a formal committee process and evidence-grading system, and each recommendation carries an evidence grade.

Evidence basis. Recommendations are explicitly graded and updated annually or more often when warranted.

Limiting factor. The detailed grading method, search process, and full conflict-management framework are delegated to separate Standards documents and are not reproduced here.

What would change the score. Would rise if this chapter itself included the evidence-search, selection, and recommendation-development details needed for independent method audit.

Evidence strength8/10

Central cardiorenal, insulin-technology, and treatment recommendations draw on randomized trials and outcome evidence, while the chapter clearly marks weaker C/E recommendations in less mature areas.

Evidence basis. Many high-impact recommendations are grade A and are supported by trial and meta-analytic literature in the cited bibliography.

Limiting factor. Evidence maturity varies substantially across special populations, compounding/shortage management, oncology-associated hyperglycemia, transplantation, and some implementation questions.

What would change the score. Would rise if more C/E recommendations were supported by prospective comparative trials with patient-centered outcomes.

External validity9/10

The chapter addresses a broad adult diabetes population and explicitly incorporates comorbid cardiovascular, kidney, liver, weight, transplant, cancer-treatment, pregnancy-planning, and access considerations.

Evidence basis. Person-centered selection and multiple comorbidity branches support broad clinical applicability.

Limiting factor. Cost tables and several regulatory statements are U.S.-specific; some special-population evidence is sparse.

What would change the score. Would fall in settings where medication availability, regulation, pricing, or population characteristics differ materially from those assumed by the chapter.

Implementation value10/10

The chapter is immediately usable for medication selection, insulin initiation/titration, CGM/AID decisions, hypoglycemia prevention, cost conversations, and special-population management.

Evidence basis. Figures 9.2, 9.4, and 9.5 provide decision pathways; Tables 9.1–9.4 provide regimen, medication, and cost comparisons.

Limiting factor. Implementation still requires local formulary, device access, patient education, monitoring capacity, and cross-reference to other Standards sections.

What would change the score. Would fall only if used as a stand-alone protocol without local operational rules or individualized clinical assessment.

Main strengths

The chapter combines formal graded recommendations with operational algorithms and tables. It explicitly decouples cardiorenal treatment from A1C when outcome evidence supports that move. It also integrates weight, liver disease, access, technology, and special-population safety into one medication framework.

Main limitations

Evidence strength is uneven across recommendations. Several special-population, shortage, compounding, and oncology recommendations rely on C or E evidence. The detailed evidence-grading and conflict-management process lives outside this chapter. Cost data are U.S.-specific and time-sensitive. Class-level medication summaries can hide within-class differences in outcome evidence or labeling.

Confidence in appraisal: high for chapter content and internal recommendation mapping; moderate-high for method appraisal because the chapter delegates the full Standards development method to separate source material.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Start with comorbidity, not A1C alone.
ASCVD, heart failure, CKD, obesity, and MASLD/MASH can determine the preferred agent even when additional glucose lowering is not the main need.
Prefer GLP-1–based therapy before insulin in many adults with type 2 diabetes.
Reserve immediate insulin priority for severe or symptomatic hyperglycemia, crisis, or other clear insulin-requiring situations.
Do not overbasalize.
If fasting glucose is controlled but overall glycemia remains high, evaluate postprandial patterns and add the appropriate prandial or adjunctive therapy.
Treat CGM, AID, and glucagon as medication-safety tools.
Technology and rescue therapy are integrated into pharmacologic management rather than handled as separate optional domains.
Keep cost and access inside the medication decision.
Use lower-cost therapies when necessary, but account for their hypoglycemia, weight, cardiovascular, kidney, and other tradeoffs.
Use extra caution with sparse-evidence special populations.
Compounding, shortages, oncology-associated hyperglycemia, transplantation, and SGLT-related ketosis contain more C/E guidance and warrant close source and patient-specific review.
