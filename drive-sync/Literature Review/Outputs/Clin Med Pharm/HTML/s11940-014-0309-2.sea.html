One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Anticoagulant selection in neurologic patients should be condition-specific rather than agent-first. The review favors no immediate systemic anticoagulation for acute ischemic stroke, LMWH for VTE with intracranial malignancy and for acute cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, warfarin for APS-associated thrombosis, and individualized anticoagulant choice after spinal cord injury.

The article is most useful as a snapshot of the 2014 transition from warfarin and heparin-based therapy to early direct oral anticoagulant use. Its strongest practical contribution is the side-by-side organization of pharmacology, dosing, monitoring, reversal, renal considerations, and neurologic bleeding concerns. For heparin-focused review, it contrasts UFH's rapid titratability and protamine reversibility with LMWH's more predictable pharmacokinetics, lower monitoring burden, partial protamine reversibility, renal accumulation, and historical preference in malignancy-associated VTE.

The principal limitation is temporal and methodological. The authors synthesize RCTs, meta-analyses, guidelines, and observational series without a reproducible search strategy or formal risk-of-bias framework. Several 2014 statements became obsolete as DOAC indications and reversal options evolved. Consequently, the article remains useful for historical reasoning and for locating older primary evidence, but it should not function as a current dosing or reversal reference.

Section condensation

Opinion statement and general principles

The authors frame neurologic thrombosis as a heterogeneous set of clinical problems requiring different anticoagulant choices. Warfarin is characterized as well studied and reversible but slow, interaction-prone, and monitoring-intensive; parenteral heparins as predictable but injection-dependent and only partly reversible; and the then-new oral agents as convenient and pharmacokinetically predictable but expensive, incompletely studied, and difficult to reverse.

The opinion statement supplies the article's clinical map: avoid immediate systemic anticoagulation in acute ischemic stroke; favor LMWH for VTE with intracranial malignancy; consider warfarin, LMWH, or a NOAC after spinal cord injury; use long-term warfarin with INR 2–3 for APS-associated thrombosis; and use LMWH acutely followed by warfarin for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.

Acute ischemic stroke

The review summarizes evidence that early UFH or LMWH did not conclusively reduce mortality or stroke-related morbidity and could increase hemorrhage. It also notes no conclusive warfarin benefit and greater major hemorrhage with moderate- or high-intensity warfarin. The authors therefore agree with early aspirin rather than systemic anticoagulation for acute ischemic stroke, while allowing prophylactic-dose UFH or LMWH for immobile patients to reduce VTE risk.

Thrombophilia testing is presented as selectively reasonable after the acute phase, particularly in younger patients or those with suggestive personal/family history. The authors caution that inherited thrombophilia findings after a first ischemic stroke may be coincidental and should not automatically trigger chronic anticoagulation.

Venous thromboembolism with intracranial tumors

The article reports high VTE risk in primary and metastatic intracranial malignancy and relatively low intracranial hemorrhage rates in cited anticoagulation series. Based on malignancy-associated VTE evidence and meta-analyses, the authors prefer LMWH over warfarin or UFH. For creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min, they discuss adjusted LMWH with anti-Xa monitoring or UFH followed by warfarin.

They do not regard hemorrhage-prone tumor histologies as absolute anticoagulation contraindications, although close observation is emphasized. After recent neurosurgery, they suggest systemic anticoagulation may be started around 3–5 days postoperatively with close observation. These timing and agent-selection statements are historical and require contemporary guideline verification.

Spinal cord injury

The review describes a high and variable DVT incidence after spinal cord injury, including despite prophylaxis. A cited meta-analysis did not show a significant VTE reduction from pharmacologic prophylaxis, yet the article endorses both mechanical and pharmacologic prophylaxis and urges vigilance for clinical VTE.

For established VTE, the authors consider heparin-based therapy, warfarin bridging, or a NOAC. They also note that clot recanalization may be slower in paralysis, potentially extending treatment duration.

Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS)

For venous or arterial thrombosis associated with APS, the review recommends UFH or LMWH as a bridge to warfarin with an INR target of 2–3. It cites randomized evidence that higher INR targets did not reduce recurrent thrombosis and increased bleeding.

Because recurrence after stopping anticoagulation is described as high, prolonged and often indefinite therapy is recommended. At publication, the authors considered NOACs attractive but explicitly noted absence of efficacy data in APS.

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis

The source summarizes trials and reviews suggesting neurologic benefit with UFH versus placebo, a trend toward benefit with LMWH, and a comparative mortality advantage for LMWH over UFH. The authors therefore recommend LMWH for acute cerebral venous sinus thrombosis and state that coexisting intracranial hemorrhage is not an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation.

After the acute phase, the article recommends warfarin for at least three months, with duration influenced by provoking factors and clot resolution. It acknowledges that ideal duration was uncertain and that NOACs had not been studied in this setting.

Cost considerations

The discussion distinguishes inpatient versus outpatient costs and hospital versus patient perspectives. The authors emphasize that patient copay can determine adherence and report wide observed monthly cost variation across parenteral agents and NOACs.

The practical conclusion is individualized: when no agent is clearly preferred or contraindicated, clinicians should consider both safety and the patient's ability to obtain the medication.

Treatment — warfarin

The treatment section reviews vitamin K antagonism, delayed onset, INR monitoring, drug and food interactions, and bridging for acute VTE. It advises continuing a parenteral anticoagulant for at least five days while warfarin is titrated to INR 2–3. The review reports major hemorrhage around 1–3% in many trials and highlights increased risk with unstable INR control.

Historical reversal recommendations include 10 mg IV vitamin K plus PCC and/or plasma-based strategies. The section also highlights clinically relevant interaction categories and patient factors associated with bleeding risk.

Treatment — unfractionated heparin

UFH is described as antithrombin-mediated inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa with a short, dose-dependent half-life. A weight-based treatment regimen of 80 U/kg bolus followed by 18 U/kg/hour is given, with PTT reassessment at six hours and nomogram-based titration. A lower dose is listed for ischemic stroke in the cited historical ACCP regimen.

The section emphasizes bleeding, HIT, injection-site reactions, and osteoporosis with prolonged use. Protamine 1 mg per 100 units of heparin, capped at 50 mg, is presented as reversal; protamine-associated hypotension and rare hypersensitivity/pulmonary complications are noted.

Treatment — low-molecular-weight heparins

LMWH is presented as sharing the antithrombin mechanism of UFH while producing proportionally greater factor Xa inhibition. The source gives anti-Xa:anti-IIa ratios of 3.8:1 for enoxaparin and 2.7:1 for dalteparin, and lists treatment doses of enoxaparin 1 mg/kg every 12 hours (or 1.5 mg/kg every 24 hours) and dalteparin 200 U/kg daily.

The review describes LMWH as historically preferred for malignancy-associated VTE and emphasizes predictable pharmacokinetics, limited routine monitoring, renal accumulation, bleeding/HIT risk, and partial protamine reversal. It gives a historical anti-Xa target of 0.6–1.0 for twice-daily enoxaparin and dalteparin and a 24-hour neuraxial hold/restart framework.

Treatment — fondaparinux

Fondaparinux is described as a synthetic pentasaccharide with selective factor Xa inhibition, once-daily dosing, negligible HIT risk, and weight-based treatment doses. The authors regard it as a reasonable VTE option but emphasize its long half-life and lack of an effective specific reversal agent.

For bleeding, the article notes that protamine is ineffective and cites recombinant factor VIIa as a possible historical strategy based on healthy-volunteer data.

Treatment — dabigatran, rivaroxaban, and apixaban

The direct oral anticoagulant sections summarize mechanisms, dosing, renal limits, drug-interaction pathways, bleeding risks, and the then-available reversal strategies. Dabigatran is presented as a direct thrombin inhibitor; rivaroxaban and apixaban as factor Xa inhibitors. At the time of publication, all three sections describe the absence of an established specific reversal agent.

The article's apixaban section reflects its publication date: it provides atrial-fibrillation dosing but no DVT/PE treatment regimen. This is a clear temporal marker showing why the source cannot be used as a contemporary DOAC reference.

Compliance with Ethics Guidelines

Marshall declared no conflict of interest. Connors disclosed personal fees from Boehringer Ingelheim. The article states that the authors performed no human or animal studies, confirming that the paper is a secondary review rather than primary clinical research.

Current-practice verification — external, not source-derived

This section is deliberately separated from the article summary. It verifies a small number of time-sensitive claims using current U.S. regulatory labeling and FDA safety information.

Enoxaparin renal dosingCurrent Lovenox labeling continues to use 1 mg/kg subcutaneously once daily for treatment of acute DVT/PE in severe renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min), while no dose adjustment is recommended for CrCl 30–80 mL/min with bleeding surveillance. Current label.
Apixaban VTE treatmentCurrent Eliquis labeling includes DVT/PE treatment at 10 mg twice daily for 7 days followed by 5 mg twice daily, an indication/regimen absent from this July 2014 review. Current label.
Dabigatran reversalIdarucizumab (Praxbind) is an FDA-approved specific reversal agent for dabigatran when urgent reversal is needed; the labeled dose is 5 g IV. This directly supersedes the article's 2014 statement that no established antidote existed. Current label.
Factor Xa antidote historyAndexanet alfa was approved in 2018 for rivaroxaban/apixaban reversal, but FDA concluded in 2025 that its serious risks outweighed benefits; U.S. commercial sales ended in December 2025 and the accelerated approval was withdrawn. Current reversal decisions therefore require up-to-date guidance rather than this 2014 review. FDA safety communication.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply — historical reference; do not use as stand-alone current practice guidance.
Best use: understanding the 2014 anticoagulation decision landscape, especially UFH/LMWH tradeoffs in neurologic disease, and identifying older primary studies for direct appraisal.

Basis: broad condition- and agent-specific coverage. Limit: historical practice. Would rise: current guideline integration.

Basis: heterogeneous source populations. Limit: recommendations are not validated as one unified pathway. Would rise: contemporary multicenter evidence by indication.

Implementation value
4/10

The tables are operationally clear, but current use is constrained by outdated DOAC indications, antidotes, and time-sensitive dosing/reversal guidance.

Basis: strong historical organization. Limit: medication-use content changes rapidly. Would rise: current labels/guidelines with versioned recommendations.

Main strengths

  • Condition-specific comparison rather than generic anticoagulant ranking.
  • Strong emphasis on neurologic bleeding risk, renal function, monitoring, reversal, and route/administration burden.
  • Two concise tables make historical pharmacology and dosing assumptions easy to inspect.
  • Explicit acknowledgment of evidence gaps for then-new oral anticoagulants.

Main limitations

  • Narrative review with no reproducible evidence-selection method.
  • Secondary summaries sometimes compress heterogeneous studies into directional statements without denominators or uncertainty.
  • Several clinical claims are temporally obsolete or incomplete by current labeling.
  • Current terminology and practice have moved beyond the 2014 "NOAC" era; direct primary-study and current-guideline review is required for practice decisions.

Confidence in appraisal: high for source description, moderate for underlying evidence quality. The full PDF and both tables were inspected directly; the cited primary studies were not individually re-appraised in this artifact.

Implementation / practice takeaways

For a heparin deep divePreserve the UFH-versus-LMWH contrasts: titratability and complete protamine responsiveness favor UFH in situations requiring rapid control, while LMWH offers more predictable exposure and historically strong cancer-VTE evidence but introduces renal accumulation and only partial protamine reversal.
For LiteratureAtom workTreat this paper as a secondary source. Trial-level claims should point to the cited primary publications for independent atoms; this review's statements should remain tagged as secondary reports or author conclusions.
For CDS or protocol buildingDo not encode 2014 dosing/reversal tables directly. Use them to identify decision variables — renal function, hemorrhage risk, HIT history, neuraxial timing, indication, reversibility, route, monitoring burden — then populate rules from current authoritative sources.
What to read nextPrioritize the cited primary evidence behind LMWH versus UFH/warfarin in intracranial malignancy and CVST, then pair it with current cancer-associated thrombosis, CVST, APS, neuraxial, and anticoagulant-reversal guidance.
