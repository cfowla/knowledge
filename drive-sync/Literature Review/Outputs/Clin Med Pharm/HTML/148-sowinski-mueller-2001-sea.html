One-page synthesis

Core thesis. High-flux hemodialysis removed cefazolin faster than high-efficiency dialysis, but the authors' simulations suggested that 15 or 20 mg/kg given after each dialysis session maintained mean cefazolin concentrations above the study's 8 mg/L susceptibility threshold across 2-day and 3-day interdialytic intervals.

The study enrolled 25 uninfected chronic hemodialysis patients. Ten used high-flux cellulose triacetate dialyzers at one site and 15 used several high-efficiency dialyzers at two sites. Each subject received one intravenous cefazolin dose of 15 mg/kg after dialysis. Serial serum sampling continued through the next dialysis session and for 2 hours afterward. The investigators fit a two-compartment model, estimated systemic and dialytic clearance, measured protein binding, and used the fitted parameters for 500-subject simulations of 15 and 20 mg/kg regimens over 48-hour and 72-hour intervals.

The clearest measured result is dialytic clearance: 30.9 ± 6.52 mL/min with high-flux dialysis versus 18.0 ± 6.26 mL/min with high-efficiency dialysis, P = 0.0001. Cefazolin reduction ratio was also larger with high-flux dialysis, 0.62 ± 0.08 versus 0.50 ± 0.07, P < 0.005. Systemic clearance was nearly identical, 2.87 versus 2.84 mL/min, P = 0.922. Table 4 shows that predialysis target attainment remained 98.4 to 100 percent across the single-dose simulations, while target attainment immediately after dialysis was lower, especially after high-flux dialysis and a 3-day interval.

Main constraints. Dialyzer group was tied to study site, dialysis operating parameters differed between groups, the sample was small, the high-flux group included no women, dialytic clearance was estimated indirectly, and the dosing claim comes from simulations rather than clinical outcomes. The source also contains a P-value inconsistency for the residual-urine comparison and an appended erratum correcting equation parameter designations.

Section condensation

Introduction

The paper frames cefazolin as a renewed alternative to vancomycin in dialysis practice and argues that older cefazolin dosing guidance was derived from low-permeability dialyzers. The authors identify a gap in direct dialytic-clearance data for newer high-efficiency and high-flux membranes and note uncertainty around 72-hour interdialytic intervals.

The stated objectives were to quantify cefazolin pharmacokinetics and dialytic clearance with high-efficiency and high-flux hemodialyzers and to develop practical postdialysis dosing strategies.

Methods

Twenty-five uninfected patients receiving chronic thrice-weekly hemodialysis were studied at three centers. Ten high-flux patients used CT190 or CT110 cellulose triacetate dialyzers at Indianapolis. Fifteen high-efficiency patients used F8, T175, T220, CA170, or CA210 dialyzers at Albany and Little Rock. After routine dialysis, each participant received cefazolin 15 mg/kg intravenously. The protocol collected dense serum samples through the next dialysis session and postdialysis period, plus urine in nonanuric patients.

Cefazolin was measured by HPLC. A two-compartment model was fit with ADAPT II. Dialytic clearance, systemic clearance, distribution clearance, volumes, half-life, renal clearance, and nonrenal clearance were derived. Simulations used 500 subjects per scenario and tested 15 or 20 mg/kg over 2-day and 3-day intervals using 8 mg/L as the target threshold. The appended erratum later corrected parameter designations in equations 1 and 2, so the original printed equations should not be reused without the correction.

Results

All 25 participants completed the study and no study-drug adverse effects were reported. High-flux and high-efficiency groups had similar mean age and body weight, but the high-flux group had no women. Dialysis time, blood flow, dialysate flow, BUN values, and Kt/V differed between groups, which matters because dialyzer type was not isolated from site and operating conditions.

High-flux dialysis produced higher cefazolin dialytic clearance, 30.9 versus 18.0 mL/min, P = 0.0001, and a larger cefazolin reduction ratio, 0.62 versus 0.50, P < 0.005. Systemic clearance was 2.87 versus 2.84 mL/min, P = 0.922. The overall cefazolin-to-urea reduction-ratio correlation was r = 0.73, P < 0.0001. Rebound after dialysis was small. Simulations predicted high predialysis target attainment for both 15 and 20 mg/kg, but immediate postdialysis target attainment dropped to 68.4 percent after high-flux dialysis in the 15 mg/kg, 3-day scenario.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the larger high-flux dialytic clearance as pharmacokinetically real but clinically modest over the full dosing interval. They argue that 15 or 20 mg/kg after dialysis should not require adjustment solely by high-efficiency versus high-flux dialyzer type. They also state that urine output did not appear to require dose adjustment in their cohort, though only three subjects were nonoliguric.

The strongest limitation acknowledged by the authors is indirect estimation of dialytic clearance. Direct arterial-venous or dialysate collection methods were described as the gold standard. The paper also emphasizes that mean simulated concentrations can hide individuals below the 8 mg/L threshold during dialysis. The Results report P = 0.4517 for systemic clearance by residual urine status, while the Discussion reports P = 0.34 for the same means. This artifact preserves the discrepancy.

Erratum

The May 2001 erratum states that equations 1 and 2 in the pharmacokinetic model description contained parameter-designation errors and supplies corrected equations. This appraisal does not reproduce or rely on the erroneous printed equations.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon for historical PK evidence; do not use alone for current practice. The measured clearance difference is useful and the dosing simulations are clear, but current clinical use needs verification against modern dialyzers, susceptibility standards, PK/PD targets, and guidance.

Clinical and pharmacotherapy appraisal

Population.
Uninfected chronic thrice-weekly hemodialysis patients. The study does not show treatment success in bacteremia, endocarditis, access infection, or other active infections.
Intervention.
Single observed dose of cefazolin 15 mg/kg IV after dialysis. Repeated 15 and 20 mg/kg regimens were simulated.
Comparator or exposure.
High-flux cellulose triacetate dialysis at one site versus several high-efficiency dialyzers at two sites. This was not randomized.
Outcomes.
PK parameters, reduction ratios, protein binding, model fit, serum-concentration simulations, and reported adverse effects.

Validity. The serial sampling and assay methods are strong for a small PK study. The main causal comparison is weaker because membrane category, site, dialysis time, blood flow, dialysate flow, and delivered Kt/V differed between groups. Direct dialytic clearance was not measured. The appended erratum means the corrected equations, not the original printed parameter designations, govern model interpretation.

Practice translation. The paper supports the historical observation that high-flux dialysis removes more cefazolin and provides a quantitative basis for postdialysis dosing simulations. It does not validate an infection-specific clinical endpoint, current susceptibility threshold, current dialyzer performance, or a current guideline recommendation. Those require external verification before changing practice.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use the measured result.
High-flux Cldial was about 71 percent greater than high-efficiency Cldial in this cohort, 30.9 versus 18.0 mL/min.
Keep the simulation boundary.
The 15 and 20 mg/kg dosing conclusions are model-based target-attainment results, not clinical cure data.
Do not ignore dialysis timing.
The weakest simulated target attainment occurred after dialysis following a 3-day interval, especially with 15 mg/kg and high-flux HD.
Check the erratum.
Any reuse of the model equations should use the corrected parameter designations from the appended May 2001 erratum.
Do not overread urine output.
The urine-output conclusion rests on three nonoliguric subjects and the paper contains a P-value inconsistency for that comparison.
For current dosing.
Verify modern PK data, dialyzer characteristics, susceptibility criteria, and current recommendations before using this paper as a dosing rule.
