One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In 9297 adults at high cardiovascular risk without known heart failure or low ejection fraction, ramipril 10 mg daily reduced a composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death and also reduced several major individual cardiovascular outcomes compared with placebo.

The main comparison randomized 4645 participants to ramipril and 4652 to placebo. The primary composite occurred in 14.0% versus 17.8% (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.70–0.86; P<0.001). Cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and all-cause mortality were each significantly lower with ramipril. Revascularization, all heart-failure events, worsening angina, new diabetes, and diabetes-related complications also favored ramipril; hospitalization for unstable angina and hospitalization for heart failure were not significantly reduced.

Methodologically, the trial is strong: large, randomized, double-blind, centrally adjudicated, intention-to-treat, multinational, and with 99.9% vital-status ascertainment. Important cautions are the active run-in that removed 1035 patients before randomization, declining adherence and ACE-inhibitor crossover, incomplete direct measurement of left ventricular function, and early stopping for benefit. The trial remains landmark evidence, but its broad “high-risk” treatment implication should be translated through current guideline indications rather than applied as a universal contemporary rule.

Section condensation

Abstract / background

The investigators began from evidence that ACE inhibitors improved outcomes in patients with left ventricular dysfunction and hypothesized that renin–angiotensin-system inhibition could also prevent atherothrombotic events and diabetes-related complications in a broader high-risk population. HOPE was designed to test that hypothesis prospectively in patients without known heart failure or low ejection fraction.

The corrected abstract reports a significant reduction in the primary composite and multiple individual outcomes. The cardiac-arrest abstract value must be read as RR 0.62; P=0.02, not the originally printed RR 0.63; P=0.03.

Methods

HOPE used a double-blind 2×2 factorial design. Men and women ≥55 years were eligible with established vascular disease or diabetes plus another cardiovascular risk factor. A run-in exposed 10,576 eligible patients to ramipril 2.5 mg then placebo; 1035 were excluded before randomization for nonadherence, side effects, abnormal creatinine/potassium, or consent withdrawal. The full-dose comparison included 4645 ramipril and 4652 placebo participants; a separate 244-patient low-dose arm was not part of the main comparison.

Visits occurred at 1 month, 6 months, then every 6 months. Primary/secondary events were centrally adjudicated. Analyses were intention-to-treat with Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests, stratified by vitamin E assignment; Cox interaction tests were used for subgroups. The data and safety monitoring board recommended termination in March 1999 after repeated crossing of benefit boundaries; vital status was known for 99.9% of randomized participants.

Results

Baseline characteristics were similar. The primary composite was reduced from 17.8% to 14.0% (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.70–0.86). Cardiovascular death fell from 8.1% to 6.1%, MI from 12.3% to 9.9%, stroke from 4.9% to 3.4%, and all-cause death from 12.2% to 10.4%. Noncardiovascular mortality did not differ. Benefits were evident by 2 years and were broadly consistent across prespecified subgroups; in 4772 participants with documented EF ≥0.40, the primary RR was 0.73 (95% CI 0.63–0.84).

Secondary/other outcomes were mixed but generally favorable: revascularization, all heart failure, cardiac arrest, worsening angina, new diabetes, and diabetes-related complications were reduced; hospitalization for unstable angina and hospitalization for heart failure were not statistically significant. At study end BP averaged 136/76 with ramipril vs 139/77 mm Hg with placebo. Ramipril-related discontinuation was more frequent for cough (7.3% vs 1.8%), hypotension/dizziness (1.9% vs 1.5%), and angioedema (0.4% vs 0.2%).

Discussion / authors’ interpretation

The authors interpreted the results as evidence that ACE inhibition provides vascular protection beyond populations with heart failure or low EF, noting benefit despite substantial baseline use of aspirin, beta-blockers, and other therapies. They argued that the small BP difference probably did not explain the entire benefit and proposed additional vascular and metabolic mechanisms.

The corrected discussion states that heart failure was reduced among patients with no evidence of impairment of left ventricular systolic function. The authors estimated that treating 1000 patients for four years would prevent about 150 events in approximately 70 patients. This statement is an author-level synthesis rather than a single independently adjudicated endpoint.

Validity

Strengths: randomization, blinding, placebo control, large sample, prespecified outcomes, central adjudication, intention-to-treat analysis, multinational recruitment, nearly complete vital-status follow-up, and concordant benefit across major patient-centered endpoints. The primary result is precise and not dependent on a single surrogate.

Limitations: the pre-randomization run-in enriches for patients able to tolerate and adhere to therapy; assigned-dose adherence declined and placebo participants increasingly received open-label ACE inhibitors; LV function was not measured in all participants; and early stopping for benefit can increase uncertainty around the final effect magnitude. The trial also predates many current prevention strategies, so absolute benefits may not transfer unchanged.

Safety

Ramipril was generally tolerated but caused more discontinuation for cough, with smaller absolute excesses for hypotension/dizziness and angioedema. A crude model calculation from rounded percentages gives an excess cough-discontinuation risk of 5.5 percentage points (NNH ≈18 for discontinuation due to cough); this is not a source-reported NNH and has no trial-reported CI in Table 2.

Practice translation and current verification

External verification, separate from the 2000 source. As of August 12, 2026, the current ACC/AHA chronic coronary disease guideline remains the 2023 multisociety guideline. Its RAAS-inhibitor section supports RAAS inhibition for higher-risk CCD patients with LVEF ≤40% and/or comorbid diabetes, CKD, or hypertension, while noting less certain benefit in CCD with LVEF >40% and none of those comorbidities. The guideline explicitly discusses HOPE and notes the relatively low concurrent use of other risk-reduction medications compared with later trials.

A 2025 AHA/ACC chronic coronary disease performance-measures update continues targeted ACE-inhibitor/ARB quality measures for patients with diabetes or LV systolic dysfunction. Together, these current sources support viewing HOPE as foundational efficacy evidence rather than a blanket modern mandate to add ramipril to every patient meeting the original HOPE entry criteria.

Use nowUse HOPE to understand why RAAS inhibition became central in selected high-risk vascular patients and to contextualize current CCD recommendations.
MonitorBP, renal function, potassium, cough, hypotension, and angioedema when RAAS inhibition is used clinically; apply current guideline indications and contraindications.
Do not overclaimThe new-diabetes finding is secondary/other outcome evidence from this trial and is not, by itself, a reason to prescribe an ACE inhibitor solely to prevent diabetes.
