Verdict: Read first for a heparin deep dive; do not use alone for practice.
Best use: a concrete example of institution-specific UFH nomogram auditing and implementation in a population at high intracranial-bleeding risk. The clinical signal is hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

The institution increased its high-risk UFH starting infusion from 12 to 18 units/kg/h, kept a no-bolus strategy, and retained a conservative aPTT target of 50-70 seconds. The QI cohort reached therapeutic aPTT sooner by the reported medians (15 vs 28 hours), but the Kaplan-Meier comparison did not meet conventional statistical significance (P=0.07). Observed bleeding occurred in 3/21 patients in each cohort, and no thrombotic events were observed, but the authors explicitly state that the study was far too small to assess safety or demonstrate reduction in thrombosis.

The strongest contribution is operational: the protocol change was reviewed by P&T, adopted in 2016, reevaluated later, expanded institution-wide, and monitored through a critical-care dashboard. The principal validity problem is causal attribution: historical and QI cohorts were nonconcurrent, differed substantially in indications, and had different baseline aPTT values. The source itself estimates baseline aPTT could explain roughly 3 hours of the median difference. The endpoint remains aPTT target attainment rather than bleeding, thrombosis, mortality, or neurologic outcome.

Clinical question and methods

P: Adult neurocritical care patients needing therapeutic anticoagulation but considered at high risk of intracranial hemorrhage.
I: UFH 18 units/kg/h, no initial bolus, target aPTT 50-70 s.
C: Historical UFH 12 units/kg/h, no initial bolus, same aPTT target.
O: Primary - time to therapeutic aPTT. Secondary - subtherapeutic/supratherapeutic aPTT exposure; observed bleeding and thrombotic complications.

The study compared 21 retrospectively identified historical patients (May-October 2014) with 21 prospectively observed QI patients (February-July 2015). Selection of the high-risk protocol was discretionary. No other protocol changes were reported. Pharmacists knew the increased starting rate; the treating team was reportedly unaware of the dose increase.

Time to first aPTT of 50-70 seconds was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier methods and log-rank testing. Baseline aPTT was evaluated with univariate linear regression. Percentage of time subtherapeutic was compared with Mann-Whitney testing and supratherapeutic time with a t-test. The report states adherence to SQUIRE 2.0.

The authors estimated the 10.7-second baseline difference conferred about a 3-hour advantage to the QI cohort, leaving roughly 12 hours of the observed median improvement potentially due to dose or other factors.

In an April 2021 manual reevaluation, 9 evaluable patients reportedly reached therapeutic levels in a mean 14 hours. The paragraph reports 120 total aPTT measurements but then gives 37/128 and 29/124 as denominators for subtherapeutic and supratherapeutic results. This internal denominator inconsistency limits exact reuse of those follow-up percentages.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 9/10

Directly addresses UFH nomogram optimization in high-bleeding-risk neurocritical care, a narrow but highly pertinent anticoagulation problem.

Evidence basis: Exact 12 vs 18 units/kg/h no-bolus protocols, aPTT target, QI follow-up, and safety observations.

Limiting factor: Small and institution-specific.

Would raise score: A larger multicenter neurocritical cohort with patient-centered outcomes.

Novelty 7/10

The no-bolus, lower-aPTT-target neurocritical nomogram and longitudinal QI follow-up fill a sparsely studied practice niche.

Evidence basis: Authors report little prior direct evidence for no-bolus nomograms in this population.

Limiting factor: QI report rather than a controlled efficacy trial.

Would raise score: Contemporary comparative trials or multicenter registry evidence.

Method strength 4/10

The historical-control design is transparent but vulnerable to confounding and secular changes.

Evidence basis: 21 patients per cohort; prospective QI cohort compared with prior retrospective cohort; predefined analyses.

Limiting factor: Nonrandomized, nonconcurrent cohorts with different indications and baseline aPTT.

Would raise score: Concurrent randomized or well-adjusted multicenter comparison.

Evidence strength 4/10

The main signal favors faster target attainment but is a surrogate and the primary comparison was not statistically significant.

Evidence basis: Median 15 vs 28 h; P=0.07; equal observed bleeding counts; no thrombotic events.

Limiting factor: Underpowered safety assessment and no demonstrated patient-centered benefit.

Would raise score: Adequate power for bleeding/thrombosis and robust causal estimates.

External validity 3/10

Results come from one tertiary academic neurocritical care unit using a local aPTT-based protocol.

Evidence basis: Institution-specific nomogram and physician-selected high-risk population.

Limiting factor: Small sample, heterogeneous indications, local monitoring practices.

Would raise score: Replication across institutions and anti-Xa/aPTT monitoring strategies.

Implementation value 8/10

The report gives a concrete nomogram change, target, audit process, institutional rollout, and dashboard monitoring approach.

Evidence basis: Dose, target, no-bolus policy, P&T oversight, 12-month/2020/2021 reevaluations, dashboard measure.

Limiting factor: Implementation details are stronger than efficacy evidence; direct adoption is not justified.

Would raise score: External validation plus explicit adjustment algorithm and safety-monitoring thresholds.

Validity

Major threats: historical nonconcurrent control, physician-selected protocol use, small N, heterogeneous and imbalanced indications, baseline aPTT imbalance, limited adjustment, and a surrogate primary endpoint. The study cannot support a claim of equivalent bleeding risk or reduced thrombosis.

Strengths: exact protocol parameters are explicit; outcomes and bleeding definitions are described; the authors acknowledge central limitations; the QI process includes repeated institutional reassessment and operational monitoring.

Practice translation

The paper supports the practice of auditing local UFH nomogram performance rather than assuming a conservative nomogram is effective. It does not justify universal adoption of 18 units/kg/h without bolus for high-risk neurocritical care. Local assay characteristics, baseline aPTT, indication, bleeding risk, timing after intracranial pathology/neurosurgery, and institutional monitoring processes remain material.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use as a model for local QI: define target, audit time-to-target, examine bleeding/thrombotic events, review outliers, and reassess after implementation.

Do not overclaim

Do not interpret 3/21 vs 3/21 bleeding as safety equivalence, or absence of thrombosis as evidence of efficacy.

Build / monitor

A local dashboard can track percentage of UFH measurements in target range, but patient-centered safety and efficacy outcomes should accompany surrogate monitoring.

Read next

Primary comparative evidence on weight-based UFH nomograms, low- vs standard-intensity UFH, and neurocritical-care anticoagulation timing/safety is needed to contextualize this QI report.
