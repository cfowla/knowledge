One-page synthesis

Core thesis. KDIGO treats HCV as a kidney-care problem as well as a liver infection. The guideline links systematic screening, DAA treatment, dialysis infection control, transplant strategy, and HCV-associated glomerular disease into one CKD-specific management framework.

The formal guidance is broad. There are 62 numbered guidance statements: 19 are Level 1A, 8 are Level 1B, 7 are Level 1C, 2 are Level 1D, 3 are Level 2B, 1 is Level 2C, 5 are Level 2D, and 17 are ungraded. Chapter 1 covers HCV detection, repeat testing, liver fibrosis and portal hypertension assessment, and kidney/HBV/HAV/HIV evaluation. Chapter 2 recommends DAA evaluation across CKD, dialysis, and transplant populations, with regimen selection driven by treatment history, drug interactions, kidney function, fibrosis, transplant plans, and comorbidity. Chapter 3 puts infection-control practice, surveillance, and outbreak response ahead of patient isolation or dedicated dialysis machines. Chapter 4 makes kidney transplantation the preferred kidney-replacement strategy for CKD G5 despite HCV, expands use of HCV-infected donor kidneys, and ties timing of DAA therapy to donor and center factors. Chapter 5 prioritizes antiviral therapy for HCV-associated glomerulonephritis and adds immunosuppression for selected severe or refractory disease.

The evidence supplement supports the treatment update more strongly for virologic cure than for long-term kidney, graft, quality-of-life, or mortality outcomes. DAA studies consistently report high SVR12, but many are single-group or observational studies. Evidence is thinner for comparative regimen choice, isolation strategies in dialysis, and HCV-associated glomerulonephritis. That difference between virologic efficacy and broader clinical outcomes is the main evidence boundary.

Chapter condensation

Chapter 1. Detection and evaluation

Screen for HCV at initial CKD evaluation, when starting or transferring to in-center hemodialysis, at kidney-transplant evaluation, and, with weaker evidence, when starting peritoneal dialysis or home hemodialysis. In-center hemodialysis patients should undergo repeat HCV screening every 6 months. Resolved infection still requires NAT surveillance for reinfection. KDIGO also recommends fibrosis staging, initial noninvasive testing, portal-hypertension assessment when advanced fibrosis is suspected, and routine kidney, HAV, HBV, and HIV assessment.

Chapter 2. DAA treatment

All CKD G1-G5, dialysis, and kidney-transplant patients with HCV should be evaluated for DAA therapy. Regimen selection is conditional, not a fixed hierarchy. The guideline names prior HCV treatment, drug interactions, GFR, fibrosis, transplant candidacy, and comorbidity as selection factors. It requires medication-interaction review before therapy and calcineurin-inhibitor monitoring in transplant recipients. HBV testing precedes DAA treatment because reactivation can occur.

Chapter 3. Hemodialysis infection control

The prevention strategy is standard infection control: hand hygiene and glove changes, safe injectable-medication preparation and administration, environmental cleaning, and separation of clean from contaminated supplies. KDIGO recommends audits and active tracking of HCV results. It recommends against dedicated HCV dialysis machines and suggests against isolating HCV-infected patients. When a likely dialysis-related new infection appears, the response should intensify testing and infection-control practice.

Chapter 4. Kidney transplantation

Kidney transplantation remains the preferred treatment for CKD G5 regardless of HCV. Liver fibrosis and portal hypertension determine whether kidney-alone or simultaneous liver-kidney transplantation is appropriate. DAA timing depends on donor type, expected wait time, center policy on HCV-infected donor kidneys, and fibrosis. KDIGO recommends considering HCV-infected donor kidneys regardless of recipient HCV status, with informed consent and confirmed early access to DAAs for HCV-uninfected recipients.

Chapter 5. HCV-associated glomerulonephritis

DAAs are first-line therapy for HCV-associated glomerulonephritis. Stable disease without nephrotic syndrome should receive antiviral treatment before other therapy. Cryoglobulinemic flare or rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis warrants DAA therapy plus immunosuppression, with plasma exchange as an option. Persistent histologically active disease after antiviral therapy warrants immunosuppression, with rituximab as first-line immunosuppressive treatment.

Guideline methods and transparency

KDIGO used an independent Evidence Review Team from Brown University, PICOS-based questions, predefined screening criteria, standardized extraction and critical appraisal, and GRADE for evidence and recommendation strength. The 2022 update focused systematic reviews on DAA treatment, transplantation, and HCV-associated glomerular disease. It retained the 2018 evidence review for detection/evaluation and hemodialysis infection control.

The appendix is unusually useful because it reports where the KDIGO process did not meet individual Institute of Medicine standards. Examples include no public final review protocol, no protocol peer review, no systematic citation chasing of eligible studies, no web search, incomplete processes for unpublished-data solicitation, no formal analytic framework for each review question, and several gaps in documenting the rationale for synthesis decisions. On guideline development, the appendix reports no patient member or patient advocate in the development group and several conflict-of-interest process differences from IOM standards.

KDIGO also states its search was not exhaustive. It did not search specialty or regional databases, hand-search journals, or systematically search review articles and textbook chapters. Those are explicit source limitations, not inferred criticisms.

What to trust most

The most reusable content is the CKD-specific organization of care: who to screen and when, infection-control priorities in hemodialysis, the need to assess drug interactions and HBV before DAA therapy, the transplant decision framework, and the separation of straightforward HCV-associated glomerulonephritis from severe or refractory disease.

What needs caution

Do not read Figure 1 as a current formulary or regimen ranking. The source says it is not ranked and refers readers elsewhere for current HCV treatment details. The evidence profiles also show that high SVR12 does not automatically translate into equally strong evidence for death, graft, kidney-function, or quality-of-life outcomes. The internal N discrepancy for sofosbuvir/velpatasvir in G5D adds another reason to check primary data before reusing that number.

Practical use

This guideline is well suited for CKD/HCV screening protocols, hemodialysis infection-control policy, transplant referral pathways, DAA medication-interaction checks, and HCV-associated glomerulonephritis decision support. It is less suitable as a stand-alone 2026 regimen-selection source without current verification. For CDS, preserve the recommendation grade, source date, CKD/transplant population, and the distinction between graded recommendations and ungraded guidance.
