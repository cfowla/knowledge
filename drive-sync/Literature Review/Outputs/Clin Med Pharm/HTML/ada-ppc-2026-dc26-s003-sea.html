One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Prevention is risk-stratified and multimodal. For type 2 diabetes, the strongest foundation is structured lifestyle intervention with sustained weight loss and physical activity, with metformin reserved for selected high-risk groups and other pharmacotherapy used mainly to support weight, glycemic, or cardiovascular goals rather than as stand-alone prevention. Cardiovascular risk factors should be treated aggressively rather than allowing concern about incident diabetes to undermine proven cardiovascular therapy. For presymptomatic type 1 diabetes, surveillance is structured by stage and risk, while teplizumab is the disease-modifying option discussed for selected people aged ≥8 years with stage 2 disease.

What changes practice. The chapter gives clear monitoring intervals, DPP targets, high-risk thresholds for metformin and intensified prevention, B12 surveillance considerations during long-term metformin therapy, and explicit instructions not to stop statins because of diabetes risk. It also broadens prevention beyond glycemia by incorporating access, sleep, cardiovascular risk, and person-centered benefit–harm decisions.

What should not be overclaimed. Several pharmacologic and lifestyle associations are secondary reports, not direct evidence generated by this guideline. Vitamin D, testosterone, valsartan, and lifestyle modification in stage 1/2 type 1 diabetes do not have evidence supporting routine preventive use here. The teplizumab delay evidence summarized in the chapter is centered on one prevention trial and selected at-risk relatives. A printed HLA-DR4 subgroup confidence interval is malformed in the source and should not be silently repaired.

Section-by-section synthesis

Monitoring and risk detection

Prediabetes should trigger at least annual monitoring, while presymptomatic type 1 diabetes receives more structured surveillance with A1C roughly every 6 months and annual 75-g OGTT, modified by individual risk and potentially supplemented by CGM. The rationale is practical: laboratory detection identifies a window in which prevention or delay is possible, while A1C limitations and diagnostic details are delegated to Section 2.

The chapter explicitly distinguishes prediabetes as hyperglycemia preceding type 2 diabetes from presymptomatic type 1 disease. This matters because the monitoring tools overlap but the disease trajectories and preventive options differ.

Lifestyle behavior change for type 2 diabetes prevention

The DPP remains the operational model: structured reduced-calorie eating, at least 150 min/week of moderate activity, and a weight-loss goal of roughly 5–7% (the original DPP target was 7%). The chapter reports a 58% reduction in incident type 2 diabetes over 3 years in DPP and sustained benefit in long-term follow-up of DPPOS, Finnish DPS, and Da Qing. At 21 years, lifestyle and metformin still increased median diabetes-free survival by 3.5 and 2.5 years versus placebo, although most treatment effect accrued during the original DPP phase and major cardiovascular events were not significantly different across groups.

Nutrition is individualized rather than reduced to one macronutrient formula. Mediterranean, plant-based, DASH, and lower-carbohydrate patterns are discussed as reasonable evidence-based options. Physical activity beyond aerobic exercise—including resistance training and interruption of sedentary time—can support prevention. Delivery is part of effectiveness: group formats and certified technology-assisted programs can expand reach, yet retention and access disparities remain important implementation limitations.

Sleep characteristics

Sleep is presented as a modifiable risk context rather than a stand-alone diabetes-prevention prescription. The chapter summarizes a U-shaped duration relationship with the lowest risk around 7 h/day, up to about 50% higher risk with very short or long sleep, a 40% higher risk with poor sleep quality, and 2.5-fold higher odds of type 2 diabetes with evening chronotype in one cited analysis.

These are association-level findings. They support attention to sleep within broader health-behavior care but do not establish that changing sleep duration or chronotype alone prevents diabetes.

Pharmacologic interventions and metformin

Metformin is the preferred diabetes-prevention drug when pharmacotherapy is selected, especially in younger high-risk adults, BMI ≥35 kg/m², higher fasting glucose or A1C, and prior GDM. The chapter reports that metformin was less effective overall than lifestyle in DPP but comparable in selected subgroups; among people with prior GDM, metformin and intensive lifestyle each reduced diabetes risk by about 50%. Long-term metformin also requires attention to vitamin B12 deficiency, with stronger surveillance after prolonged use or in people with clinical risk factors.

Other drugs require indication-specific reasoning. Valsartan and testosterone are not recommended solely for diabetes prevention. Vitamin D trials and meta-analyses suggest at most modest, uncertain prevention effects and do not support widespread use. Metformin may be used proactively in selected high-risk patients receiving high-dose glucocorticoids or PI3Kα inhibitors; the supporting evidence includes small randomized studies and an alpelisib prevention study rather than large prevention programs.

Prevention of vascular disease and person-centered goals

Prediabetes is treated as a cardiovascular-risk state. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, tobacco use, weight, and other modifiable factors warrant active management. Statins modestly increase incident diabetes risk—the chapter cites a DPP pooled HR of 1.36—but cardiovascular and mortality benefit is judged to outweigh this harm, so statins should not be withheld or stopped for this reason. Pioglitazone after stroke/TIA in people with insulin resistance may lower recurrent vascular events and diabetes risk, but weight gain, edema, and fractures constrain its use.

Risk is heterogeneous. DPP participants meeting higher-risk phenotypes had substantially higher diabetes incidence than older community cohorts with laboratory-only prediabetes. The chapter therefore favors person-centered goals, with more intensive prevention for BMI ≥35 kg/m², higher glucose/A1C, or prior GDM, while avoiding reflexive intervention intensity in older adults whose competing risks and likelihood of regression differ.

Prevention or delay of symptomatic type 1 diabetes

Observational TEDDY and DAISY data link lower physical activity and higher-glycemic-index or sugar intake with progression among autoantibody-positive children, but the chapter explicitly states that efficacy of lifestyle intervention for stage 1/2 type 1 diabetes has not yet been reported. These associations are hypothesis-supporting rather than practice-changing intervention evidence.

Teplizumab is the central disease-modifying intervention. The summarized prevention trial randomized 44 people to a 14-day course and 32 to placebo; median time to stage 3 disease was 48.4 versus 24.4 months, and progression occurred in 43% versus 72% (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.22–0.78). Common adverse reactions were transient lymphopenia (73%) and rash (36%). Treatment should be discussed with selected people aged ≥8 years with stage 2 type 1 diabetes and delivered by appropriately trained personnel.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best used as the current ADA prevention framework and as a map to the primary prevention evidence. Do not use it as a substitute for trial-level appraisal when exact effects, subgroup validity, or harms drive a decision.

Principal limit: Relevance does not itself establish evidence certainty.

Novelty

Annual Standards updates synthesize and revise current practice, but the chapter is not primary research.

Evidence basis: Updated monitoring, DPP, metformin, cardiovascular, and teplizumab guidance.

Principal limit: Most evidence is synthesized from prior trials and observational studies.

Would change score: New comparative prevention trials or major new intervention classes would increase novelty.

Evidence strength

Core type 2 prevention guidance rests on major randomized trials and long follow-up, while some topics rely on observational data, small trials, or expert evidence.

Evidence basis: DPP/DPPOS, DPS, Da Qing, IRIS, metformin evidence, and the teplizumab trial.

Principal limit: Several recommendations are grade E or B; presymptomatic type 1 lifestyle evidence is observational, and teplizumab delay evidence is centered on a single prevention trial.

Would change score: Additional replicated trials and long-term patient-centered outcomes would increase certainty.

8/10

External validity

The chapter spans broad high-risk populations and implementation settings but repeatedly requires risk stratification and individualization.

Evidence basis: Adult DPP populations, older-adult ARIC data, prior GDM, cancer therapy, glucocorticoid exposure, and stage 2 type 1 diabetes.

Principal limit: Some evidence comes from selected trial populations; participation/retention disparities and age-specific risk modify applicability.

Would change score: More pragmatic studies across diverse settings and underrepresented populations would improve generalizability.

9/10

Implementation value

The chapter provides thresholds, monitoring intervals, prevention targets, drug-selection conditions, and access considerations that map directly to care pathways.

Evidence basis: Annual/6-month monitoring, 5–7% weight loss, ≥150 min/week activity, metformin high-risk criteria, B12 monitoring, and teplizumab eligibility.

Principal limit: Some implementation details are delegated to other Standards sections and external program requirements.

Would change score: Integrated algorithms and locally adaptable workflows would raise implementation value further.

Main strengths

  • Clear, graded recommendations linked to recognizable prevention pathways.
  • Long-term type 2 diabetes prevention evidence is preserved, including DPP/DPPOS and international prevention cohorts/trials.
  • Implementation concerns—cost, delivery mode, access, retention, medication monitoring, and risk stratification—are integrated into practice guidance.
  • Prevention is broadened beyond glucose to cardiovascular risk and person-centered goals.

Main limitations

  • The full guideline-development method and evidence-grade definitions are outside this section.
  • Primary studies were not independently reappraised here; trial effects are secondary reports.
  • Some evidence is observational or from small trials, especially sleep, stage 1/2 type 1 lifestyle factors, glucocorticoid prevention, and PI3Kα-inhibitor hyperglycemia prevention.
  • The HLA-DR4 subgroup confidence interval is malformed in the source PDF.
  • Cross-referenced diagnostic tables and other Standards sections are necessary for complete implementation but are outside this file.
