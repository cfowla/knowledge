Coverage: Abstract through concluding remarks were synthesized. Graphic abstract, Table 1, Figure 1, Figure 2, Table 2, and the supplementary figure were reconciled. References were not treated as evidence objects; all 241 are exported as a separate task queue.

Scope warning. This is a narrative mechanistic review. It is not a clinical guideline or prospective treatment study. Its proposal to add anti-inflammatory therapy during chemotherapy is a hypothesis requiring preclinical and clinical validation, not a practice recommendation.

One-page synthesis

The review advances a mechanistic model in which cytotoxic chemotherapy can be both antineoplastic and pro-inflammatory. Chemotherapy-induced senescence may generate a SASP rich in cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and MMPs; individual drugs are linked to inflammatory signaling through NF-kB, MAPK, TLR4, NLRP3/inflammasome pathways and ROS. Doxorubicin, cisplatin, paclitaxel, 5-fluorouracil, carboplatin, etoposide, daunorubicin, cyclophosphamide and mitomycin C are used as examples.

The review then connects chronic inflammation to cancer initiation, angiogenesis, EMT/metastasis, progression and recurrence. ROS/RNS are positioned as mutagenic and DNA-repair-disrupting signals; HIF-1/VEGF and inflammatory cytokines support angiogenesis; NF-kB, STAT3, TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6 and PGE2 are linked to EMT, invasion, immune suppression, proliferation and apoptosis resistance. Cancer stem cells are proposed as a recurrence bridge: intrinsic drug resistance allows residual CSC survival, while inflammatory signaling and MDSC-mediated immune suppression may support renewed proliferation. Chemotherapy itself can also be immunosuppressive or immunostimulatory depending on dose, schedule and agent; some regimens induce immunogenic cell death, and the review discusses chemo-immunotherapy in the context of PD-L1 induction.

Core judgment: The article is useful as a mechanistic map and hypothesis generator, but its evidence architecture is not strong enough to support clinical addition of anti-inflammatory drugs to chemotherapy. The absence of a systematic search, study-selection framework, risk-of-bias assessment and evidence grading substantially limits confidence in the causal and practice-level conclusions.

Section condensation

Introduction and chemotherapy mechanisms

The authors frame chemotherapy failure and relapse partly through tumor-microenvironment biology. They review standard cytotoxic mechanisms including apoptosis, cell-cycle arrest, autophagy, DNA alkylation/damage, antimetabolite activity, microtubule interference, ROS generation and topoisomerase inhibition. Many of these pathways can culminate in oxidative stress, ATM/ATR-p53/p21 signaling and accelerated senescence.

The article's specific objective is to examine chemotherapy drugs with pro-inflammatory properties, then connect inflammation to tumor initiation, angiogenesis, EMT/metastasis, progression and recurrence.

Chemotherapy-induced inflammation

Senescent tumor cells are described as activating NF-kB and C/EBP-beta programs that produce SASP components such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, GM-CSF, MMP-1/MMP-3 and MCP-2. The review also highlights drug-specific mechanisms: cyclophosphamide-associated inflammatory mediators/MDSCs, paclitaxel-TLR4/NF-kB signaling, doxorubicin-associated gut barrier/LPS-TLR4 and inflammasome signaling, cisplatin-related ROS/NF-kB/TNF-alpha signaling, and p38-MAPK activation by several agents.

These claims are summarized in Table 1 and the supplementary figure. They are mechanistically coherent, but the underlying evidence varies by model system and is not graded for certainty.

Inflammatory disease and cancer

The review uses chronic inflammatory disease as contextual evidence that sustained inflammatory signaling can contribute to carcinogenesis. It reports secondary estimates of colon-cancer occurrence in inflammatory bowel disease (about 5-10% after 20 years and 10-12% after 30 years) and notes that approximately 50-70% of patients with lung cancer have COPD. GERD/esophageal cancer and chronic hepatitis or alcohol-associated liver cancer are discussed as additional inflammation-linked examples.

These numerical examples come from cited sources rather than original analyses in this review; they should be checked against the primary references before being reused quantitatively.

Tumor initiation, angiogenesis and EMT/metastasis

For initiation, ROS/RNS and nitric oxide are presented as sources of DNA damage, mutation and impaired repair, while NF-kB/STAT3 signaling is linked to survival and epigenetic changes. For angiogenesis, inflammatory mediators including ROS, STAT3, NF-kB, IL-1beta, TNF-alpha, IL-6, COX-2 and PGE2 are connected to HIF-1, VEGF and other pro-angiogenic factors.

For EMT/metastasis, the review emphasizes neutrophils and monocytes, MMPs, cytokines, adhesion/vascular-permeability changes, and transcriptional pathways involving NF-kB, STAT3, ROS, PGE2, snail, slug, twist and ZEB-family factors. Figure 1 and Table 2 serve as the principal pathway maps.

Progression and recurrence

Inflammatory signaling is proposed to support progression by suppressing CTLs/NK cells/DCs, recruiting Tregs/MDSCs, increasing proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. STAT3 and NF-kB are emphasized as central regulators of survival, cell-cycle and inflammatory gene programs.

For recurrence, CSCs are described as escaping chemotherapy through ABC transporters, ALDH activity, anti-apoptotic programs, high DNA-repair capacity, stemness pathways and G0 latency. NF-kB, STAT3 and PGE2 may then reinforce CSC stemness and survival, while inflammatory recruitment of MDSCs suppresses antitumor immunity. Figure 2 integrates these mechanisms into the review's relapse hypothesis.

Adaptive immunity and chemo-immunotherapy

The authors emphasize dose, drug and schedule dependence. Maximum tolerated doses may cause lymphocyte/myeloid suppression, whereas lower doses can reduce inhibitory cells and enhance effectors. Low-dose cyclophosphamide is used as an example; several drug classes are also described as capable of immunogenic cell death through calreticulin exposure, ATP release, inflammasome activation and other danger signals.

The review further notes that several chemotherapy agents can increase PD-L1 expression on tumor cells and suggests combining them with anti-PD-L1 therapy. This is a source claim and mechanistic rationale, not comparative clinical-effect evidence presented by the review itself.

Concluding remarks

The authors hypothesize that chemotherapy-induced chronic inflammation may contribute to treatment inefficiency and recurrence. They propose that simultaneous anti-inflammatory therapy could improve some cancer-treatment outcomes, while explicitly stating that the idea requires additional in vitro, in vivo, preclinical and clinical validation.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best use: mechanistic orientation, hypothesis generation, and identifying primary studies/pathways for follow-up. Do not use this review alone to add anti-inflammatory therapy to chemotherapy, change cytotoxic dosing, or infer patient-level benefit from the proposed pathway model.

Relevance 8/10

Rationale: Directly relevant to chemotherapy, inflammation, immune effects and tumor microenvironment biology.

Evidence basis: Broad mechanistic synthesis spanning commonly used cytotoxic agents.

Principal limit: The review is not organized around a specific clinical decision.

Would raise the score: Direct linkage to validated patient-centered outcomes or a defined clinical question.

Novelty 6/10

Rationale: The integrative "double-edged sword" framing and anti-inflammatory adjunct hypothesis are useful, but most component mechanisms derive from prior literature.

Evidence basis: Cross-links chemotherapy-induced inflammation, CSC persistence, recurrence and immunotherapy.

Principal limit: Synthesis rather than new empirical data.

Would raise the score: Prospective evidence testing the integrated causal model.

Method strength 3/10

Rationale: The article is a narrative review without a reproducible search or study-selection method.

Evidence basis: Extensive citation base and mechanistic organization.

Principal limit: No databases/search dates, eligibility criteria, risk-of-bias process or evidence grading are reported.

Would raise the score: A prespecified systematic search, transparent selection, risk-of-bias assessment and evidence-certainty framework.

Evidence strength 4/10

Rationale: The central hypothesis is biologically plausible and broadly cited but rests on heterogeneous evidence types without pooled or formally graded certainty.

Evidence basis: Multiple mechanistic pathways and drug examples are supported by cited literature.

Principal limit: In vitro, animal, clinical and review evidence are blended; causal and clinical-effect magnitudes are not synthesized consistently.

Would raise the score: Direct prospective clinical evidence showing that chemotherapy-induced inflammation causally worsens outcomes and that modifying it improves outcomes.

External validity 5/10

Rationale: The breadth across drugs and cancer pathways suggests broad relevance, but heterogeneity prevents assuming a uniform effect across regimens, doses, tumors or hosts.

Evidence basis: Multiple chemotherapy classes and cancer contexts are represented.

Principal limit: Context dependence of inflammation and immune effects is substantial.

Would raise the score: Stratified clinical evidence across cancers, regimens, doses and patient populations.

Implementation value 4/10

Rationale: The review is useful for hypothesis generation and mechanistic mapping but does not justify altering chemotherapy or adding anti-inflammatory therapy in practice.

Evidence basis: Identifies candidate pathways, drug classes and research targets.

Principal limit: No validated clinical protocol, dosing strategy, safety framework or outcome trial supports the adjunctive anti-inflammatory proposal.

Would raise the score: Randomized or strong prospective clinical trials plus regimen-specific implementation guidance and safety data.

Principal methodological limitation: no reproducible review method is reported. The article does not provide databases, search dates, search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, study-selection flow, risk-of-bias assessment, evidence grading, or quantitative synthesis. That makes completeness and selection bias difficult to judge.
Evidence-maturity caution: the review often moves from mechanistic studies to clinical hypotheses. Biological plausibility does not establish that suppressing inflammation during chemotherapy improves cancer outcomes; inflammation may also contribute to antitumor immunity, and chemotherapy immune effects are dose/regimen dependent.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use the review as a pathway map to identify chemotherapy-associated inflammatory mechanisms and candidate primary references.

Do not overclaim

Do not infer that anti-inflammatory co-therapy is beneficial, safe, or indicated from this article alone.

Best next evidence

Prioritize the cited primary studies testing drug-specific inflammatory effects, then clinical studies linking inflammatory biomarkers to recurrence or treatment outcomes.

Research translation

A defensible clinical test of the hypothesis would need regimen-specific exposure, inflammatory biomarkers, tumor/immune phenotyping, patient-centered outcomes, and explicit safety assessment.
