One-page synthesis

Core thesis: The institutional anti-Xa-guided UFH VTE protocol was very likely to produce at least one therapeutic anti-Xa value within 48 hours, but therapeutic control was unstable over time. The study therefore supports a distinction between ever therapeutic and consistently therapeutic.

Among 205 adults with DVT and/or PE who remained on the institutional UFH protocol for 48 consecutive hours, 200 (97.6%) achieved at least one anti-Xa value in the institutional therapeutic range of 0.3–0.7 units/mL. Median time to the first therapeutic value was 12.8 hours. However, no more than 60% of patients were therapeutic at any individual 6-hour interval, only 76.1% achieved two consecutive therapeutic values by 48 hours, and just 13.7% had all measured values therapeutic during the 48-hour period.

The bolus-plus-infusion and infusion-only cohorts did not differ significantly for the primary endpoint, time to first therapeutic anti-Xa, mortality, or the principal 24/48-hour anti-Xa control measures. The statistically shorter length of stay in the bolus cohort (median 10 vs 16 days) is not persuasive evidence of a bolus benefit because bolus selection was nonrandom, reasons for withholding the bolus were undocumented, and the infusion-only cohort was older and had lower baseline hemoglobin. The study itself acknowledges that patient-oriented outcomes were not the main focus and that the monitoring endpoints have limited direct decision-making value.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors evaluated whether an institutional UFH VTE dosing protocol monitored by anti-Xa could achieve and maintain therapeutic anticoagulation during the first 48 hours. In 205 patients, 97.6% achieved at least one therapeutic anti-Xa level; the median time to first therapeutic level was 12.8 hours; neither measure differed significantly between bolus-plus-infusion and infusion-only cohorts.

The abstract correctly foreshadows the central tension: high probability of reaching at least one therapeutic value but lower consistency of maintaining therapeutic values. The authors call for alternative dosing-strategy studies that include patient-oriented outcomes.

Introduction

The rationale is based on UFH response variability and the need for laboratory-guided titration. The institution uses anti-Xa monitoring with a therapeutic range of 0.3–0.7 units/mL. Prior work using aPTT suggested that many patients with PE do not remain therapeutic during the first 48 hours, and the authors identify a gap in anti-Xa-based assessment of their own protocol.

The introduction also links the study to weight-based UFH dosing literature but does not establish that anti-Xa monitoring itself improves clinical outcomes. Those background claims are cited from prior sources rather than generated by this study.

Methods — design and population

This was a single-center retrospective chart review using a data repository and electronic medical record. Adults with confirmed DVT and/or PE had to receive the institutional VTE heparin protocol for 48 consecutive hours and have at least one anti-Xa level. Extensive exclusions attempted to reduce confounding from recent anticoagulants, thrombolysis, coagulopathies, outside-hospital UFH initiation, and unconfirmed/superficial thrombosis.

The analytic cohorts were defined by whether the patient received an initial UFH bolus. This was not a randomized treatment assignment; providers could omit the bolus based on patient factors such as bleeding risk, and the reason for omission was not required to be documented.

Methods — protocol and monitoring

The starting infusion was weight-banded and approximated 18 units/kg/h with a maximum initial rate of 1,600 units/h. The optional initial bolus approximated 80 units/kg with a maximum of 8,000 units. Subsequent rate and bolus adjustments were determined by anti-Xa and a 70-kg threshold. Levels were obtained every 6 hours until two consecutive therapeutic values, then daily.

Operationally, bedside nurses performed protocol-driven titration. Rate changes or holds generated a pharmacist verification order, and pharmacists checked that the adjustment matched the protocol and contacted nursing when correction was needed. For analysis, anti-Xa values were assigned to 6-hour windows around nominal time points.

Methods — outcomes and statistics

The primary endpoint was at least one therapeutic anti-Xa level within 48 hours. Secondary endpoints included interval-specific therapeutic status, time to first therapeutic value, mortality, hospital length of stay, sub-/supratherapeutic exposure over 24 and 48 hours, and exploratory patient-factor analyses.

The paper reports Student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum testing for continuous comparisons as appropriate and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, with a 5% significance threshold. Analyses were conducted in R 3.6.3. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed.

Results

Of 8,376 patients with active heparin infusion orders, 205 met criteria. The bolus-plus-infusion cohort contained 89 patients and the infusion-only cohort 116. Median age was 63 years; the bolus cohort was younger (61 vs 65 years, p=0.033) and had higher baseline hemoglobin (10.5 vs 9.7 g/dL, p=0.039). Other listed baseline characteristics were not significantly different.

The primary endpoint occurred in 200/205 patients (97.6%), with no cohort difference (96.6% vs 98.3%, p=0.65). Median time to first therapeutic anti-Xa was 12.8 hours overall (13.3 vs 12.7 hours, p=0.48). Only 13.7% of patients had all measured anti-Xa levels therapeutic across the first 48 hours; 66.8% had at least one subtherapeutic value and 31.7% at least one supratherapeutic value. Post hoc analysis found older patients (≥65 years) reached the first therapeutic anti-Xa sooner than younger patients (12.1 vs 14.0 hours, p=0.014); other examined factors were not significant.

Discussion

The authors interpret the 97.6% primary-endpoint rate as evidence that the protocol is effective at achieving at least one therapeutic anti-Xa, while emphasizing that maintenance was poor: only 40–60% of patients were therapeutic at individual 6-hour intervals and only 13.7% remained therapeutic across all measured values. They contrast this with prior aPTT-based findings but acknowledge protocol and assay differences.

The only statistically significant between-cohort clinical utilization difference was length of stay, shorter in the bolus cohort. The paper appropriately cautions that the bolus cohort was younger and had higher hemoglobin, making confounding plausible. The discussion also notes that only five patients never achieved a therapeutic level, preventing meaningful analysis of failure predictors.

Limitations

Major limitations include retrospective single-center design, modest sample size, no a priori power calculation, possible inconsistencies from multiple reviewers/data collectors, decreasing anti-Xa sampling over time, absence of measured protocol compliance, uneven numbers of anti-Xa observations across time windows, and potential selection bias from the large ICU subset.

For the bolus comparison specifically, the reason for withholding the initial bolus was not documented. This is a high-impact limitation because clinicians could choose no bolus based on perceived bleeding risk, which makes the two cohorts clinically nonexchangeable. The study also focused on monitoring-oriented endpoints and did not establish a patient-centered benefit from achieving or maintaining the anti-Xa target.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that most patients reached at least one therapeutic anti-Xa level within 48 hours but fewer maintained therapeutic values consistently. They call for further research on alternative dosing strategies and patient-oriented outcomes.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin protocol / anti-Xa deep dive. The paper is operationally useful because it exposes an institutional nomogram and quantifies how “ever therapeutic” can overstate stability. It is not strong evidence for bolus-versus-no-bolus effectiveness or for practice change.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses UFH VTE dosing, anti-Xa monitoring, time to target, protocol titration, and pharmacist verification.

Evidence basis: full dosing table plus 24/48-hour anti-Xa outcomes. Limiter: one institution. Would raise: multicenter replication in contemporary VTE pathways.

Novelty
6/10

Useful application of anti-Xa monitoring to a protocol-performance question and, per the authors, among the first to focus on maintenance across the first 48 hours.

Limiter: fundamentally descriptive protocol audit. Would raise: prospectively tested dosing strategy or validated optimization model.

Method strength
4/10

Clear eligibility, explicit protocol, and transparent tables, but retrospective single-center design and nonrandom cohort formation limit causal inference.

Limiter: undocumented reason for bolus omission, no a priori power calculation, variable sampling. Would raise: prospective/randomized design, prespecified analysis, adherence measurement.

Evidence strength
5/10

The descriptive anti-Xa findings are well supported by reported denominators and repeated time-window data.

Limiter: monitoring/surrogate outcomes dominate, bleeding was not a dedicated reported outcome, and patient-centered effects are unresolved. Would raise: clinical endpoints with adjusted or randomized comparisons.

External validity
4/10

The exact nomogram is institution-specific, including weight bands, rounded rates, 70-kg titration threshold, and pharmacist workflow.

Limiter: single-center population and local operational choices. Would raise: replication across diverse hospitals and protocol variants.

Implementation value
8/10

High operational value: starting doses, titration rules, monitoring cadence, anti-Xa categories, and pharmacist verification are all explicit.

Limiter: the study cannot tell a site that adopting these exact rules will improve clinical outcomes. Would raise: implementation study linking protocol fidelity and patient outcomes.

Validity

  • Selection/confounding: provider-selected bolus omission and different baseline age/hemoglobin undermine cohort comparability.
  • Measurement: anti-Xa windows had changing denominators and declining sampling frequency; protocol adherence was not directly measured.
  • Statistical: no a priori power calculation; multiple secondary/post hoc comparisons; only five primary-endpoint failures prevented robust predictor analysis.
  • Outcome hierarchy: reaching an anti-Xa target is a surrogate/monitoring outcome. Mortality was reported but the study was not designed to detect clinical benefit.
  • Generalizability: local dose rounding and a 70-kg titration threshold are not necessarily transferable to other protocols.

What the source supports

It supports the claim that, under this specific protocol and study selection criteria, most included patients had at least one therapeutic anti-Xa within 48 hours, while sustained therapeutic control was substantially less consistent.

What the source does not support

It does not support causal claims that omitting or giving the initial bolus changes outcomes, that this protocol is superior to other UFH nomograms, that anti-Xa is clinically superior to aPTT, or that achieving the target improves patient-centered outcomes in this cohort.

Practice / implementation takeaways

1. Separate attainment from maintenance“At least one therapeutic level” can look excellent (97.6%) while continuous control remains poor (13.7% all therapeutic).
2. Preserve denominator-by-time reportingInterval denominators fell substantially. Any dashboard or protocol audit should show n alongside percentages at each time point.
3. Capture bolus-omission rationaleIf a protocol allows an initial bolus to be withheld, documenting why is necessary for meaningful comparative evaluation and safety review.
4. Measure protocol fidelityAnti-Xa control cannot be interpreted cleanly without knowing whether scheduled levels and titration actions were executed according to protocol.
5. Keep pharmacist verification auditableThe nurse-titration / pharmacist-verification workflow is a practical safety architecture worth preserving as a traceable operational element.
6. Do not overread length of stayThe 10-vs-16-day difference is plausibly confounded by patient selection and baseline differences; it should not be treated as a bolus effect.
Use now? Use as an operational benchmark and protocol-design reference. Do not use as stand-alone evidence to alter bolus strategy or claim patient-outcome superiority.
