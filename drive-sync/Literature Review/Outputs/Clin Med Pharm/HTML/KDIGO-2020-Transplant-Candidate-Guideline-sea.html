One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Kidney transplant candidacy should be judged by the candidate's total modifiable and nonmodifiable risk burden rather than by isolated demographic labels or single comorbidities. The guideline generally favors access to transplantation when expected benefit remains acceptable, while using targeted stabilization, specialist review, and disease-specific risk mitigation before listing or surgery.

The guideline is broad. It covers access and referral timing, age and pediatric issues, psychosocial risk, adherence, tobacco, obesity and surgical anatomy, diabetes, recurrent kidney disease, infections, malignancy, pulmonary and cardiovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease, neurologic disease, gastrointestinal and liver disease, hematologic disorders, bone and mineral metabolism, and immunologic assessment. Its strongest access message is that age, obesity, diabetes, many recurrent kidney diseases, anticoagulation needs, and several chronic comorbidities should not be used as automatic exclusions. In contrast, active or unstable disease, severe irreversible organ dysfunction, certain untreated or active malignancies, uncontrolled infection, and selected progressive conditions should delay or preclude transplantation.

The evidence base is heterogeneous. The evidence-profile supplement shows high-certainty support for some questions, such as transplantation benefit across age strata and pre-transplant glucose tolerance testing, but many screening and risk-prediction questions are low or very-low certainty. The transplant-versus-waitlist profile concludes that transplantation is generally superior regardless of age or obesity, while the pre-emptive-transplantation registry profile remains very-low certainty because results change with the comparator. Psychosocial testing, nonadherence, TB testing, cancer screening, intracranial imaging in ADPKD, and several recurrence questions are supported mainly by sparse or indirect evidence.

The guideline is unusually transparent about its own methods. Appendix A provides detailed search strategies. Appendix B explicitly lists IOM standards that were and were not met. Important gaps include no analytic framework or standard clinical-question format, no peer-reviewed or public final systematic-review protocol, no independent search-strategy peer review, one primary data extractor with a second checker rather than full independent dual extraction, and incomplete conflict-of-interest and patient-participation safeguards by the IOM checklist.

Best use: foundational transplant-candidate evaluation framework, checklist design, protocol architecture, and historical rationale for multidisciplinary risk assessment. Use with caution: exact waiting periods, cancer rules, infectious-disease testing, cardiovascular screening, DOAC handling, and any rapidly evolving practice area should be checked against newer evidence and current local or national guidance before implementation.

Selected load-bearing findings

  • Transplantation versus waitlist: the profile reports transplantation as generally superior across age groups and obesity. Age evidence was rated high; obesity evidence was low; the HIV-positive comparison was very low certainty.
  • Pre-emptive transplantation: two registry studies with 26,503 patients produced conflicting results because the comparator differed; overall certainty was very low.
  • Mortality predictors: increasing age, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and peripheral vascular disease showed associations with post-transplant mortality, but certainty varied substantially by predictor.
  • Graft-loss predictors: morbid obesity and hypoalbuminemia showed signals in some analyses, while age and kidney-disease-specific associations were less consistent.
  • Glucose tolerance testing: pre-transplant impaired glucose tolerance or fasting glucose predicted new-onset diabetes after transplantation; this profile was rated high certainty.
  • Cardiac assessment: evidence for pre-transplant revascularization and echocardiography was low certainty and primarily observational, supporting risk stratification more than proof that screening or intervention improves transplant outcomes.

Methods and evidence-development appraisal

What KDIGO reports

  • Brown University's Center for Evidence Synthesis in Health served as the Evidence Review Team.
  • Guideline development included topic selection, literature searching, study screening, data extraction, critical appraisal, evidence profiles, GRADE assessment, recommendation development, and public review.
  • Figure 1 reports 45,914 citations from 32 searches, 762 full-text articles retrieved, and 190 included studies.
  • Appendix A provides explicit MEDLINE and EMBASE syntax for the review topics, supporting reproducibility of the historical searches.
  • Graded recommendations use Level 1 or Level 2 strength and A-D evidence quality. Not Graded guidance is used when formal grading was not applied.

What Appendix B says was not fully met

  • No analytic framework and no standard-format clinical questions for the systematic review.
  • No peer review or public release of the final systematic-review protocol.
  • No independent information-specialist peer review of the search strategy; cited-study reference-list searching and web searching were not part of the stated process.
  • One reviewer extracted data and a second checked it, rather than fully independent extraction by two or more reviewers for all quantitative and critical data.
  • Some qualitative-synthesis and reporting standards were not met, including explicit discussion of how study flaws might bias results and rationale for pooling or not pooling results.
  • Several IOM conflict-of-interest standards were not fully met, including explicit intellectual-bias handling, limits on the proportion of conflicted guideline members, and chair restrictions.
  • Patient/public involvement was present mainly through public comment on the scope and guideline; Appendix B records no dedicated patient/consumer training strategy.

Interpretive bottom line

The 2020 guideline remains a strong map of how transplant programs can structure candidacy evaluation. Its most durable contribution is architectural: do not convert a single risk factor into an automatic denial when a broader benefit-risk assessment and risk-mitigation plan is possible. The main limitation is not lack of breadth but uneven direct evidence for many operational screening and exclusion decisions. The evidence supplement makes that limitation visible, with many profiles rated low or very low certainty.

Practice and implementation takeaways

  • Use the guideline as a candidate-evaluation checklist architecture, not as a single binary eligibility rule.
  • Separate conditions that permanently preclude kidney-alone transplantation from conditions that should delay evaluation until treated or stabilized.
  • Preserve multidisciplinary review for psychosocial, adherence, cardiovascular, pulmonary, oncologic, infectious, vascular, neurologic, hematologic, and immunologic risks.
  • When translating the guideline into CDS or protocol content, store the source recommendation identifier, grade, conditions, exceptions, and source version together. Several statements are repeated across sections, so deduplication should preserve cross-references.
  • Treat cancer waiting times, infection screening, cardiovascular testing, perioperative anticoagulation, and other fast-moving domains as version-sensitive content requiring a current-source check before clinical deployment.
