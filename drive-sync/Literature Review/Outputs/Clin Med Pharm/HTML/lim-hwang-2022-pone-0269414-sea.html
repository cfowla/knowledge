Summary, Evaluation, and Appraisal

Comparison of cardiovascular and renal outcomes between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin in patients with type 2 diabetes without prior cardiovascular or renal disease

Scope warningThis study estimates observational associations. It does not establish therapeutic equivalence, noninferiority, inpatient interchangeability, or causal superiority between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin.

One-page synthesis

Core result. Both SGLT2 inhibitor groups had lower adjusted risks than DPP-4 inhibitor controls for several cardiorenal outcomes. Direct dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin comparisons were not statistically significant, but the confidence intervals were wide because events were uncommon.

The paper addresses a useful formulary question because it compares dapagliflozin and empagliflozin in the same real-world cohort. It matched 921 patients receiving each SGLT2 inhibitor to 1,842 DPP-4 inhibitor controls. The study excluded prior ASCVD, heart failure, and substantial CKD, so it mainly tests a lower-risk diabetes population. Median follow-up differed by group, about 37 months for each SGLT2 inhibitor and 55 months for control.

Dapagliflozin and empagliflozin each had lower adjusted hazards than control for composite coronary events, HHF, and the combined HHF/renal endpoint. Dapagliflozin also had a statistically significant lower composite ischemic hazard versus control, while empagliflozin did not. None of the direct dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin clinical-outcome comparisons reached statistical significance. The direct estimates were too imprecise to prove equivalence. This paper supports a class-effect argument as one piece of evidence, not as a stand-alone interchangeability study.

Most important limitation. Very few events occurred in the SGLT2 inhibitor groups. For renal events there were only two events in each SGLT2 inhibitor group. The direct renal comparison therefore produced an extreme HR of 0.045 with a 95% CI from 0.000 to 20.370. A non-significant result here is not evidence that the drugs are equivalent.

Methods

Study population and exposure

The investigators retrospectively identified patients prescribed dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, or a DPP-4 inhibitor. They excluded type 1 diabetes, prior heart failure, prior ASCVD, GFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, exposure shorter than three months, medication possession ratio below 75%, and simultaneous or sequential use of both SGLT2 inhibitors. The index date was medication initiation.

Matching and analysis

Nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching used a 1:1:2 ratio with age, sex, 10-year ASCVD risk, systolic blood pressure, albuminuria or proteinuria, HbA1c, GFR, antiplatelet use, renin-angiotensin system blocker use, and statin use. Cox proportional-hazards models produced adjusted HRs and 95% CIs. The study used stepwise backward multivariable modeling after univariable screening at p<0.200.

Outcomes

Composite coronary events included acute coronary syndrome and coronary revascularization. Composite ischemic events added stroke. Renal events included renal death, renal replacement therapy initiation, and admission for acute kidney injury or CKD progression. The study also assessed HHF, the combined HHF/renal endpoint, and serial total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HbA1c, and GFR.

Results

Matched cohort

Baseline characteristics were similar after matching. Median age was 56 to 57 years, about two-thirds were men, median HbA1c was about 8%, median GFR was about 97 mL/min/1.73 m2, and albuminuria or proteinuria was present in roughly 35%.

Atherosclerotic outcomes

For composite coronary events, adjusted HRs versus control were 0.267 for dapagliflozin and 0.467 for empagliflozin. The direct empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin HR was 2.196, 95% CI 0.742 to 6.502, p=0.156. For composite ischemic events, dapagliflozin versus control was significant at HR 0.458, while empagliflozin versus control was HR 0.607 with p=0.102. The direct comparison was HR 1.434, 95% CI 0.631 to 3.258, p=0.389.

HF and renal outcomes

HHF occurred in 3 dapagliflozin, 6 empagliflozin, and 40 control patients. Renal events occurred in 2, 2, and 30 patients. The combined HHF/renal endpoint occurred in 4, 8, and 64 patients. Both SGLT2 inhibitors had statistically significant lower adjusted HHF and combined HHF/renal hazards versus control. Atrial fibrillation strongly predicted HHF, while lower GFR and albuminuria or proteinuria predicted renal outcomes.

Laboratory findings

Total cholesterol did not differ over follow-up. At 24 and 36 months, empagliflozin had higher LDL cholesterol and lower HbA1c than the other groups. GFR was better preserved in both SGLT2 inhibitor groups versus control at later time points.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. It is directly relevant to a dapagliflozin-versus-empagliflozin formulary rationale, but its observational design and sparse direct-comparison events limit what it can prove.

Relevance 9/10

Direct dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin evidence is highly relevant to an SGLT2 formulary class-effect question.

Evidence: Matched real-world head-to-head cohort with DPP-4 inhibitor control.

Limit: It does not test inpatient interchangeability.

Would change: A prospective randomized or prespecified comparative-effectiveness study would raise confidence.

Novelty 6/10

At publication, direct comparative outcome data between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin were limited.

Evidence: The study directly compares both SGLT2 inhibitors in a primary-prevention-like cohort.

Limit: The design is retrospective and builds on established class-effect evidence.

Would change: Prospective head-to-head evidence with prespecified hypotheses would raise the score.

Method strength 5/10

Propensity-score matching and Cox models improve comparability but do not remove observational bias.

Evidence: 1:1:2 matching used clinical, laboratory, and medication covariates; outcomes were analyzed with adjusted hazard models.

Limit: Single-center retrospective design, residual confounding, unequal follow-up, and few events.

Would change: Prospective allocation, stronger confounding control, and balanced follow-up would raise the score.

Evidence strength 5/10

The SGLT2 inhibitor versus DPP-4 inhibitor associations are consistent, but direct drug-to-drug estimates are imprecise.

Evidence: Adjusted HRs favor both SGLT2 inhibitors for several HF and renal outcomes.

Limit: Wide direct-comparison confidence intervals and low event counts cannot establish equivalence or noninferiority.

Would change: A powered head-to-head trial or large active-comparator target-trial emulation would raise the score.

External validity 5/10

The matched cohort is clinically recognizable but narrow.

Evidence: Patients had type 2 diabetes without prior ASCVD, HF, or CKD and came from routine care.

Limit: Single Korean tertiary center, adherence and duration exclusions, and low-risk selection limit generalization.

Would change: Replication across health systems, regions, risk strata, and kidney function would raise the score.

Implementation value 6/10

The paper supports a class-effect argument but should remain supportive rather than decisive for formulary substitution.

Evidence: Both SGLT2 inhibitors showed similar directional cardiorenal associations versus DPP-4 inhibitors.

Limit: It does not address inpatient initiation, acute illness, dose substitution, or therapeutic equivalence.

Would change: Direct pragmatic comparative-effectiveness data tied to formulary use would raise the score.

Bias and validity

Propensity matching reduces measured baseline imbalance but cannot remove unmeasured confounding. Follow-up was substantially longer in the control group. Laboratory sampling was not prospectively scheduled. Event counts were low, particularly for direct drug comparisons, so confidence intervals are wide. The cohort was selected for at least three months of medication use and at least 75% medication possession, which may favor adherent survivors and narrows applicability.

Claim discipline

The observed similarity between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin is compatible with a class effect. It is not a test of equivalence. The authors' class-effect conclusion should be read as an interpretation of non-significant, imprecise head-to-head estimates plus similar comparisons against control.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Best use. Supportive real-world evidence that dapagliflozin and empagliflozin show similar directional cardiorenal associations in a matched lower-risk type 2 diabetes cohort.

Do not use for. Proving therapeutic equivalence, dose interchangeability, inpatient initiation safety, or superiority of one drug over the other.

For a formulary rationale. Pair this study with randomized outcomes evidence, higher-quality indirect or direct comparative evidence, guideline and label considerations, and operational factors. This source alone is too imprecise for a one-drug-is-equivalent conclusion.
