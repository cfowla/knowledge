Scope warning: This artifact summarizes and appraises the supplied 2021 review. It is not a current guideline and should not be used alone to select glutamine or arginine therapy.

One-page synthesis

The review asks whether arginine or glutamine supplementation improves wound healing or related physiologic and clinical outcomes. The authors report five arginine studies and 39 glutamine studies in the quantitative evidence base. Arginine pooling is limited mainly to hydroxyproline/collagen deposition; glutamine pooling covers nitrogen balance, length of stay, mortality, intestinal permeability, CRP, IL-6, TNF-alpha, and T-cell lymphocytes.

The most clinically direct pooled result is mortality: 46/340 deaths with glutamine versus 89/356 controls, OR 0.48 (95% CI 0.32–0.72; p=0.0004; I²=14%), with an NNT of 10 reported by the paper. Other statistically significant pooled effects include LOS -2.65 days (95% CI -3.10 to -2.21; I²=84%), L/M ratio -0.01 (I²=97%), CRP -1.10 (I²=82%), IL-6 -5.78 (I²=74%), TNF-alpha -8.15 (I²=97%), and nitrogen balance SMD 0.39 (I²=91%). T-cell lymphocytes were not significant, SMD -0.16 (95% CI -0.33 to 0.01; p=0.07; I²=93%).

Critical interpretation constraint: very high heterogeneity affects most pooled outcomes, and the paper has several internal reporting inconsistencies. The abstract gives an arginine hydroxyproline CI of 3.54–4.45, whereas Results and Figure 8 give 3.54–5.45. Figures 9 and 15 label effects as standardized mean differences while prose calls them MD. Figure 15 totals 627 participants (315+312), whereas prose states 540. The abstract says ten electronic databases, while the Methods names six search sources.

Methods and design

Search and eligibility

The authors state that the review followed PRISMA and PICO. The Methods names PubMed, American Physiological Society Publications, Taylor & Francis Online, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Google Scholar grey literature, with searches for papers published before October 2020 and reference-list checking. One researcher extracted data and another cross-checked it. Eligible studies were RCTs in adults older than 18 years with acute or chronic wounds receiving arginine or glutamine. The planned intervention section specifies at least five days of supplementation; comparators were placebo or no treatment.

Extraction, bias, and meta-analysis

Data were taken from figures (using WebPlotDigitizer), tables, and text. The paper reports conversion of units and distribution summaries as necessary. Cochrane risk-of-bias domains were assessed. Review Manager 5.4.1 was used with fixed-effects pooling, 95% CIs, mean differences or odds ratios; nitrogen balance and T-cell outcomes were stated to use standardized mean differences. The authors considered I² >50% significant heterogeneity and treated multiple-dose results from the same study as independent comparisons.

Methodological concern: fixed-effect pooling is retained even for outcomes with I² of 74–99%, and no publication-bias/GRADE certainty framework is reported in the supplied source. Treating multiple-dose comparisons as independent can also understate dependence when arms share participants.

Authors’ limitations

The paper states that only five arginine studies were included, arginine nitrogen-balance and T-cell effects could not be meta-analyzed because too few studies reported them, sample sizes were relatively small, and high heterogeneity for both amino acids may have affected results.

Authors’ conclusion

The authors conclude that arginine or glutamine supplementation may positively influence wound healing or related parameters. This conclusion should be read with the heterogeneity and reporting constraints above rather than as proof of a single effective regimen.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Do not use for practice as a standalone source. Best use: background evidence map and hypothesis/context generation. Read the underlying trials or a current guideline before changing clinical practice.

Relevance : 7/10

Directly addresses amino-acid supplementation and wound-healing-related outcomes.

Evidence basis: Includes clinical and surrogate outcomes across arginine/glutamine trials.

Principal limiting factor: Mixed populations/outcomes and an older evidence cutoff.

Would improve with: A focused, updated review with prespecified clinically important wound outcomes.

Novelty : 5/10

Provides a focused synthesis of arginine and glutamine rather than a single-condition review.

Evidence basis: Combines mechanistic framing, trial tables, and pooled analyses.

Principal limiting factor: Many underlying trials and prior reviews predate this 2021 synthesis.

Would improve with: New high-quality trials or an updated network of current evidence.

Method strength : 4/10

Uses PRISMA/PICO and Cochrane risk-of-bias methods, but several analytic/reporting choices weaken confidence.

Evidence basis: Explicit eligibility, extraction, risk-of-bias domains, and meta-analysis methods.

Principal limiting factor: Fixed-effect pooling despite I² often 74–99%, multiple-dose results treated as independent comparisons, unclear protocol/registration, and internal reporting inconsistencies.

Would improve with: Prespecified protocol, duplicate independent extraction, random-effects/sensitivity analyses, dependency handling, publication-bias assessment, and corrected flow/reporting.

Evidence strength : 4/10

Several pooled effects are statistically significant, but heterogeneity and source-integrity inconsistencies are substantial.

Evidence basis: Mortality OR 0.48 with I²=14%; multiple other pooled estimates with CIs.

Principal limiting factor: Most other pooled outcomes have very high heterogeneity; arginine evidence is sparse; many outcomes are surrogate/intermediate.

Would improve with: Consistent effects in modern, well-powered RCTs with clinically meaningful wound endpoints and low heterogeneity.

External validity : 4/10

Evidence spans surgery, burns, critical illness, and pressure ulcers, but regimen and population diversity limits transportability.

Evidence basis: Broad clinical populations and multiple administration routes/doses.

Principal limiting factor: Heterogeneous populations, doses, routes, durations, and nutrition contexts.

Would improve with: Stratified analyses demonstrating reproducible benefit in defined populations/regimens.

Implementation value : 3/10

The review is useful as an evidence map but does not support a single actionable dosing or practice rule.

Evidence basis: Tables summarize trial doses/durations and discussion addresses route/timing.

Principal limiting factor: No robust regimen-specific certainty; conclusion refers generally to the “right dose”; no current guideline integration.

Would improve with: Current guideline concordance plus regimen-specific benefit/risk evidence.

Principal strengths

Explicit eligibility and bias-assessment framework; trial-level dose/population tables; multiple clinically relevant outcomes; forest plots with confidence intervals; mortality analysis has relatively low heterogeneity.

Principal limitations

Small arginine evidence base; high heterogeneity for most pooled outcomes; fixed-effect model despite that heterogeneity; multiple-dose comparisons treated independently; no clear GRADE/publication-bias assessment in the supplied source; internal inconsistencies in search counts, flow diagrams, CI reporting, effect-measure labels, and Figure 15 denominators.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What this source can support: amino-acid supplementation has biologic plausibility and pooled signals across several wound-related and clinical measures, especially glutamine outcomes. What it cannot safely establish: a universally effective dose, route, duration, target population, or current standard-of-care recommendation.

For clinical use, verify current guidance and the specific underlying trials relevant to the patient population. Do not generalize the pooled mortality or LOS estimates across all critical-care/surgical populations without accounting for regimen, route, baseline nutrition, illness severity, and modern practice changes.
