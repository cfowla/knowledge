2. One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this small contemporaneous cohort of patients with confirmed VTE managed with an anti-Xa–based IV UFH protocol, COVID-19 positivity was not associated with a detectably higher UFH dose or longer time to cross anti-Xa 0.3 units/mL. Two COVID-19-positive patients had delayed anti-Xa attainment with UFH requirements above 50,000 units/day, so clinically meaningful resistance remained possible.

The paper is useful because it reframes reported “heparin resistance” in COVID-19 around the monitoring assay and the weight dependence of absolute daily-dose definitions. It also provides a clearly described anti-Xa workflow: optional 80 units/kg bolus, 18 units/kg/h starting infusion based on actual body weight, anti-Xa at 6 hours and every 6 hours until two consecutive therapeutic levels, then daily.

The main evidentiary limitation is structural: most patients crossed anti-Xa 0.3 on the first post-initiation level while starting at the same protocol dose, making the primary outcome strongly constrained by the protocol itself. Moreover, crossing ≥0.3 is not the same as being within the therapeutic range of 0.3–0.7; 64.1% of first levels were actually supratherapeutic. “No significant difference” in this small retrospective study should therefore be read as failure to detect a difference, not evidence of equivalence.

3. Clinical question / PICO

P — Population

Adults with radiographically confirmed VTE receiving IV UFH for at least 24 hours and with at least three anti-Xa levels. Exclusions included pregnancy, targeted temperature management, ECMO, and DOAC exposure before UFH initiation.

I/E — Exposure

COVID-19 positive patients treated using the institution's anti-Xa–monitored IV UFH VTE protocol.

C — Comparator

COVID-19 negative patients treated with IV UFH during the same timeframe and monitored with the same anti-Xa assay strategy.

O — Outcomes

Primary: UFH dose required to reach anti-Xa ≥0.3 units/mL. Secondary: time and adjustments to ≥0.3; dose, time and adjustments to therapeutic 0.3–0.7; high-dose UFH; clinically significant resistance; bleeding signals.

4. Section condensation

Background

The authors frame apparent heparin resistance in COVID-19 as potentially arising from inflammation, altered heparin binding/clearance, reduced antithrombin, or laboratory monitoring effects. Elevated factor VIII and fibrinogen can shorten aPTT relative to the true heparin effect while anti-Xa is less affected by those particular factors, creating “pseudo-resistance” when aPTT is used.

The study question is therefore narrower than “does COVID cause heparin resistance?”: among patients with confirmed VTE managed with anti-Xa monitoring, do COVID-positive patients require more IV UFH to achieve anticoagulation than COVID-negative patients?

Methods

This IRB-approved retrospective medication-use evaluation examined adults treated March–June 2020. The institutional VTE pathway used an optional 80 units/kg bolus and 18 units/kg/h initial infusion based on actual body weight; provider-discretion dose capping was possible. Anti-Xa was checked 6 hours after initiation and every 6 hours until two consecutive values were 0.3–0.7 units/mL, then daily. All anti-Xa testing used the same ACL TOP 750 CTS analyzer and HemosIL Liquid Anti-Xa reagent.

The primary endpoint was dose to anti-Xa ≥0.3 units/mL. Prespecified secondary endpoints separated this lower-bound threshold from attainment of the full 0.3–0.7 range. Patients still below 0.3 at 24 hours were evaluated for “clinically significant heparin resistance,” defined using the >35,000 units/day criterion plus delayed threshold attainment. Group comparisons used chi-square/Fisher exact and Mann–Whitney U tests.

Results

Of 321 screened patients, 64 met criteria. COVID-positive patients were substantially sicker: ICU admission/transfer 85% vs 52.3% (P=.014), mechanical ventilation 65% vs 31.8% (P=.013), acute kidney injury 25% vs 2.3% (P=.010), and median length of stay 18.5 vs 7 days (P=.006). Age, weight, BMI and loading-dose use did not differ significantly.

The primary dose was 18 units/kg/h in both groups. First anti-Xa ≥0.3 occurred at a median 6.13 vs 6.25 hours; 85% vs 90.9% reached ≥0.3 at the first level. Full-range therapeutic attainment took 21.1 vs 15.8 hours, without statistical significance. High daily UFH exposure >35,000 units occurred in 35% vs 36.4%. Two COVID-positive patients met the authors' clinically significant resistance definition; both required >50,000 units/day and took 28.23 and 53.62 hours to reach ≥0.3. Initial anti-Xa values were supratherapeutic in 64.1% overall.

Bleeding / safety

A hemoglobin decrease ≥2 g/dL was more frequent in COVID-positive patients (80% vs 47.7%, P=.028), while hemoglobin <7 g/dL did not differ (35% vs 29.5%, P=.633). One COVID-negative patient received protamine; no patient received ≥2 units of packed RBCs during or immediately after UFH.

Major bleeding and clinically relevant non-major bleeding could not be classified using full ISTH criteria. Because the COVID-positive group was also more critically ill and hospitalized longer, the hemoglobin-drop comparison is not a clean estimate of UFH-attributable bleeding.

Discussion

The authors argue that anti-Xa monitoring may reduce apparent heparin resistance caused by elevated factor VIII/fibrinogen and emphasize the need to interpret suspected resistance in light of the assay used. They also note that true resistance mechanisms—such as antithrombin deficiency, increased heparin binding or clearance—can lower both aPTT and anti-Xa and may require higher UFH doses or alternative anticoagulation.

A particularly important observation is that an absolute >35,000 units/day definition is weight-sensitive. In this cohort, 23 patients met that daily-dose threshold, yet most achieved anti-Xa promptly; their median weight was 99.1 kg. The authors therefore restricted “clinically significant” resistance to patients still below anti-Xa 0.3 at 24 hours, identifying two COVID-positive cases.

Limitations and conclusion

The authors acknowledge the small single-center sample and Type II error risk; selection of patients maintained on UFH ≥24 hours; lack of inflammatory-marker comparison in controls; first-wave-only COVID population; lack of evaluation of late maintenance-phase resistance; and the unvalidated, weight-dependent >35,000 units/day definition.

They conclude that COVID-positive and COVID-negative patients appeared to have similar UFH dose requirements and time to anticoagulation when monitored by anti-Xa, while clinically significant resistance could still occur. They call for further study of anti-Xa monitoring and a better definition of heparin resistance.

6. Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High topic relevance and a useful anti-Xa/heparin-resistance framing, but the study is too small and methodologically constrained to establish equivalence, monitoring superiority, or a reliable incidence of true resistance.
Relevance
9 / 10

Rationale: Directly addresses IV UFH dosing, anti-Xa monitoring, VTE and heparin resistance.

Evidence basis: confirmed VTE population and explicit UFH/anti-Xa protocol. Limiting factor: COVID-specific context. Would rise: applicability beyond COVID or direct monitoring-comparator data.

Novelty
6 / 10

Rationale: Adds a contemporaneous COVID-negative control to anti-Xa–monitored VTE patients and challenges crude daily-dose definitions.

Limiting factor: observational single-center question with modest sample. Would rise: prospective mechanistic or comparative monitoring design.

Method strength
5 / 10

Rationale: Clear eligibility, same assay/reagent, confirmed VTE, and contemporaneous controls are strengths.

Limiting factor: retrospective n=64, major severity imbalance, no multivariable adjustment, selection requiring ≥24 h UFH/≥3 levels, provider-discretion dose caps. Would rise: larger multicenter prospective design with adjustment.

Evidence strength
5 / 10

Rationale: Directly reports dose/time/anti-Xa outcomes, but the primary endpoint is protocol-constrained and statistical nonsignificance is not equivalence.

Limiting factor: no confidence intervals/equivalence margins, only two resistance cases, incomplete bleeding definition. Would rise: prespecified noninferiority/equivalence analysis and patient-centered safety outcomes.

External validity
3 / 10

Rationale: Single northeastern U.S. center during March–June 2020 with strict inclusion criteria and first-wave disease.

Limiting factor: variants, COVID severity, anticoagulation practices and patient mix may differ substantially elsewhere. Would rise: multicenter validation across eras and broader VTE populations.

Implementation value
6 / 10

Rationale: Provides a reproducible monitoring cadence and a useful diagnostic distinction between apparent and clinically significant resistance.

Limiting factor: no complete titration nomogram and no anti-Xa-vs-aPTT comparative effectiveness test. Would rise: validated algorithm linked to bleeding, thrombosis and workflow outcomes.

Validity: what the study does well

  • Confirmed treatment indication: inclusion required radiographically confirmed VTE, reducing contamination by empiric prophylactic/intermediate-dose COVID anticoagulation.
  • Contemporaneous control group: COVID-positive and negative patients were managed in the same timeframe, reducing concern about lot/potency-era differences in UFH.
  • Single assay platform: all anti-Xa analyses used the same analyzer and reagent.
  • DOAC exclusion: pre-UFH DOAC exposure was excluded specifically to avoid interference with the heparin anti-Xa assay.
  • Authors recognize the weight problem: they explicitly caution that >35,000 units/day can misclassify heavier patients as resistant.

Critical issues

1. Primary endpoint has limited discriminatory power. The starting infusion was 18 units/kg/h and 85–91% of patients crossed anti-Xa 0.3 at the first level. The median primary-outcome dose was therefore almost predetermined to be 18 units/kg/h in both groups. This design is poorly suited to detect subtler between-group pharmacodynamic differences.
2. Threshold attainment is not therapeutic-range attainment. Anti-Xa ≥0.3 includes values above 0.7; 64.1% of initial levels were supratherapeutic. The abstract's shorthand of “therapeutic anti-Xa” should therefore be interpreted cautiously when the reported primary endpoint is merely ≥0.3.
3. Nonsignificance does not show equivalence. The study was small, had only 20 COVID-positive patients, and reports no equivalence/noninferiority margin. Similar medians and P>.05 cannot establish that the groups have equivalent heparin requirements.
4. Severity confounding is substantial. COVID-positive patients had more ICU care, mechanical ventilation, AKI, higher peak creatinine, and longer hospitalization. These differences can affect anticoagulation exposure, sampling, bleeding and drug disposition, yet the analysis is unadjusted.
5. The study cannot prove anti-Xa is superior to aPTT. Every analyzed patient was managed using anti-Xa. The rationale for anti-Xa over aPTT is mechanistic and literature-based; monitoring strategies were not randomized or directly compared.
6. Bleeding ascertainment is weak. ISTH major and clinically relevant non-major bleeding could not be collected. Hemoglobin decline is nonspecific in a much sicker COVID cohort and is insufficient to characterize comparative UFH safety.

Appraiser calculation: why >35,000 U/day is weight-sensitive

At the protocol starting rate of 18 units/kg/h, the daily dose equals 432 units/kg/day. A patient weighing approximately 81 kg reaches 35,000 units/day without any dose escalation (35,000 ÷ 432 ≈ 81). This supports the authors' own concern that a fixed absolute daily-dose threshold can label routine weight-based dosing as “resistance” in larger patients. This calculation is derived from the study's reported starting rate; it is not a separately reported study result.

Practice translation

Use now? Maybe, as supportive operational evidence rather than practice-changing evidence. The study supports the feasibility of anti-Xa–based UFH titration in patients with COVID-associated VTE and gives a useful framework for investigating apparent resistance. It does not justify switching a monitoring system solely on its own, and it does not validate the modified heparin-resistance definition.

Clinical monitoring implication: when high UFH doses coexist with unexpectedly low aPTT, consider whether the finding reflects assay-related pseudo-resistance versus true low heparin effect. If anti-Xa is also low despite substantial UFH exposure, the source's discussion suggests evaluating true resistance mechanisms rather than simply assuming aPTT interference.

7. Implementation / practice takeaways

Heparin resistance
Avoid dose-only labeling.

A fixed >35,000 U/day threshold is heavily influenced by body weight and can overcall resistance in routinely weight-dosed patients.

Monitoring
Separate assay discordance from true low heparin effect.

Elevated factor VIII/fibrinogen can create aPTT-based pseudo-resistance; anti-Xa can help adjudicate this specific scenario.

Target definition
Distinguish ≥0.3 from 0.3–0.7.

Crossing the lower boundary is not equivalent to therapeutic-range control, especially when early supratherapeutic levels are common.

Protocol design
Audit both speed and overshoot.

A protocol that rapidly crosses 0.3 may still overshoot >0.7. Time-in-range, supratherapeutic exposure, dose adjustments and clinical events are more informative together.

Resistance workup
Use phenotype + mechanism.

Delayed anti-Xa response at high UFH exposure is more clinically persuasive than high absolute daily dose alone; consider antithrombin, binding/clearance factors and assay interference where appropriate.

Evidence use
Do not overgeneralize.

This first-wave, single-center COVID cohort should not define contemporary incidence, variant-specific behavior, or universal UFH monitoring policy.
