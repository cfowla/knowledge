Core thesis

The program demonstrates that routine ED HIV testing can be operationalized through a BPA while separating ED screening tasks from downstream HIV diagnosis disclosure and care coordination. The most transferable component is organizational: ED clinicians/nurses trigger and obtain screening, while ID nursing/social work/providers assume confirmatory-result handling, patient contact, barrier mitigation, and longitudinal care.

What the data support

Within this health system, the program identified 176 new HIV diagnoses among 75,914 tests, with two-thirds of new diagnoses attributed to the BPA alone rather than an STI, opportunistic infection, pregnancy, or other documented high-risk reason. Among the full cohort, 104/176 (59.1%) had any documented ID appointment within 90 days; among those 104, median time to initial appointment was 13 days. ART was documented in 107 patients with median initiation at 9 days.

What the data do not establish

The study does not establish the causal effect of the BPA or ED–ID collaboration because there is no linkage-to-care comparator before implementation and no alternative workflow control. Follow-up outside Henry Ford was incompletely captured, so linkage and viral-suppression estimates are vulnerable to missing-data bias. The article also contains internal numerical and statistical-method discrepancies that should be resolved before using exact percentages in secondary work.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction

The paper frames EDs as an important access point for patients who may not receive routine preventive care and argues that clinician time constraints, uncertainty about follow-up, discomfort with positive-result disclosure, and risk-based assumptions hinder routine HIV screening. Henry Ford's earlier targeted counseling/testing/referral model produced limited test volume, prompting a transition in 2019 to non-targeted opt-out screening embedded in the EMR.

The proposed implementation advantage is not the alert alone but the paired handoff model: reactive screening automatically triggers differentiation testing, and ID clinicians/nursing assume result disclosure, appointment arrangement, and continued engagement before the first outpatient visit. The study objective is descriptive—characterize the program and the patients newly diagnosed, then report linkage and treatment outcomes.

Methods — design and participants

Retrospective observational chart review at one urban Detroit ED with approximately 95,000 annual visits. Records from November 2019 through October 2025 were reviewed. All newly diagnosed HIV infections identified from ED screening were included, whether testing arose from the BPA or another reason such as pregnancy, STI evaluation, opportunistic infection, or high-risk features. IRB approval was obtained with waiver of informed consent.

This design is suitable for describing implementation yield and observed downstream outcomes but cannot isolate the effect of the BPA from secular change, ID staffing, public-health outreach, or other program components.

Methods — intervention and outcome definitions

The BPA prompted a fourth-generation HIV test for adults aged 18–65 with a CBC ordered, no documented HIV infection, and no prior HIV test in the EMR. Nurses informed patients and offered opt-out. Positive screens automatically proceeded to an HIV differentiation assay. An ID nurse disclosed confirmed results, provided counseling, scheduled ID follow-up, and involved social work; patients who could not be contacted were referred to state disease-intervention specialists.

Primary outcomes included linkage to care, time to first ID appointment, ART initiation, baseline viral load/CD4, and viral suppression at 3, 6, and 12 months. Viral suppression was defined as HIV-1 RNA <30 copies/mL. Linkage was defined as attending the first ID appointment after diagnosis. Outcomes without Henry Ford documentation were treated as missing and excluded from the corresponding outcome analyses.

Methods — abstraction and statistics

One ID physician abstracted charts using an electronic form; a second abstractor reviewed 30% of charts. Testing indication was assigned from ED diagnoses and medical decision-making documentation, with “BPA only” reserved for cases where the provider was not already considering STI, opportunistic infection, high-risk features, or pregnancy.

The methods state that year-specific positivity rate ratios versus 2020 were estimated with Poisson log-link models using an offset for test counts and robust standard errors. Two-sided p<0.05 was used without multiplicity adjustment. However, Table 1's footnote states that p values came from Pearson chi-square tests without Yates correction. The manuscript does not reconcile these descriptions.

Results

Among 75,914 ED HIV tests, 176 new diagnoses were identified (0.232%). Annual positivity ranged from 0.133% in partial-year 2025 to 0.401% in the short 2019 launch interval; none of the full-year comparisons versus 2020 reached p<0.05. Provider BPA acceptance was reported as 72.4% overall. Of new diagnoses, 117/176 (66.5%) were attributed to BPA-only screening.

The newly diagnosed cohort had median age 33 years; 83% were Black and 73.3% male. Prior ED use was common (74.4%), 39.2% were hospitalized at diagnosis, and 34.7% had a concomitant STI. Within 90 days, 104/176 (59.1%) had any documented ID appointment; median time to first appointment among those 104 was 13 days. ART was documented in 107 patients with median initiation at 9 days. Baseline median viral load was 95,953 copies/mL (n=123) and median CD4 306 cells/mm³ (n=119).

Discussion

The authors interpret the 66.5% BPA-only share of new diagnoses as evidence that routine automated screening identifies infections that risk-based testing may miss. They attribute the 72.4% provider acceptance rate to transfer of positive-result disclosure and follow-up work from ED staff to the ID team, and argue that the program is feasible even during stressed ED operations.

The manuscript further argues that the overall 0.23% new-diagnosis yield supports routine screening and that relatively preserved median CD4 counts suggest earlier detection. These interpretations are plausible but partly inferential: the design does not measure the counterfactual number of diagnoses without the BPA, and no pre-BPA linkage or ART benchmark is presented.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge retrospective design, single-setting demographic limits, inability to determine why providers opted out, lack of data on patients who declined testing, incomplete outside-system follow-up, deaths during follow-up, delayed ART for selected opportunistic infections, and COVID-era staffing disruptions. Most importantly, 72 patients lacked in-system clinic-attendance data, preventing distinction between outside care and true failure to link.

They also explicitly acknowledge the absence of pre-implementation data needed to test whether ED–ID collaboration caused the observed linkage. Independent review adds two manuscript-level concerns: conflicting viral-suppression percentages between Results prose and Table 4, and conflicting descriptions of the statistical test used for annual positivity comparisons.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that an automated non-targeted opt-out ED screening program with close ED–ID collaboration is a feasible model for finding undiagnosed HIV and supporting rapid linkage and ART. The descriptive data support feasibility and workflow plausibility at this institution. They do not establish comparative effectiveness or guarantee similar linkage outcomes elsewhere.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance8/10

Rationale: Strong fit for ED workflow, clinical decision support, screening implementation, linkage-to-care design, and cross-service handoff architecture.

Evidence basis: Five-year operational experience with measurable screening and downstream care outcomes.

Principal limit: Not a pharmacy-specific intervention and does not test medication decision support.

Would raise score: Multisite replication or direct comparison of workflow variants.

Novelty6/10

Rationale: The BPA/opt-out concept is established; the useful addition is the integrated ED–ID workflow with longitudinal follow-up outcomes.

Evidence basis: Detailed role allocation plus 2019–2025 descriptive outcomes, including viral suppression.

Principal limit: No novel comparative design or new screening technology.

Would raise score: Prospective comparative implementation or a resource/cost-effectiveness analysis.

Method strength5/10

Rationale: Appropriate for description but weak for causal attribution.

Evidence basis: Retrospective chart review; one primary abstractor with 30% secondary review; prespecified outcomes; annual rate ratios reported.

Principal limit: Single site, no pre-implementation comparator for linkage outcomes, incomplete external follow-up, and internal statistical-reporting inconsistencies.

Would raise score: Controlled pre/post or stepped-wedge design, explicit inter-rater reliability, and complete cross-system ascertainment.

Evidence strength5/10

Rationale: Moderate for what happened in this program; low-to-moderate for claims that collaboration caused the outcomes.

Evidence basis: Large screening denominator (75,914) and concrete care-continuum measures.

Principal limit: Available-case follow-up, missing outside-system care, no causal comparator, and inconsistent percentages between prose and Table 4.

Would raise score: Complete follow-up, causal comparator, and reconciled statistical reporting.

External validity4/10

Rationale: Transferability is plausible but constrained by setting and infrastructure.

Evidence basis: High-volume urban ED, integrated ID team, defined workflow, and an underserved population.

Principal limit: Single Detroit health system, local legal/EMR context, CBC-triggered eligibility, and a cohort that was 83% Black.

Would raise score: Replication across health systems, rural/suburban sites, and varying staffing/consent models.

Implementation value8/10

Rationale: The article is unusually usable as a workflow reference.

Evidence basis: Figure 1 specifies trigger logic, confirmatory testing, role ownership, state-health-department fallback, and ID follow-up.

Principal limit: Limited detail on build specifications, alert firing burden, staffing FTE, costs, downtime, duplicate testing prevention, and alert governance.

Would raise score: Technical BPA specification, eligible-alert denominator, workload/cost data, and implementation playbook.

Strengths

Operational specificity. Trigger conditions, testing pathway, role ownership, confirmatory testing, patient-contact responsibility, social-work involvement, and state-health-department fallback are visible and reusable.

Meaningful longitudinal outcomes. The study moves beyond test yield to care-continuum measures including time to appointment, ART initiation, and viral suppression.

Large screening denominator and long observation window. More than 75,000 tests over roughly six calendar years provide a substantial descriptive base.

Critical limitations

No causal comparator. The central claim that ED–ID collaboration drove successful linkage is not testable from this design.

Missing outcome ascertainment. Outside-system care was not captured; available-case viral-suppression denominators represent only about 40–44% of the original 176-person cohort at each follow-up timepoint.

Internal reporting inconsistencies. Viral-suppression percentages in Results do not match the corresponding fractions/Table 4 at 3 and 6 months; the statistical test described in Methods conflicts with the Table 1 footnote.

Single-site implementation dependence. Success depends on ID nursing, social work, public-health outreach, an integrated EMR, and a local consent environment that may not exist elsewhere.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Build pattern worth copying
Separate screening from positive-result ownership.

ED staff can own notification/opt-out and specimen collection while a dedicated ID team owns confirmatory-result review, disclosure, navigation, and ongoing engagement.

CDS design principle
Use the alert as a workflow router, not the intervention by itself.

The paper's highest-value insight is the downstream human service attached to the BPA.

Measure explicitly
Eligible encounters, alert fires, acceptance, patient opt-out, completed tests, positives, new diagnoses, linkage, ART, retention, suppression.

Keep provider cancellation separate from patient refusal.

Add before local adoption
Alert governance and workload data.

Specify firing frequency, repeat-test suppression logic, false-positive handling, staffing capacity, after-hours ownership, downtime, escalation, and cost.

Do not overgeneralize
Observed 59.1% in-system linkage is not a universal benchmark.

Outside-system follow-up was incomplete and local team structure materially shaped the pathway.

Research next step
Comparative implementation study.

A controlled pre/post, stepped-wedge, or multi-site study could test whether the integrated handoff improves diagnosis, linkage, time to ART, and suppression while quantifying burden and cost.

External currency verification

This appraisal is grounded in the supplied article. Two practice-context points were checked against current official sources because the protocol requires recency verification for clinical guidance:

  • CDC HIV testing guidance: current CDC HIV Nexus guidance continues to recommend at least one routine HIV test for patients aged 13–64 and an opt-out approach in routine care. Source: https://www.cdc.gov/hivnexus/hcp/diagnosis-testing/index.html
  • Rapid ART: NIH Adult and Adolescent ARV Guidelines (updated/reviewed September 25, 2025) recommend initiating ART immediately or as soon as possible after diagnosis to improve uptake, linkage, time to suppression, and virologic suppression. Source: https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-arv/initiation-antiretroviral-therapy

These external checks support the article's general practice context; they do not validate the study's causal claims or its local implementation outcomes.
