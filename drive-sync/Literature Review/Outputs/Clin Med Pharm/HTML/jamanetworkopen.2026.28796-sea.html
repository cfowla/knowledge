Scope warning. This is observational comparative-safety evidence. It can inform risk assessment but does not establish that choosing one antipsychotic instead of another causes a lower hyponatremia risk.

One-page synthesis

Core finding: In the weighted Japanese claims cohort, aripiprazole was associated with lower 180-day hyponatremia hazard than olanzapine (aHR 0.52; 95% CI 0.35-0.76). The other primary adjusted comparisons did not exclude the null.

The paper intentionally separates signal generation from hypothesis testing. In 458,745 FAERS monotherapy reports, the seven evaluable non-reference SGAs all had reporting odds ratios below olanzapine; spontaneous-report data cannot provide incidence or absolute risk. The second stage used 55,394 eligible new users in the Japanese MDV claims database, stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting, and cause-specific Cox models. Aripiprazole remained the only statistically significant primary weighted comparison.

The cohort had 366 hyponatremia events (0.7%) and limited baseline sodium testing (92.6% had no baseline sodium measurement recorded), making outcome ascertainment and monitoring intensity central concerns. Sensitivity analyses broadly support the aripiprazole association, but some alternate specifications also made brexpiprazole or quetiapine significant; a severe-outcome sensitivity estimate for blonanserin was based on only one event. The study therefore supports a comparative-safety signal rather than a definitive ranking of SGAs.

Source integrity issues preserved: Figure 1's final displayed box repeats 88,741 after 33,347 exclusions even though the abstract, text, Table 2, and analyses use 55,394 as the final cohort. Separately, the Results prose gives inpatient initiation as 31,723 (57.2%) whereas Table 2 gives 31,723 (57.3%).

Section condensation

Abstract / Key Points

The study compared individual SGA-associated hyponatremia risk using FAERS and a Japanese hospital claims database. Among 55,394 claims patients, aripiprazole showed a lower weighted hazard than olanzapine (aHR 0.52; 95% CI 0.35-0.76), while the authors explicitly retained residual-confounding caution.

Introduction

The rationale is that hyponatremia is clinically consequential, comparative SGA safety evidence is limited, and randomized head-to-head trials are generally not powered for this adverse event. FAERS and claims data are presented as complementary: SRS data are efficient for rare-signal detection but lack denominators; claims data permit incidence estimation and measured-confounder adjustment.

Methods

FAERS reports from 1997 Q4-2023 Q3 were curated through JAPIC processing. Hyponatremia used narrow SMQ 20000141; olanzapine was the reference; analyses required at least three reports and antipsychotic monotherapy. The MDV stage used a new-user design from April 2008-April 2024, ages 18-69, a 180-day baseline period, explicit washout/exclusion rules, and up to 180 days of follow-up.

Pairwise propensity scores with olanzapine included measured baseline covariates. Stabilized IPTW targeted the average treatment effect; balance used |SMD| <0.1. Cause-specific Cox models generated weighted aHRs, with add-on antipsychotic use time-varying. Prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses varied care setting, sodium threshold, and discontinuation grace period.

Results

FAERS contained 17,805,418 reports overall and 458,745 SGA-monotherapy reports. Seven evaluable non-reference SGAs had lower RORs than olanzapine; blonanserin and perospirone were not estimable under the ≥3-report rule. MDV yielded 55,394 final patients from 961,010 routinely prescribed users; 366 developed the outcome.

In primary weighted Cox analyses, only aripiprazole was statistically lower than olanzapine. Stabilized-weight diagnostics were generally acceptable, and the proportional-hazards assumption was not reported as violated. Inpatient stratification retained statistical significance for aripiprazole, while outpatient direction was similar but imprecise.

Discussion

The authors interpret FAERS as hypothesis-generating and the claims analysis as the stronger comparative component. They note that null findings for other agents may reflect confounder control and/or reduced power. Lurasidone had a low FAERS ROR but did not meet the claims cohort size threshold.

Mechanistic discussion invokes SIADH, dopamine/serotonin receptor effects, anticholinergic dry mouth/free-water intake, and aripiprazole's partial D2 agonism. The paper correctly labels these explanations as speculative and untested in this study.

Limitations / Conclusion

Major limitations are spontaneous-report bias and no denominator in FAERS; claims misclassification and underascertainment; missing fluid intake, polydipsia, disease severity, and adherence; confounding by indication and detection bias; incomplete historical exposure; Japan acute-care generalizability; no multiplicity adjustment; and noncausal observational design.

The authors conclude that aripiprazole was associated with lower hyponatremia risk than olanzapine after measured adjustment and that further studies are needed before the comparative association is treated as definitive.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High clinical-pharmacy relevance and a sensible two-stage observational design, but not strong enough to establish a causal antipsychotic safety hierarchy.

Evidence: Clinically meaningful sodium outcome; individual-drug comparisons.
Main limit: Not an intervention trial and no monitoring strategy tested.
Would change with: Prospective comparative validation or replicated multicenter cohorts.

Strengths

Complementary databases; clear separation of signal generation and testing; new-user design; measured-confounder balancing; robust-SE Cox models; time-varying add-on therapy; sensitivity analyses; and transparent limitations.

Principal threats to validity

Differential sodium testing and health-care utilization can alter detection; confounding by indication remains plausible; claims cannot measure several clinically important factors; multiplicity was not formally controlled; and the apparent Figure 1 count error is a reporting-quality concern even though downstream cohort counts are internally consistent at 55,394.

Practice translation

What this can supportConsidering aripiprazole's lower observed hyponatremia hazard as one comparative safety factor when multiple SGAs are otherwise clinically appropriate.
What it cannot supportClaiming aripiprazole prevents hyponatremia, declaring olanzapine causally riskier, or changing therapy on this estimate alone.
MonitoringThe study evaluates drug-outcome associations, not a sodium-monitoring strategy. Patient-specific monitoring decisions still require clinical context and current standards.
Most important replication needLaboratory-rich external cohorts with better control of baseline sodium, polydipsia/fluid intake, disease severity, adherence, and monitoring intensity.

Clinical question (observational PICO framing)

Population: MDV antipsychotic new users aged 18-69 with 180-day baseline washout; ancillary FAERS monotherapy reports.
Exposure: initiation of individual second-generation antipsychotics.
Comparator: olanzapine.
Outcome: hyponatremia within 180 days (Na <135 mEq/L and/or specified ICD-10 diagnoses).
Main estimate: aripiprazole aHR 0.52 (95% CI 0.35-0.76) in the primary weighted claims analysis.

Current-label context (external verification, not study evidence)

Current U.S. labeling provides context that sodium-related adverse events are recognized but does not validate the paper's head-to-head estimate. The 2026 aripiprazole label lists hyponatremia as a rare adult premarketing adverse reaction; the July 2026 olanzapine label lists SIADH among postmarketing reactions. Neither labeling statement supplies the comparative incidence ratio estimated in this paper.
