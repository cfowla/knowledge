One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-utility 2026 adult CDI management update with concrete doses, diagnostic/severity thresholds, recurrence pathways, and a detailed fulminant-CDI/FMT workflow. Its main weakness is methodological: the review did not use systematic evidence synthesis or formal grading.

The update favors fidaxomicin 200 mg twice daily for 10 days as first-line treatment for nonfulminant CDI, while retaining oral vancomycin 125 mg four times daily for 10 days as an acceptable practical alternative. It rejects routine test-of-cure, uses WBC >15,000 cells/µL and/or creatinine >1.5× baseline as severe-disease markers, and defines fulminant disease by shock, ileus, or megacolon. Recurrence management moves early toward microbiota restoration: offer after a second recurrence/third episode, and consider earlier use in high-risk or particularly morbid cases.

The fulminant pathway is the most operationally distinctive part of the article: multidisciplinary management, vancomycin 500 mg q6h orally/enterally plus metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h, rectal vancomycin with ileus, and reassessment for FMT or colectomy after 48 hours without improvement. The authors advocate multidose conventional FMT for severe/fulminant treatment-refractory disease based on observational evidence and distinguish that use from FDA-approved microbiota products intended to prevent recurrence. They also take a restrictive position on oral vancomycin prophylaxis during systemic antibiotics and on probiotics, while favoring dietary fiber and explicit antibiotic stewardship.

Section condensation

Prevention and household counseling — BPA 1

The authors emphasize sporicidal environmental cleaning, barrier precautions in health care settings, soap-and-water handwashing, and home decontamination when reinfection is a concern. They reassure patients that absolute household transmission risk is low and state that separate bathrooms are unnecessary when regular bathroom disinfection is feasible.

Table 1 extends prevention counseling to high-touch surfaces, laundry, shoe removal, pet-paw cleaning, produce washing, and the concept that preserved gut microbiota is the main host defense against CDI. Some of these household recommendations are pragmatic rather than trial-proven.

Diagnosis and severity — BPA 2–3

Diagnosis requires compatible clinical illness—typically new unexplained diarrhea, at least 3 unformed stools in 24 hours—plus supportive testing. The authors prefer a multistep approach beginning with a sensitive assay such as GDH or PCR and then confirming toxin production; discordant PCR-positive/toxin-negative results should be interpreted clinically rather than automatically dismissed.

Hospitalized patients should be categorized as nonsevere, severe, or fulminant. Severe disease is signaled by WBC >15,000 cells/µL and/or creatinine >1.5× baseline; fulminant disease includes shock, ileus, or megacolon. The review cites fulminant CDI mortality around 30–40%, reinforcing the need for rapid escalation.

Initial treatment, adjuncts, and follow-up — BPA 4–6

Fidaxomicin is favored for nonfulminant CDI because recurrence is lower than with vancomycin, while vancomycin remains acceptable based on practical factors such as access and cost. Metronidazole is relegated to combination therapy for fulminant disease. Four-times-daily vancomycin can be given during waking hours rather than forcing overnight q6h dosing.

Bile-acid binders should not be used as CDI therapy or concurrently with oral vancomycin/fidaxomicin because they may bind the antibiotic. Psyllium can be used after recovery for stool bulking. Routine test-of-cure is discouraged because colonization/shedding can persist; recurrent testing is reserved for clinically compatible relapse.

Recurrence and microbiota restoration — BPA 7–9

For a first recurrence, the authors favor changing strategy: vancomycin taper/pulse after initial fidaxomicin, or fidaxomicin after initial vancomycin, with the EXTEND pulsed regimen as an option. They recommend planning for recurrence proactively, including risk assessment and discussion of microbiota restoration.

Microbiota-based therapy should be offered after a second recurrence/third episode and can be considered earlier in high-risk or unusually difficult cases. The review distinguishes mildly/moderately immunocompromised patients—where microbiota therapy may be reasonable—from severely immunocompromised patients, where deferral with suppressive antibiotics is preferred. Long-term vancomycin 125 mg daily is positioned as a fallback for selected patients unable to receive or failing microbiota restoration.

Fulminant CDI and FMT — BPA 10–11

The fulminant regimen is explicit: vancomycin 500 mg q6h orally/enterally plus metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h, with rectal vancomycin 500 mg q6h when ileus limits colonic drug delivery. Reassess response by 48 hours; patients who worsen or fail to improve should be considered for conventional FMT or colectomy.

The article supports multidose conventional FMT delivered by lower endoscopy for severe/fulminant treatment-refractory CDI, while emphasizing that commercial microbiota products are not studied or approved for acute severe/fulminant treatment. FMT should not be used with bowel perforation or obstruction/high-grade stricture, and most responders need multiple administrations. The evidence base is observational rather than randomized.

Secondary prevention, PPIs, and stewardship — BPA 12–14

The authors advise against routine oral vancomycin prophylaxis during systemic antibiotic exposure and against probiotics for primary or secondary prevention. Instead, they emphasize prompt evaluation of recurrent diarrhea and a varied high-fiber diet to support microbiota recovery. This OVP stance rests on limited/conflicting evidence, including an underpowered randomized trial.

PPIs should be reviewed for indication but need not be stopped when clearly indicated; cessation can be discussed when there is no legitimate indication. The final recommendation is direct antibiotic stewardship counseling: prior CDI should make unnecessary antibiotic exposure especially undesirable, but patients should still receive appropriate antibacterial therapy when clinically indicated.

Table 1 — Counseling for household prevention

HAND HYGIENE
- Soap-and-water handwashing is foundational; alcohol hand sanitizer does not kill spores.

ENVIRONMENTAL CLEANING
- Use EPA-approved sporicidal agents on high-touch surfaces.
- Article example: diluted bleach 1:9 with at least 10 minutes contact time.
- Dry bedding/clothes on high heat; consider professional cleaning for soiled carpets/upholstery.

HOUSEHOLD PRACTICES
- Remove shoes at the door; wipe pet paws; wash produce under running water.

RISK COMMUNICATION
- Household exposure increases relative risk, but absolute risk remains low; reassure healthy family members.

  fulminant → Figure 2C pathway

Fulminant CDI → multidisciplinary team
  → vancomycin 500 mg q6h PO/enteral + metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h

Evidence and source-integrity issues

Methodological ceiling: The authors explicitly state that systematic reviews were not performed, so no formal evidence-quality or recommendation-strength ratings accompany the Best Practice Advice statements. This limits how precisely readers can distinguish well-established recommendations from expert-preference areas.
Likely citation mismatch in Table 1: the statement that asymptomatic pets and children under age 2 may harbor toxigenic strains cites reference 13, but reference 13 is a Mayo Clinic Proceedings article on reperfusion for acute myocardial infarction. The reference was preserved as printed rather than silently corrected.
Fulminant FMT: the mortality signal is clinically important, but the authors acknowledge no randomized controlled trials for FMT in fulminant CDI. The recommendation therefore depends on observational data, mechanistic rationale, and expert experience.
OVP: the authors advise against oral vancomycin prophylaxis during systemic antibiotics despite a cited 13.5% absolute reduction in recurrence in the only RCT; they note the result was not statistically significant and the trial was underpowered. This is a preference-sensitive area where evidence remains unsettled.
Currency not externally verified: FDA/product availability and regulatory statements were summarized exactly as reported in this 2026 source. This run did not independently verify post-publication regulatory changes.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Nonfulminant first episode
Prefer fidaxomicin when feasible; vancomycin remains acceptable. Do not use metronidazole monotherapy.
Recurrence
Change strategy at first recurrence and plan early for microbiota restoration in high-risk patients; routinely offer after second recurrence/third episode.
Fulminant disease
Escalate immediately to multidisciplinary care and high-dose vancomycin + IV metronidazole; add rectal vancomycin for ileus and reassess at 48 hours.
Follow-up
No routine test of cure. Re-evaluate true recurrent diarrhea, review PPI indication, discourage unnecessary antibiotics, and do not rely on probiotics.
Clinical-use bottom line: The article is strong as a practical 2026 CDI update and algorithm reference. For contentious or high-stakes decisions—especially OVP, fulminant FMT, and commercial microbiota products—pair it with current formal guidelines, local capabilities, and independently verified regulatory status.
