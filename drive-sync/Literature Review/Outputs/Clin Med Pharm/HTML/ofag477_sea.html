One-page synthesis

Core thesis: The post-2020 rise in CRE incidence across these 10 surveillance sites coincided with a marked increase in NDM-producing CRE, particularly NDM-producing E. coli. Relative to KPC-CRE, NDM-CRE was more often community-associated, less tightly linked to traditional healthcare exposures, more frequently associated with international travel, and genomically more heterogeneous.

The most clinically important signal is not simply “more CRE,” but a changing mechanism mix. KPC remained the most common single carbapenemase in 2023 (0.86 per 100,000), yet NDM rose to 0.66 per 100,000 and was the only carbapenemase type with a statistically significant monotonic trend in the main figure. NDM-E. coli incidence increased from 0.03 to 0.41 per 100,000 (P=.008).

The epidemiologic phenotype also differed. NDM cases had fewer documented healthcare exposures, a substantially higher community-associated proportion, and more international travel. However, “community-associated” is defined by absence of documented healthcare risk factors rather than proven community acquisition, travel history was incomplete and not included in the adjusted model, and site-level heterogeneity is not shown in the attached main manuscript. These constraints prevent interpreting the results as a national causal model of community NDM transmission.

Clinical / public-health question

PECO-like frame. Population: residents of 10 EIP surveillance areas with an incident CRE isolate of specified Escherichia coli, Enterobacter, or Klebsiella species from urine or a normally sterile site. Exposure/comparison: calendar year and NDM-producing versus KPC-producing CRE. Outcomes: crude incidence, epidemiologic classification, healthcare exposures, travel history, organism distribution, clinical outcomes, and genomic alleles/sequence types.

This is not an intervention study. The central question is whether NDM-CRE is increasing and whether its epidemiology differs from the historically dominant KPC-CRE phenotype.

Methods — what was actually done

Surveillance population and case definition

The analysis used active laboratory- and population-based CRE surveillance from 10 CDC Emerging Infections Program sites. An incident case was the first isolation within a 30-day period of E. coli, Enterobacter cloacae complex, Klebsiella aerogenes, K. oxytoca, K. pneumoniae, or K. variicola resistant to at least one carbapenem (doripenem, ertapenem, imipenem, or meropenem) from urine or a normally sterile site. Colonization screening cultures were excluded.

Healthcare-associated (HA) classification included hospital-onset cases or community-onset cases with specified prior-year healthcare exposures, dialysis, or recent indwelling devices. Community-associated (CA) classification required no such documented risk factor. This definition is operational and chart-dependent: CA does not prove that acquisition occurred in the community.

Laboratory characterization

Clinical/state/CDC carbapenemase testing could establish CP-CRE status. CDC testing on submitted isolates included MALDI-TOF identification, reference broth microdilution, mCIM, and real-time PCR for blaKPC, blaNDM, blaVIM, blaIMP, and blaOXA-48-like. A subset of PCR- or mCIM-positive isolates from 2018–2022 underwent WGS for carbapenemase alleles and MLST sequence types; 2023 WGS was unavailable.

Because isolate submission was incomplete and WGS covered only a subset, genomic findings are descriptive rather than a complete population census of strain diversity.

Statistics

Annual crude incidence rates used U.S. Census population estimates. The two-tailed Mann–Kendall test assessed temporal trends in overall CRE, CP/non-CP CRE, carbapenemase type, and organism-specific NDM incidence. NDM-only and KPC-only cases were compared using categorical/continuous tests and a multivariable logistic regression with year, epidemiologic classification, sex, age category, and organism selected a priori.

The adjusted model estimates the odds that a CRE case is NDM rather than KPC among the included cases; it is not a model of the absolute risk of acquiring NDM-CRE in the general population. Travel was not included because it was collected only from 2019 and had substantial missingness.

Results — load-bearing findings

1. CRE burden and carbapenemase distribution

Among 8,421 incident CRE cases, 7,044 (83.6%) had carbapenemase testing and 1,707/7,044 (24.2%) were CP-CRE. Of CP-CRE, 1,002 (58.7%) were KPC, 364 (21.3%) NDM, 131 (7.7%) OXA-48-like, 13 (0.8%) IMP, 8 (0.5%) VIM, and 140 (8.2%) had an unknown carbapenemase gene; 49 cases had more than one carbapenemase gene and 37/49 included NDM.

Overall crude CRE incidence increased from 5.34 to 7.65 per 100,000 (P=.02). NDM-CRE increased from 0.07 to 0.66 per 100,000 (P=.02), while KPC showed no significant trend. NDM-E. coli increased from 0.03 to 0.41 per 100,000 (P=.008); NDM-Enterobacter and NDM-Klebsiella did not show significant trends.

2. NDM versus KPC phenotype

Complete CRFs were available for 353 NDM and 975 KPC cases. NDM cases were more often E. coli (199/353, 56.4%), whereas KPC cases were predominantly Klebsiella spp. (690/975, 70.8% in the narrative). NDM cases had fewer documented comorbidities (any comorbidity 88.6% vs 96.7%; P<.001) and lower Charlson scores (median 2 vs 3; P<.001).

Most isolates were from urine and most infections were UTIs in both groups. NDM was more often CA (19.8% vs 5.1%; P<.001) and less often associated with prior healthcare facility stay (58.8% vs 85.4%), chronic dialysis (2.8% vs 9.9%), urinary catheterization (29.0% vs 49.4%), or other indwelling devices (13.5% vs 39.0%). ICU admission, length of stay, and 30-day crude mortality did not differ significantly.

3. Adjusted comparison

Each additional calendar year was associated with 40% higher adjusted odds that an included CRE case was NDM rather than KPC (aOR 1.40; 95% CI 1.28–1.54). CA classification was associated with NDM (aOR 3.2; 95% CI 1.98–5.17). Age 0–24 years versus ≥75 years had aOR 3.99 (95% CI 1.59–10.02). Organism was the strongest modeled discriminator: E. coli versus Klebsiella spp. aOR 10.20 (95% CI 7.29–14.28), and Enterobacter spp. aOR 2.69 (95% CI 1.84–3.94).

4. Travel signal

From 2019–2023, international travel in the prior year was documented in 77/282 (27.3%) NDM cases versus 26/717 (3.6%) KPC cases (P<.001). Among CA cases with travel data, 19/32 (59.4%) NDM versus 1/22 (4.5%) KPC had international travel. India was the most common destination among NDM travelers (42.5%). Hospitalization during travel was documented less often in NDM travelers (32.5%) than KPC travelers (61.5%).

This is a strong descriptive signal but not an adjusted independent risk estimate: travel documentation was incomplete, travel was not included in the multivariable model, and acquisition timing/location could not be established.

5. Genomic findings

WGS was available for 409/721 (56.7%) KPC- or NDM-K. pneumoniae cases and 143/316 (45.3%) KPC- or NDM-E. coli cases. KPC-3 predominated among KPC isolates (39/69 E. coli; 236/367 K. pneumoniae). NDM-5 predominated among NDM isolates (57/74 E. coli; 17/42 K. pneumoniae). KPC-E. coli was dominated by ST131 and KPC-K. pneumoniae by ST258; NDM-E. coli commonly included ST405 and ST167, while NDM-K. pneumoniae commonly included ST147 and ST16.

NDM isolates showed an expanding set of observed sequence types as isolate availability increased. The pattern supports non-single-clone dissemination and is biologically compatible with horizontal movement of blaNDM, but the study does not directly quantify plasmid transfer or prove horizontal transmission events.

Discussion & interpretation

The authors interpret the data as a reversal from earlier pre-pandemic declines in CRE, with NDM emerging as a major driver of the newer increase. They emphasize that NDM differs epidemiologically from KPC: younger/less healthcare-exposed cases, more community-associated classification, a strong E. coli association, substantial international travel, and multiple sequence types rather than a narrowly clonal pattern.

The treatment discussion is mechanistically important but secondary to the actual study design. The manuscript notes that metallo-β-lactamases are not inhibited by several newer β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations and highlights cefiderocol and aztreonam–avibactam as key therapeutic options while citing emerging resistance concerns. Those statements are literature-based context; this study did not measure treatment efficacy, comparative susceptibility to these agents across the surveillance cohort, or treatment-outcome relationships.

Best-supported inference: Surveillance systems and stewardship programs that track “CRE” as a single category risk missing a meaningful mechanism shift. NDM deserves separate local tracking because its organism distribution, exposure profile, and treatment implications differ from KPC.
Do not overread the CA signal. The study shows a higher proportion of NDM cases meeting an operational “community-associated” definition. It does not establish the acquisition setting for individual cases, and incomplete external healthcare/travel records can misclassify exposure status.

Limitations & source-internal consistency checks

Authors' stated limitations

  • The 10 EIP surveillance areas are geographically diverse but not necessarily representative of the U.S. population.
  • Respiratory and many wound cultures were excluded, and the system targeted clinical infection rather than colonization, potentially underestimating total CRE occurrence.
  • Carbapenemase testing was incomplete (annual 73–89%; overall about 84%), creating possible CP-CRE undercounting or misclassification.
  • Healthcare exposures and travel depended on medical-record documentation; travel had substantial missingness and was excluded from the multivariable model.
  • The study could not determine when or where acquisition occurred.
  • WGS involved a subset of isolates and lacked 2023 sequence data.

Independent appraisal limitations

  • Aggregate geography: the main manuscript does not show site-stratified incidence trajectories, so it cannot establish whether the NDM increase was broadly distributed or concentrated in particular regions.
  • Six annual time points: the Mann–Kendall trend test is appropriate for monotonic trend assessment, but only six yearly observations limit temporal resolution and cannot explain causes of the post-2020 change.
  • Comparator conditioning: logistic regression asks “NDM versus KPC among included CRE cases,” not “who in the population will acquire NDM.” Odds ratios should not be converted into absolute patient risk.
  • Crude outcomes: ICU admission and mortality were not adjusted for severity, source, treatment, or comorbidity; similarity in crude outcomes does not demonstrate equal virulence or treatment difficulty.
  • Travel confounding: the strong travel association is clinically interesting but cannot be treated as an independent predictor without better ascertainment and adjusted analysis.
  • Genomic sampling: observed ST diversity rises alongside isolate availability in several NDM strata; no normalized diversity statistic or plasmid-transmission analysis is provided.

Source-internal consistency checks

Age-group discrepancy: the abstract and Table 3 report aOR 3.99 (95% CI 1.59–10.02) for age 0–24 years versus ≥75 years. The Results narrative instead says “aged 25–49 years” for that same aOR/CI. Table 3 and the abstract agree with each other; this artifact preserves the discrepancy rather than silently rewriting the source.
Table 1 percentage anomaly: the printed K. pneumoniae and K. oxytoca percentages under NDM/KPC columns appear to use the total N=1328 denominator (for example, 79 shown as 5.9%) rather than their subgroup denominators, unlike adjacent rows. The narrative's combined KPC Klebsiella figure (690/975, 70.8%) is internally coherent. This artifact therefore prioritizes counts and clearly supported proportions.
Table 1 median-age anomaly: the table prints a total median age of 70 years while subgroup medians are 67 (NDM) and 66 (KPC). A combined median exceeding both subgroup medians is mathematically anomalous, suggesting a source/table error. Age-category counts and Table 3 are more reliable for interpretation.
Discussion fold-change wording: the manuscript calls the NDM rise “nearly five-fold.” This is compatible with 2019→2023 (0.15→0.66 ≈4.4-fold) but not 2018→2023 (0.07→0.66 ≈9.4-fold); the baseline for that sentence is not explicit.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first. High-value current epidemiology for antimicrobial resistance, diagnostic stewardship, and CRE risk framing. Use it to understand a mechanism shift and to justify local NDM-specific surveillance; do not use it alone to select therapy or define an empiric-treatment threshold.
Relevance
9/10

Why: Directly informs CRE/NDM surveillance and stewardship. Evidence basis: 8,421 cases, 10 EIP sites, mechanism-specific incidence and epidemiology. Limiter: not a treatment study. Would rise: linkage to local decision thresholds or treatment outcomes.

Novelty
8/10

Why: Population-based, multi-site comparative description of NDM versus KPC adds more than single-center reports. Limiter: supportive U.S. reports already exist and are cited. Would rise: national site-stratified genomic surveillance with acquisition pathways.

Method strength
8/10

Why: Active surveillance, standardized CRFs, census denominators, reference lab methods, prespecified model covariates. Limiter: incomplete carbapenemase testing, chart-based exposures, partial WGS, six annual points. Would rise: complete mechanism testing, prospective exposure capture, site-level modeling.

Evidence strength
8/10

Why: The central trend and NDM/KPC contrasts are supported by large counts, rates, CIs, and consistent descriptive patterns. Limiter: observational and noncausal; travel missingness; some source table inconsistencies. Would rise: replication with complete data and adjusted acquisition models.

External validity
7/10

Why: Large, geographically diverse U.S. surveillance population. Limiter: 10 selected EIP areas are not nationally representative and site heterogeneity is not shown. Would rise: national standardized surveillance or concordant regional estimates.

Implementation value
8/10

Why: Supports mechanism-specific reporting, travel/exposure history, community surveillance, and local NDM tracking. Limiter: no tested workflow, no empiric-treatment rule, no intervention outcomes. Would rise: validated risk tool or stewardship pathway with patient outcomes.

Trust statement

High confidence that the manuscript documents a real increase in NDM-CRE within the covered surveillance areas and a different NDM-vs-KPC case profile. Moderate confidence in national generalization and in the inference of local community transmission, because surveillance areas are selected, exposures are chart-dependent, travel is incompletely ascertained, and acquisition location is unknown.

Practice / implementation takeaways

For antimicrobial stewardship

  • Track carbapenemase mechanism, not just aggregate CRE, when local data permit.
  • Do not assume the historical KPC phenotype represents all CRE; NDM is increasingly associated with E. coli and fewer classic healthcare exposures.
  • Use this paper as epidemiologic context, not as direct evidence for a specific regimen.

For diagnostics / microbiology

  • Mechanism-level detection can carry therapeutic and infection-prevention relevance because NDM differs materially from KPC.
  • Local reporting that distinguishes NDM, KPC, OXA-48-like, and other mechanisms may be more actionable than a single CP-CRE bucket.
  • Rising NDM-E. coli argues for attention beyond classic nosocomial Klebsiella patterns.

For risk assessment

  • Recent international travel is a meaningful history element, especially for community-onset CRE, but absence of travel does not exclude NDM.
  • “Community-associated” should trigger broader exposure inquiry, not be equated with proven community acquisition.
  • Local epidemiology remains essential before changing empiric coverage.

For infection prevention / public health

  • Expand surveillance questions beyond inpatient exposures to outpatient care, travel, household/community contacts, and potentially animal/environmental interfaces where relevant.
  • Monitor high-risk NDM-associated clones, but distinguish clone spread from mobile-element spread.
  • Site-level trend reporting would improve actionability.

What should not change from this paper alone

  • No blanket expansion of empiric MBL-active therapy.
  • No inference that NDM and KPC have equivalent clinical severity because crude mortality was similar.
  • No use of the reported aORs as an absolute patient-level NDM risk calculator.

Best next evidence

  • Prospective exposure interviews and site-stratified incidence.
  • Complete longitudinal WGS including plasmid context and 2023+ isolates.
  • Mechanism-specific susceptibility, treatment, and outcome studies.
  • Validated local prediction/diagnostic pathways for severe CRE infection.
