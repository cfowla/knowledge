Scope warning. This paper measures 12-week glycemic, weight, and short-term adverse-event outcomes. It does not test inpatient therapeutic interchange, cardiovascular outcomes, heart-failure outcomes, kidney outcomes, or long-term comparative safety.

One-page synthesis

The authors compared empagliflozin with dapagliflozin as add-on therapy in patients with type 2 diabetes and inadequate glycemic control. They report 280 randomized participants and 255 analyzed participants after 25 dropouts. At 12 weeks, the article reports improvements in weight, fasting blood glucose, and HbA1c in both groups. The authors interpret several comparisons as favoring empagliflozin and report fewer adverse-effect events in the empagliflozin column.

The direct comparison is relevant to agent-level formulary questions, but the paper has serious internal reporting problems. Participant-flow counts do not reconcile. Abstract and Methods doses do not match. Narrative changes in weight, fasting glucose, and HbA1c do not match changes implied by Table 2. The article calls p=0.032 non-significant despite defining p<0.05 as significant. Table 3 reports 15 genital infections among 127 dapagliflozin participants as 7.08%, although that fraction is 11.81%. Several baseline and safety p-values also fail simple recalculation from the displayed summary data. These problems prevent reliable use of the reported between-agent differences.

Verdict: Do not use for practice. Use this paper only as a hypothesis-generating direct comparison. It should not establish empagliflozin superiority, dapagliflozin inferiority, therapeutic equivalence, or an inpatient formulary interchange rule.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract describes a randomized comparison of empagliflozin 25 mg with dapagliflozin 10 mg for 12 weeks. It reports reductions in body weight, fasting blood sugar, and HbA1c in both groups, with larger reported reductions in fasting blood sugar and HbA1c for empagliflozin. It also reports fewer genital and urinary infections with empagliflozin.

The abstract already contains one central contradiction. It describes the between-group body-weight comparison as non-significant at p=0.032, while the Methods later define p<0.05 as statistically significant.

Introduction

The introduction frames SGLT2 inhibitors as an oral class that lowers glucose through reduced renal glucose reabsorption and notes expected effects on body weight and other cardiometabolic measures. It positions the study as a direct empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin comparison in patients who remain inadequately controlled on first-line therapy.

The background cites prior placebo-controlled and class-level evidence. Those citations provide context only. They are not evidence generated by this trial.

Material and methods

The article reports a 12-week randomized controlled trial conducted from March to May 2020 at four private clinical settings and the diabetic clinic of Sheikh Zayed Medical College. Eligibility required type 2 diabetes with HbA1c 7.5% to 11% on background oral therapy. The study excluded renal dysfunction, active genitourinary infection, pregnancy, several endocrine conditions, cancer, and specified medication exposures. The authors state that the institutional review board approved the protocol and that participants provided written informed consent.

Randomization is described only as simple random sampling. The paper does not report allocation concealment, blinding, trial registration, or a sample-size calculation. It says primary baseline-to-week-12 changes used paired t tests and adverse effects used chi-square tests. It does not clearly specify a test for the between-group efficacy p-values that appear in Table 2.

Results

The Results state that 410 patients were recruited, 356 enrolled, 76 excluded, and 280 randomized. After 10 empagliflozin and 15 dapagliflozin dropouts, 128 and 127 participants were analyzed. Table 2 and the narrative both report improvement in weight, fasting glucose, and HbA1c, but their numerical changes do not agree. The BMI final p-value is 0.016, while the weight, fasting glucose, and HbA1c final p-values are 0.032, 0.001, and 0.002.

Table 3 reports no serious adverse effects leading to discontinuation. It lists 19 total adverse-effect events in the empagliflozin column and 40 in the dapagliflozin column. The dapagliflozin genital-infection row is internally inconsistent because 15 events among 127 participants are labeled 7.08%. The source does not clarify whether total adverse effects represent unique patients or summed event categories.

Discussion

The authors interpret both agents as effective and well tolerated and state that empagliflozin had an advantage for body weight, fasting blood sugar, HbA1c, and reported adverse effects. They compare their findings with earlier studies of each agent and with one longer observational head-to-head study.

The Discussion acknowledges that renal-impairment safety was not assessed. It does not resolve the numerical conflicts within the study's own tables and narrative.

Conclusion and disclosures

The authors conclude that both agents have excellent efficacy, safety, and tolerability and can be used safely as add-on therapy. They call for larger and longer studies and further evaluation in cardiovascular disease and renal impairment.

The article reports no conflict of interest and no funding agency.

Implementation takeaways

Best use

Use as a citation showing that a small direct empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin comparison exists. Treat its apparent between-agent differences as hypothesis-generating only.

Do not use for

Do not use the paper to claim empagliflozin is clinically superior, dapagliflozin is less safe, the agents are therapeutically equivalent, or one should replace the other on an inpatient formulary.
