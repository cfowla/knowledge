Clinical question and evidence frame

Question raised by the correspondence

For adults at least 50 years old, particularly older adults for whom enhanced influenza vaccines are already available, does mRNA-1010 provide clinically meaningful added value beyond licensed alternatives, and how should its increased reactogenicity and strain-specific performance be interpreted?

The correspondence does not supply a new randomized comparison against enhanced vaccines. Its evidence frame is therefore a critique of comparator choice, endpoint relevance, strain matching, and tolerability, with an authors’ response using the original Fluent trial and cited external studies for context.

Liu, Zhang, and Jiang letter

Comparator and clinical positioning

The letter accepts the reported 26.6% relative efficacy versus standard-dose vaccine but argues that this benchmark does not answer the more clinically relevant question in older adults: whether mRNA-1010 adds value over enhanced or non-egg-based options already in use. The authors cite recombinant-vaccine efficacy and ACIP preference for high-dose, recombinant, or adjuvanted vaccines in adults ≥65 years to support that concern.

This is the strongest practical criticism in the exchange. Superiority to a conventional comparator establishes efficacy relative to that comparator, but it does not establish a preferred position among enhanced options.

A/H3N2 mechanistic question

The letter argues that avoidance of egg adaptation should be most relevant for A/H3N2 because egg-adapted antigens can alter antibody recognition, yet the correspondence reports lower relative efficacy against A/H3N2 than A/H1N1 (22.2% vs 29.6%). The letter notes that the strain-specific confidence intervals were wide and overlapping.

The correspondents ask for strain-specific efficacy stratified by comparator product and antigenic or genetic match. This request is methodologically appropriate for interpretation but remains exploratory because the authors later state the trial was not powered for definitive strain-specific conclusions.

Locquet and Dogné letter

Risk–benefit and uptake

The second letter argues that technological novelty is insufficient by itself to establish public-health value. The relevant questions are real-world effectiveness, safety, and communication. It highlights higher reactogenicity with mRNA-1010 and reports serious adverse events in 2.2% of mRNA-1010 recipients versus 1.9% with standard-dose comparator.

The letter emphasizes that even transient adverse reactions can influence vaccine hesitancy in seasonal influenza, where perceived individual risk may be low. The authors therefore question how much incremental value should be assigned if benefits are demonstrated mainly through RT-PCR-confirmed endpoints rather than reductions in hospitalization, complications, or mortality.

Communication

The correspondents conclude that transparent communication about reactogenicity in the context of expected benefit is essential for trust and uptake. This is an implementation argument, not direct evidence that reactogenicity will reduce uptake in this population.

Authors’ reply

Primary efficacy and comparator issue

The reply reiterates that mRNA-1010 met all prespecified superiority criteria versus a licensed standard-dose vaccine, with 26.6% relative vaccine efficacy (95% CI, 16.7–35.4) against protocol-defined RT-PCR-confirmed influenza-like illness. The authors argue that the magnitude is consistent with enhanced-vaccine trials that also used standard-dose comparators.

They nevertheless agree that comparisons with enhanced vaccines are clinically important. They cite a separate phase 3 trial in which mRNA-1010 produced superior immune responses to a high-dose influenza comparator and state that Moderna is collecting real-world comparative-effectiveness data versus an enhanced vaccine. Immunogenicity superiority is a surrogate and does not resolve comparative clinical efficacy.

Strain-specific clarification

The reply states that 2024–2025 circulating A/H3N2 viruses showed limited evidence of egg-adaptive antigenic change but did show antigenic drift. The authors explicitly caution that Fluent was not designed or powered for definitive strain-specific conclusions.

They report 22.2% overall relative efficacy against A/H3N2 (95% CI, 4.3–36.9) and 30.5% in an additional analysis restricted to antigenically matched A/H3N2 (95% CI, 4.6–49.4). The latter estimate is compatible with a clinically meaningful effect but remains imprecise and exploratory.

Reactogenicity response

The authors agree that communication is important and acknowledge more frequent adverse reactions with mRNA-1010. They characterize these reactions as predominantly transient and mild to moderate (grade 1 or 2) and state that they did not warrant medical attention.

The reply argues that temporary reactogenicity should be interpreted in the context of clinical benefit, including reported higher efficacy in high-risk populations and against emergency-department visits and hospitalizations. Those latter claims are not quantified in this correspondence and should be checked in the primary trial before practice use.

Bottom line: Use this source to qualify the Fluent trial, not to replace it. The strongest defensible takeaway is that mRNA-1010 was superior to a standard-dose influenza vaccine in the original phase 3 trial, while comparative value versus enhanced vaccines, definitive strain-specific advantage, and the balance between incremental efficacy and reactogenicity remain incompletely resolved.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use the right comparatorWhen discussing added value in older adults, standard-dose influenza vaccine may not be the most decision-relevant benchmark if enhanced options are available.
Do not overread H3N2The authors explicitly state that Fluent was not powered for definitive strain-specific conclusions; the matched A/H3N2 estimate is exploratory and imprecise.
Separate reactogenicity from serious harmMore frequent temporary reactions can matter for acceptance even when reported serious-adverse-event rates are similar in magnitude.
Hard outcomes matterFor practice translation, confirm hospitalization, emergency-department, complication, and mortality outcomes in the primary trial rather than relying on correspondence shorthand.
Surrogate ≠ comparative efficacySuperior immunogenicity versus high-dose vaccine in a cited separate phase 3 study does not establish superior clinical effectiveness.
What not to inferDo not infer superiority to enhanced influenza vaccines, definitive A/H3N2 advantage, or current guideline/regulatory preference from this correspondence alone.
