One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In stable symptomatic systolic HF, adding once-daily metoprolol CR/XL to then-standard therapy reduced mortality and major hospitalization outcomes while improving physician-rated functional class and a patient global treatment evaluation. The disease-specific Minnesota Living with Heart Failure score did not significantly improve.

The trial randomized 3991 patients (1990 metoprolol CR/XL; 2001 placebo). The prespecified second primary end point—total mortality or all-cause hospitalization, time to first event—occurred in 641 vs 767 patients, a reported 19% risk reduction (95% CI 10%-27%; P<.001). Total mortality or worsening-HF hospitalization was reduced by 31% (311 vs 439; 95% CI 20%-40%; P<.001). The paper repeats the previously reported total-mortality finding of 145 vs 217 deaths, a 34% risk reduction.

The morbidity signal was clinically coherent: fewer patients had any hospitalization (29.1% vs 33.3%), cardiovascular hospitalization (19.8% vs 24.7%), or worsening-HF hospitalization (10.0% vs 14.7%); worsening-HF hospitalizations totaled 317 vs 451 and corresponding hospital days 3401 vs 5303. NYHA class change favored metoprolol (P=.003) and the McMaster Overall Treatment Evaluation favored metoprolol (P=.009), but the adjusted Minnesota Living with Heart Failure score difference was not significant (mean difference -0.9; 95% CI -3.4 to 1.6; P=.20).

Why it matters: The trial links beta1-blockade to patient-centered outcomes—survival, hospital use, symptoms, and tolerability—not just hemodynamic or surrogate measures.
Key constraints: Early stopping, very small NYHA IV representation, exclusion of decompensated/bed-confined patients, exclusion of EF >0.40 and early post-MI HF, a quality-of-life substudy with incomplete paired forms, and sponsor relationships limit how broadly or quantitatively the findings should be generalized.

Section condensation

Structured abstract

The abstract frames an unresolved question after mortality benefits from beta1-blockade had emerged: whether treatment also reduces hospital use and improves symptoms and quality of life. It identifies the randomized double-blind design, 313 sites, 3991 participants, the once-daily titration strategy to 200 mg, the two primary outcomes, and the 741-patient quality-of-life substudy.

The abstract reports lower incidence of all predefined endpoints with metoprolol CR/XL, including 19% lower total mortality/all-cause hospitalization, 31% lower total mortality/worsening-HF hospitalization, fewer worsening-HF admissions and hospital days, improved NYHA class, and improved McMaster OTE score. The authors conclude that survival, HF hospitalization burden, functional class, and patient well-being improved.

Introduction

The introduction places MERIT-HF in the transition from ACE-inhibitor-centered therapy toward neurohormonal blockade. Earlier small beta-blocker studies and the contemporary CIBIS-II and MERIT-HF mortality reports suggested a major survival effect, but hospitalizations, symptom burden, safety, and quality-of-life effects remained less fully characterized.

The specific purpose of this JAMA report is therefore broader than the original mortality report: to characterize combined mortality/morbidity endpoints, recurrent hospital use, physician- and patient-reported status, withdrawal, and tolerability.

Methods — organization and population

MERIT-HF used a 2-week single-blind placebo run-in followed by double-blind randomization. The allocation procedure balanced treatment groups by site and several prognostic characteristics. A blinded Independent Endpoint Committee classified events from source records using prespecified definitions; an Independent Safety Committee monitored safety.

Major eligibility required symptomatic HF for at least 3 months, NYHA II-IV, LVEF ≤0.40, age 40-80 years, resting HR ≥68/min, optimized therapy for at least 2 weeks, and stability during run-in. Exclusions included MI/unstable angina within 28 days, a beta1-blocker indication/contraindication, severe decompensated HF, and supine SBP <100 mm Hg.

Methods — treatment, endpoints, and statistics

Metoprolol CR/XL began at 25 mg once daily for NYHA II and 12.5 mg once daily for NYHA III-IV, with dose doubling about every 2 weeks toward 200 mg/day as tolerated. Follow-up visits occurred every third month. NYHA class was physician assessed; the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure questionnaire and McMaster OTE captured patient-reported status.

Primary outcomes were total mortality and total mortality/all-cause hospitalization (time to first event). Other predefined combined endpoints and hospitalization counts were assessed. Analyses were intention-to-treat; time-to-event comparisons used log-rank testing and Cox models. The group-sequential design used prespecified interim stopping boundaries; the trial stopped at the second planned interim analysis after the mortality criterion was exceeded.

Results — major endpoints and hospitalizations

Baseline groups were well matched. Total mortality/all-cause hospitalization fell 19%; total mortality/worsening-HF hospitalization 31%; death/transplantation 32%; cardiac death/nonfatal MI 39%; and mortality/HF hospitalization/HF ED visit 32% (all P<.001). The paper also reiterates the prior total-mortality result: 145 vs 217 deaths, 34% risk reduction.

Hospital burden decreased across multiple measures. Any hospitalization affected 581 vs 668 patients; cardiovascular hospitalization 394 vs 494; worsening-HF hospitalization 200 vs 294. Worsening-HF admissions numbered 317 vs 451 and accounted for 3401 vs 5303 hospital days. The proportion of days alive outside hospital was 95% vs 93% (P<.001).

Results — symptoms, quality of life, withdrawal, and safety

NYHA class change favored metoprolol CR/XL (P=.003). McMaster OTE also favored metoprolol (P=.009), with 50% vs 40% reporting improvement; among metoprolol responders with importance ratings, 72% regarded improvement as important or greater for daily activities. In contrast, Minnesota Living with Heart Failure scores did not significantly differ (adjusted mean difference -0.9; 95% CI -3.4 to 1.6; P=.20).

Permanent study-drug withdrawal for any cause was 279 vs 310; withdrawal due to any adverse event was 196 vs 234. Heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and angina-related withdrawal were more frequent on placebo; bradycardia, hypotension, and dizziness were slightly more frequent on metoprolol. No significant unfavorable signal appeared in prespecified subgroup analyses, although several subgroup estimates had low power.

Comment and source-stated limitations

The authors interpret the morbidity results as consistent with the mortality benefit and with other beta-blocker trials. They emphasize fewer HF hospitalizations and hospital days, improved NYHA class/OTE, and acceptable tolerability with low-dose initiation and gradual titration. They also speculate that reduced HF hospital days could lower health-care costs, but no formal economic analysis is presented.

The paper explicitly limits generalizability to groups not enrolled: bed-confined severe HF, EF >0.40, and early post-MI HF. NYHA IV representation was small and associated with wide CIs. The authors also discuss discordance between OTE and the Minnesota questionnaire rather than treating all quality-of-life instruments as uniformly positive.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best used as landmark evidence for morbidity, mortality, and tolerability of metoprolol succinate CR/XL in stable symptomatic HFrEF. Do not use it alone as a complete current HFrEF treatment algorithm.

Validity

  • Randomization/blinding: strong. The allocation procedure was designed to balance important prognostic variables; patients and investigators were double-blinded after run-in.
  • Outcome ascertainment: strong for major clinical events, with blinded independent adjudication and no loss of vital status reported.
  • Early stopping: prespecified and triggered by mortality efficacy, but it shortens follow-up and can exaggerate apparent treatment magnitude compared with a trial that runs to its planned event total.
  • Multiplicity: the paper analyzes numerous secondary and subgroup outcomes, but the major reported morbidity effects were predefined and consistently directional.
  • Subgroups: should be interpreted as safety/consistency analyses; several had fewer than 180 events and wide CIs, especially NYHA IV.
  • Patient-reported outcomes: mixed. OTE favored treatment, whereas the Minnesota questionnaire did not; the source itself discusses possible instrument sensitivity differences.
  • Sponsor/conflicts: AstraZeneca funded the study; specified investigators had honoraria/advisory relationships with AstraZeneca. This does not invalidate the randomized results but increases the importance of protocol prespecification, blinded adjudication, and independent replication.

Approximate NNT / NNH

The paper does not report NNT/NNH. This SEA does not convert the time-to-event primary outcomes into a definitive NNT because follow-up varied and the trial stopped early. Crude event proportions could generate approximate values, but they would be less faithful than the reported time-to-event risk reductions.

Clinical-practice translation

PICO

P: stable adults aged 40-80 with symptomatic chronic HF, NYHA II-IV, LVEF ≤0.40 on standard therapy. I: metoprolol succinate CR/XL once daily, initiated low and titrated toward 200 mg/day. C: placebo. O: mortality, all-cause/HF hospitalization, recurrent hospital use, NYHA class, patient-reported well-being, and withdrawal/tolerability.

Source-derived practice signal: In this enrolled population and treatment context, gradual metoprolol CR/XL titration produced substantial reductions in mortality and HF-related hospital utilization without an overall excess of adverse-event withdrawal.
Current-practice verification — external: The 2024 ACC HFrEF Expert Consensus Decision Pathway describes contemporary chronic HFrEF GDMT as four core classes: ARNI, an evidence-based beta-blocker, SGLT inhibitor, and MRA. AHA material reviewed in 2025 continues to identify metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, and bisoprolol as beta-blockers with the best HF evidence. A 2026 AHA/ACC/ADA/ASN CKM guideline likewise places beta-blockers within quadruple HFrEF therapy. Therefore, MERIT-HF remains directly relevant to the beta-blocker component of HFrEF care, but its 1990s background regimen should not be mistaken for the full contemporary regimen.
  • Use now? Yes as foundational efficacy/titration evidence for metoprolol succinate in stable HFrEF; no as a standalone current regimen.
  • Do not extrapolate directly to: HFpEF, acute decompensated/bed-confined HF, very early post-MI HF, or the small NYHA IV subgroup without additional evidence.
  • Monitoring implication from the source: bradycardia, hypotension, and dizziness were modestly more frequent reasons for withdrawal with metoprolol, supporting slow titration and tolerability monitoring.
  • Formulation: the evaluated drug was controlled-/extended-release metoprolol succinate, not immediate-release metoprolol tartrate.
