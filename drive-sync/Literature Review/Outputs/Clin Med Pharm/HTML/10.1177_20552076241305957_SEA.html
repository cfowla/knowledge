Title: Anti-factor Xa during unfractionated heparin therapy in critically ill patients: Development of prediction models using machine learning

Journal: DIGITAL HEALTH, Volume 11, 2025, 1-9

DOI: 10.1177/20552076241305957

Authors

Boris Delange; Guillaume Bouzillé; Pauline Guillot; Anaëlle Bichon; Océane Bernard de Lajartre; Isabelle Gouin; Yoann Launey; Alexandre Mansour; Mathieu Lesouhaitier; Jean-Marc Tadié; Arnaud Gacouin; Marc Cuggia; Adel Maamar.

Design

Single-center retrospective cohort with development and internal test evaluation of six machine-learning classifiers using ICU clinical data warehouse records from December 21, 2019 to November 22, 2021.

Scope warning

This paper evaluates prediction of subsequent anti-Xa categories. It does not demonstrate that a model-guided UFH dose improves bleeding, thrombosis, mortality, time in therapeutic range, or other patient-centered outcomes.

One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. High topical relevance to ICU UFH monitoring and CDS, but the reported discrimination should not be treated as externally validated or deployment-ready. The major reason is the interval-level train/test split across repeated ICU stays.

Core thesis

Routine ICU data can support machine-learning prediction of whether the next anti-Xa result during UFH therapy will be sub-therapeutic, therapeutic, or supra-therapeutic. Random forest was the authors' preferred overall model.

Key result

Random forest achieved test AUROC 0.80 [0.77-0.83] and AUPRC 0.61 [0.58-0.65]. Reported sensitivity was 0.56 [0.53-0.59], specificity 0.82 [0.82-0.82], accuracy 0.71 [0.68-0.73], and F1 0.57 [0.53-0.61].

What drives prediction

Current UFH rate, prior UFH rate, and start-of-interval anti-Xa were the most important available features. Removing the start anti-Xa reduced random-forest performance only modestly (AUROC 0.77; AUPRC 0.59).

Most important limitation

Intervals, not patients or ICU stays, were randomly split. Training represented 279 stays and testing 224 while the entire included cohort contained only 286 unique stays. At least 217 stays therefore appear in both sets, so the test set is not stay-independent.

Appraisal inference: the overlap creates a substantial risk that reported test performance is optimistic because temporally adjacent or otherwise correlated intervals from the same ICU stay can occur on both sides of the split. This inference is based on the source's own allocation method and stay counts; the authors did not identify it as a limitation.

Clinical / prediction question

Population

Critically ill adults receiving UFH in medical, surgical, or cardiac surgery ICUs, with serial anti-Xa monitoring and complete required predictor data.

Prediction inputs

Sixteen routine features including demographics, ECMO, renal function/RRT, inflammation markers, hematologic/volume surrogates, bilirubin, current/prior UFH dosing, boluses, prior anti-Xa, and interval duration.

Outcome

Next anti-Xa category: <0.3 sub-therapeutic; 0.3-0.7 therapeutic; >0.7 supra-therapeutic.

Comparators

Random forest, linear SVM, RBF SVM, XGBoost, neural network, and penalized logistic regression were compared with each other. The paper did not prospectively compare model-guided dosing against a standard UFH nomogram.

Section condensation

Abstract

The study frames rapid attainment of therapeutic UFH anticoagulation as a safety and efficacy problem in ICU patients and proposes machine learning to predict the next anti-Xa range. The abstract reports 3,790 anti-Xa intervals from 211 patients and identifies random forest as the best overall model, with AUROC 0.80, AUPRC 0.61, sensitivity 0.56, and specificity 0.82.

The abstract appropriately calls for prospective multicenter validation before clinical integration. It does not claim that clinical outcomes improved.

Background

The authors motivate UFH use in critical illness by its short half-life and need for frequent titration. They favor anti-Xa over aPTT as a more stable monitoring assay in ICU physiology and note that classic nomograms can perform poorly in critically ill populations.

The conceptual rationale is that UFH response reflects renal and hepatic function, inflammation, volume status, ECMO, and other factors that are difficult to combine manually. Prior ML work had focused mainly on aPTT; this study shifts the prediction target to anti-Xa.

Methods - population and exclusions

Data came from one tertiary teaching hospital's clinical data warehouse. Adult ICU intervals were screened from late 2019 through late 2021. The flow removed age <18, recent protamine, recent subcutaneous UFH/LMWH, recent DOAC or antithrombin exposure, intervals outside 6-48 hours, missing anti-Xa, missing UFH data, and remaining missing predictors.

The study followed TRIPOD reporting guidance and received institutional review board approval with consent waived.

Methods - outcome and features

The target was a three-class anti-Xa category. Anti-Xa was measured with the STA Liquid Anti-Xa chromogenic assay. Sixteen features were drawn from routine ICU data, including age, sex, BMI, ECMO, urine output, creatinine, RRT, fibrinogen, hemoglobin, hematocrit, bilirubin, UFH rates/boluses, prior anti-Xa, and interval duration.

Urine output and boluses were summed over intervals; other continuous variables were averaged. Missing laboratory results were linearly interpolated, continuous variables normalized, categorical variables one-hot encoded, and skewed variables log-transformed and normalized.

Methods - modeling and validation

Six classifier families were evaluated. Hyperparameters were explored using Latin hypercube sampling and 10-fold stratified cross-validation, with AUROC guiding selection. Seventy percent of intervals were used for training and 30% for testing. Class weights were available for penalized logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost to address class imbalance.

Performance metrics included multiclass AUROC (Hand-Till), macro-averaged AUPRC, accuracy, F1, sensitivity, specificity, and calibration plots. The authors state that no calibration method was applied.

Results - cohort

The final data set contained 3,790 intervals from 211 patients and 286 ICU stays. Patients were high acuity: median age 66 years, median BMI 27.6 kg/m², admission SOFA 11, 92% mechanically ventilated during the ICU stay, and 24% treated with ECMO. Median ICU length of stay among included stays was 16.3 days.

Training contained 2,652 intervals and testing 1,138. The authors report no statistically significant differences in the displayed train/test features, but the stay counts show extensive overlap between the two partitions.

Results - cross-validation and test performance

In cross-validation, XGBoost and penalized logistic regression had the highest mean AUROC at 0.79. Penalized logistic regression, random forest, and neural network each had mean AUPRC around 0.57. On the test set, random forest and XGBoost both reached AUROC 0.80, while random forest had the best AUPRC at 0.61.

Neural network produced the highest reported accuracy (0.74) and F1 (0.76). The discussion nevertheless centers random forest because of its broader discrimination/precision-recall profile. Numeric class-specific metrics are deferred to supplements not contained in this PDF.

Results - calibration, feature importance, and secondary analyses

Calibration was described as good for infra-therapeutic and normal one-vs-rest predictions but low for supra-therapeutic predictions. Current UFH rate, prior UFH rate, and prior anti-Xa were the most important features in models with available importance measures. Forty-nine percent of all anti-Xa results and 54% of first results were below 0.3.

Random forest without prior anti-Xa still achieved AUROC 0.77 and AUPRC 0.59. Excluding ECMO patients yielded AUROC 0.80 and AUPRC 0.61. These analyses suggest that headline discrimination is not entirely dependent on prior anti-Xa or ECMO patients, but they remain internal analyses of the same center.

Discussion and limitations

The authors argue that a machine-learning system could help select UFH doses likely to reach target anti-Xa more quickly. They discuss heparin resistance, the role of antithrombin in ECMO, prior aPTT-focused ML work, and possible value of reinforcement learning or PK/PD approaches.

Reported limitations include single-center design, small sample size, retrospective data, lack of thrombotic/hemorrhagic outcomes, dependence of the primary models on a prior anti-Xa value, and poor performance in the rare supra-therapeutic class. A key additional appraisal limitation is that interval-level splitting allowed the same ICU stays to contribute observations to both training and testing.

Conclusion and disclosures

The authors conclude that the models showed good predictive performance but require prospective multicenter validation before clinical integration. Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

The authors reported no financial support and no potential conflicts of interest.

Evaluation and appraisal

Overall: useful as a concept and variable-selection paper for anti-Xa-guided UFH CDS, but not strong enough to support dosing decisions or a production model. The interval-level validation split is the dominant methodological concern.
9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses UFH anti-Xa monitoring in critically ill patients and the feasibility of data-driven dosing support.

Evidence basis: ICU UFH population; anti-Xa target; routinely collected predictors.

Principal limiting factor: It evaluates prediction, not patient outcomes or a deployed dosing intervention.

What would change the score: Prospective evaluation showing improved time-in-range, bleeding, thrombosis, or workflow outcomes would raise the score.

7/10

Novelty

Rationale: The authors position this as the first ICU prediction study using anti-Xa rather than aPTT as the modeled outcome.

Evidence basis: Six model families applied to anti-Xa range classification with ECMO/RRT/inflammation-related features.

Principal limiting factor: The modeling approaches are established and the study is an incremental application rather than a new algorithmic method.

What would change the score: External replication or a validated sequential dosing policy would raise novelty; prior equivalent anti-Xa models would lower it.

4/10

Method strength

Rationale: The preprocessing and model-comparison plan is described, but the internal validation design has a major repeated-measures independence problem.

Evidence basis: 3,790 intervals from 211 patients; 70/30 interval split; 10-fold stratified CV; multiple models; class weighting.

Principal limiting factor: 279 stays are represented in training and 224 in testing despite only 286 unique stays, requiring at least 217 stays to occur in both sets. This creates substantial within-stay information leakage/optimism risk.

What would change the score: Patient- or stay-level splitting, nested validation, prespecified handling of missingness, and external prospective testing would raise the score.

4/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Discrimination is moderate, but the reported test performance is not clearly independent and clinical outcomes were unavailable.

Evidence basis: Best test AUROC 0.80 and AUPRC 0.61; class-specific calibration described; ablation and no-ECMO analyses reported.

Principal limiting factor: No thrombotic/hemorrhagic outcomes, poor supra-therapeutic performance, and potential split leakage weaken confidence that metrics reflect deployable performance.

What would change the score: Independent external validation with calibration metrics and clinical utility analysis would raise the score.

3/10

External validity

Rationale: The source itself states that single-center results cannot be generalized without validation.

Evidence basis: One tertiary teaching hospital; medical, surgical, and cardiac surgery ICUs.

Principal limiting factor: No multicenter, temporal-external, or geographic validation.

What would change the score: A prospective multicenter cohort with prespecified transportability assessment would raise the score.

4/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The feature set is clinically recognizable, but the paper does not yet supply a validated dosing action or deployment-ready decision rule.

Evidence basis: Current/prior UFH rate and prior anti-Xa dominate prediction; models were built from routinely collected ICU data.

Principal limiting factor: The emphasized model depends on prior anti-Xa, the initial-dose problem remains unresolved, supplemental hyperparameter details are absent from the retrieved PDF, and no prospective CDS evaluation was performed.

What would change the score: A locked model, reproducible code/specification, patient-level external validation, dose-selection policy, calibration threshold, and workflow trial would raise the score.

Strengths

  • Clinically relevant ICU UFH/anti-Xa target rather than aPTT.
  • Uses multiple model families and a common internal comparison framework.
  • Includes clinically plausible renal, inflammatory, volume, ECMO, and dosing features.
  • Reports both ROC- and precision-recall-oriented metrics and examines performance without prior anti-Xa and without ECMO.
  • Authors explicitly avoid claiming readiness for practice and call for prospective multicenter validation.

Critical issues

  • Non-independent test set: interval-level randomization allows repeated intervals from the same ICU stays in both training and test partitions.
  • No external validation: single center only.
  • No clinical utility endpoint: no bleeding, thrombosis, mortality, time-in-range, or comparative dosing outcome.
  • Weakest class is clinically important: supra-therapeutic prediction is limited despite the safety relevance of overdose.
  • Incomplete reproducibility from retrieved PDF: hyperparameter details and several detailed metrics are in unavailable supplements.
  • Initial dosing remains unresolved: the emphasized model uses prior anti-Xa, although an ablated model retained moderate performance.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use now

Use as a hypothesis-generating and design-reference paper for which routinely available variables may matter when modeling UFH anti-Xa response in the ICU.

Do not use now

Do not use the reported random-forest metrics as justification for bedside dose recommendations, autonomous titration, or replacing an institutional UFH nomogram.

For a CDS build

Require patient- or stay-level splitting; external validation; transparent model specification; explicit calibration assessment; class-specific safety thresholds; and evaluation against a standard dosing workflow.

Research priority

The most valuable next study would be prospective, multicenter, and workflow-integrated, with dose decisions and patient-centered safety/effectiveness outcomes rather than anti-Xa classification alone.

Best reusable insight: current UFH rate, prior UFH rate, and prior anti-Xa carry most of the predictive signal, but useful discrimination persisted after removing prior anti-Xa. That makes the paper more useful for feature-set design than its headline test-set AUROC alone would suggest.
