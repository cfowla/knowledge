One-page synthesis

Core thesis: PPHN management is most effective when treatment is matched to physiology—optimize lung recruitment and oxygenation without hyperoxia, use echocardiography to define RV/LV/shunt physiology, reduce PVR with iNO when indicated, support ventricular/systemic hemodynamics, and escalate early when refractory.

The source is strong as a practical pathophysiology-to-management review. Its most reusable elements are the echo-centered diagnostic framework, the explicit rejection of historical hyperventilation/alkalosis strategies, oxygen-target caution, iNO initiation/weaning heuristics, and Figure 11 hemodynamic algorithm. It also clearly distinguishes investigational therapies from established approaches.

Confidence is limited by narrative-review methodology and heterogeneous evidence provenance. Several claims are secondary reports of cited trials or animal experiments rather than data generated by this article. The source contains manuscript-production issues and a clinically important internal dose-range variation: Figure 11 and Table 3 do not give identical ranges for epinephrine, norepinephrine, and vasopressin. The appraisal preserves both rather than normalizing them.

Introduction & classification

PPHN is framed as failure of normal circulatory transition with sustained high PVR and hypoxemia. The review reports prevalence near 2/1,000 live births and mortality 5–10%, with most affected infants term or near-term. Etiologies include MAS, pneumonia/sepsis, RDS, HIE/asphyxia, CDH, pPROM/lung hypoplasia and congenital heart disease.

Classification is mostly etiologic: secondary PPHN dominates (Table 1, 80–90%), while idiopathic disease is about 10–20%. The review emphasizes that acute vasoconstriction and chronic prenatal remodeling may coexist, making physiology more useful than a single etiologic label.

Pathophysiology

High PVR reduces pulmonary blood flow and oxygenated pulmonary venous return. Hypoxemia and acidosis further increase PVR; persistent RV pressure load can lead to RV dilation/failure, while ventricular interdependence can compromise LV filling/output and systemic perfusion. Ductal and atrial right-to-left shunting amplifies hypoxemia when PVR exceeds SVR.

Mechanistically, the review organizes abnormal pulmonary vascular tone/remodeling around NO–cGMP, prostacyclin–cAMP, endothelin and oxidant-stress pathways. Several mechanistic claims are drawn from animal models and cellular work; those data provide biological plausibility but should not be read as direct neonatal comparative effectiveness evidence.

Diagnosis & echocardiography

Initial evaluation includes pre/postductal saturation, blood gas, chest radiograph and echocardiography. A saturation differential >5–10% or PaO2 differential 10–20 mmHg supports extrapulmonary shunting, although absence of a differential does not exclude PPHN. Hyperoxia testing is described as rarely needed in the modern echo era.

Echocardiography is the central diagnostic and management tool: it estimates pulmonary pressure, defines PDA/PFO shunt direction, evaluates septal geometry and RV/LV function, and assesses preload. The authors favor serial functional echo in moderate/severe unstable disease. Figure 5 provides a TR example with Vmax 4 m/s corresponding to PASP ~70–75 mmHg.

General supportive management

The review emphasizes temperature control, correction of glucose/electrolyte/acid-base abnormalities, nutrition, treatment of sepsis/pneumonia when suspected, adequate oxygen-carrying capacity, and reduction of stress. It discourages routine paralysis and the older hyperventilation/alkalosis strategy because of adverse neurologic/hearing associations and greater ECMO/oxygen use in cited observational evidence.

For acid-base management, the authors recommend pH >7.25 and preferably 7.30–7.40 during acute disease. This is not a trial-derived target; the rationale incorporates animal physiology and clinical experience.

Oxygen, ventilation & surfactant

The source advocates normoxemia rather than hyperoxemia. Translational work suggests PVR rises when PaO2 falls below ~45–50 mmHg and does not continue falling once PaO2 exceeds ~80 mmHg. Figure 7 supports a mid-90s preductal SpO2 physiologic range in lamb models. The key limitation is explicit: randomized infant trials comparing PaO2 targets were not available.

Ventilation aims for lung recruitment without overdistension—approximately 8–9 posterior ribs on inspiratory radiograph, appropriate PEEP, low pressures/volumes and permissive hypercapnia. In parenchymal disease, cited studies favor HFV+iNO and early surfactant/recruitment. A cited randomized trial summarized in Figure 9 reports less hypoxemia progression and lower death/ECMO with surfactant+iNO than iNO alone.

Inhaled nitric oxide

iNO is presented as first-line selective pulmonary vasodilator therapy in ventilated PPHN, with a typical starting dose of 20 ppm. The review uses a “20-20-20” initiation heuristic: OI around 20, iNO 20 ppm, and response defined by ≥20 mmHg improvement in PaO2/FiO2. Methemoglobin monitoring is suggested at 2 h, 8 h, then daily.

Weaning is described by a “30-60-90” heuristic: after a good response, begin at about 30 min if FiO2 <60% and continue only if PaO2 ≥60 mmHg or preductal SpO2 >90%. The authors’ local practice is 5-ppm decrements every 4 h to 5 ppm, then 1-ppm decrements every 2–4 h. Regulatory language in this 2021 review is historical and should be checked against current labeling before practice use.

Second-line pulmonary vasodilators & ventricular support

Sildenafil is discussed for inadequate iNO response or lack of iNO availability; IV use is attractive in critical illness but can cause hypotension. Milrinone is considered when ventricular dysfunction contributes to poor oxygenation or iNO response; the authors avoid a loading dose because of hypotension and consider a 10 mL/kg preload bolus if echo suggests low filling. Prostacyclin analogs are alternative pulmonary vasodilators, with systemic hypotension a concern for IV therapy.

PGE1 may be useful in severe RV failure with a constricting ductus by restoring a ductal “pop-off,” and the authors explicitly make this echo-guided. These suggestions combine physiology, cited secondary evidence and expert practice; they are not presented with formal evidence grades.

Systemic hemodynamics & ECMO

Hypotension is treated as a high-priority contributor to worsening PPHN. The authors give ~50 mmHg as an example systemic pressure in a term infant, favor echo-guided selection/weaning of vasoactives, and warn against very high doses. Figure 11 provides a detailed escalation algorithm.

ECMO discussion/cannulation is recommended early for iNO nonresponders who deteriorate despite conventional therapy, especially with cardiac dysfunction and hemodynamic instability. The source does not provide a validated prospective threshold algorithm for all patients; this is a practice framework.

Conclusion

The central clinical thesis is a shift from indiscriminate hyperoxygenation/hyperventilation/alkalosis toward physiology-based care: gentle recruitment, permissive hypercapnia, early echo, early iNO and surfactant when indicated, avoidance of hypoxia and hyperoxia, and cardiocentric support of RV/LV function.

Investigational approaches—including antioxidants, soluble guanylate cyclase activators and Rho-kinase inhibitors—are explicitly described as research-only. The review is clinically actionable as a framework, but it should not substitute for current guidelines, labeling or local neonatal protocols.
