One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In high-risk adults with type 2 diabetes, an intensive glucose-control strategy that achieved an average HbA1c of about 6.5% reduced the combined major macrovascular/microvascular outcome by 10% relative to standard control, but the benefit was driven by fewer renal microvascular events rather than a demonstrated macrovascular benefit. Severe hypoglycemia and hospitalization increased.

ADVANCE randomized 5,571 participants to intensive control and 5,569 to standard control. During follow-up, mean HbA1c was 6.5% versus 7.3%, with a time-weighted between-group separation of 0.67 percentage points. The combined primary outcome occurred in 18.1% versus 20.0% (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82-0.98; P=0.01), corresponding to an article-reported 5-year NNT of 52 (95% CI 30-213). Major microvascular events fell from 10.9% to 9.4% (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77-0.97), especially new/worsening nephropathy (5.2% to 4.1%; HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66-0.93). Major macrovascular events were not significantly reduced (10.6% to 10.0%; HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.84-1.06).

All-cause mortality did not differ significantly (9.6% vs 8.9%; HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.83-1.06). Severe hypoglycemia was uncommon but increased (1.5% to 2.7%; HR 1.86, 95% CI 1.42-2.40), and hospitalization rose modestly (42.8% to 44.9%; HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.01-1.13). The trial's strongest inference is therefore about renal microvascular prevention under this treatment strategy, not macrovascular or mortality reduction.

Section condensation

Background and objective

The article frames uncertainty about whether tighter glycemic control reduces vascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes despite observational associations between glycemia and vascular risk. ADVANCE tested a glucose-lowering strategy targeting HbA1c ≤6.5% in a broad, high-risk type 2 diabetes population.

Participants and randomization

Eligible participants were ≥55 years old, had type 2 diabetes diagnosed at age ≥30, and had prior major macrovascular/microvascular disease or at least one additional vascular risk factor. There was no HbA1c entry threshold. After a 6-week run-in, 11,140 participants were centrally randomized in a factorial design; glucose allocation was stratified by study center and vascular-disease history among other factors.

Baseline mean age was 66 years, 42% were women, mean diabetes duration was about 8 years, and about 32% had prior major macrovascular disease. Recruitment spanned Asia, Australasia, Europe, and North America, supporting geographic breadth while still representing an older, higher-risk population.

Intervention and comparator

Intensive control used gliclazide modified release 30-120 mg daily and other agents as required to pursue HbA1c ≤6.5%; other sulfonylureas were stopped. Standard control followed local guideline targets and did not use gliclazide MR as the study drug. Intensive participants had substantially more study contact (about 31 visits vs 11).

The supplement shows the suggested escalation pathway: add/increase gliclazide MR, then metformin, thiazolidinedione, alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, bedtime insulin, and ultimately a full insulin regimen as needed. At the final visit, insulin use was 40.5% versus 24.1%, illustrating that the comparison was a multicomponent treatment strategy rather than a gliclazide-only drug trial.

Outcomes and measurement

Primary outcomes were major macrovascular events (cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke) and major microvascular events (new/worsening nephropathy or retinopathy), assessed jointly and separately. Renal definitions included macroalbuminuria, creatinine doubling to ≥200 μmol/L, renal replacement therapy, or renal death. Retinopathy included proliferative retinopathy, macular edema, diabetes-related blindness, or retinal photocoagulation.

Suspected primary outcomes and deaths were adjudicated by an independent committee unaware of treatment assignment. HbA1c assays across participating laboratories were standardized using WEQAS samples and laboratory-specific regression against central reference values, as detailed in the supplement.

Statistics

Analyses were intention-to-treat using unadjusted Cox proportional-hazards models. Because event rates were lower than expected, investigators blinded to treatment-effect results prespecified a joint primary analysis and extended glucose follow-up by 18 months. The paper states that no adjustment for multiple statistical testing was made; subgroup heterogeneity analyses were not prespecified.

Results

Glycemic separation and treatment intensity

End-of-follow-up mean HbA1c was 6.5% in the intensive arm and 7.3% in standard care; the time-weighted difference was 0.67 percentage points (95% CI 0.64-0.70). Fasting glucose was lower by 1.22 mmol/L (95% CI 1.15-1.28). At the final visit, 90% of intensive-arm attendees still received gliclazide MR, and insulin use was about 41% versus 24% in the supplement.

Renal components

New-onset microalbuminuria was reduced (23.7% vs 25.7%; HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85-0.98; P=0.02), and macroalbuminuria showed a larger relative reduction (2.9% vs 4.1%; HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.57-0.85; P<0.001). Renal-replacement therapy or renal death trended lower (0.4% vs 0.6%; HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.38-1.08; P=0.09), whereas doubling of serum creatinine was not reduced (1.2% vs 1.1%; HR 1.15, 95% CI 0.82-1.63; P=0.42).

Mortality and safety

All-cause mortality was 8.9% versus 9.6% (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.83-1.06; P=0.28), and cardiovascular mortality was not significantly reduced (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.74-1.04; P=0.12). Severe hypoglycemia occurred in 150 versus 81 participants (2.7% vs 1.5%; HR 1.86, 95% CI 1.42-2.40; P<0.001). Hospitalization was also higher (44.9% vs 42.8%; HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.01-1.13; P=0.03).

Subgroups and interpretation

The combined vascular outcome showed no statistically significant heterogeneity across the displayed baseline subgroups (all heterogeneity P≥0.10). The authors emphasized that the overall benefit was predominantly renal and that the trial did not establish a macrovascular benefit. They also noted that the macrovascular event rate was lower than anticipated, reducing power to detect a plausible moderate effect.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first (historical landmark trial). Best used to understand the evidence boundary around intensive glucose lowering in high-risk type 2 diabetes: renal microvascular benefit with no proven macrovascular or mortality reduction and increased hypoglycemia/treatment burden.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses clinically important vascular outcomes of glycemic intensity. Evidence: large RCT with patient-relevant endpoints. Limit: drug mix and standards are from 2001-2008. Would rise: current-practice replication with contemporary agents.

Novelty
9/10

At publication, it provided one of the largest randomized tests of near-normal glycemic targets in type 2 diabetes. Limit: novelty is historical rather than current.

Method strength
9/10

Randomized factorial design, large sample, multinational recruitment, blinded endpoint adjudication, ITT analysis. Limit: open treatment strategy, unequal visit intensity, protocol changes for power, and no multiplicity adjustment.

Evidence strength
8/10

The renal microvascular signal is statistically supported and internally coherent. Limit: combined primary benefit is driven mainly by nephropathy; macrovascular and mortality estimates are inconclusive, and multiple testing complicates secondary findings.

External validity
8/10

Broad 20-country, 215-center enrollment improves transportability. Limit: participants were older and vascular-risk enriched, and the regimen predates modern glucose-lowering therapies.

Implementation value
7/10

The supplement provides a reproducible treatment algorithm and monitoring details. Limit: the specific escalation sequence is not a contemporary treatment standard; implementation value is mainly conceptual/historical unless externally updated.

Key strengths

Large randomized sample, excellent baseline balance, clinically meaningful vascular endpoints, blinded adjudication, prolonged follow-up, multinational enrollment, explicit treatment algorithm, and detailed supplement. The achieved HbA1c separation was sustained and the main renal signal appeared across related renal outcomes.

Key limitations and interpretation boundaries

The trial cannot be read as evidence that intensive glucose control reduced cardiovascular events or mortality. The macrovascular event rate was lower than projected, limiting power. The intensive strategy also involved more visits, more home monitoring, more multidrug therapy, more insulin, modestly lower blood pressure, and higher severe hypoglycemia, so the causal contrast is a strategy/intensity package rather than an isolated HbA1c target or single drug effect.

Retinopathy event rates were low, limiting power for eye outcomes. Subgroup analyses were not prespecified. The absence of multiplicity adjustment warrants caution with the many secondary comparisons. Funding from Servier, manufacturer of gliclazide MR, is relevant to appraisal but does not negate the randomized design or endpoint adjudication.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use this trial for

Understanding that lower HbA1c under the ADVANCE strategy produced a measurable renal microvascular benefit while leaving macrovascular and mortality benefit unproven.

Do not use it for

Claiming that an HbA1c target of 6.5% universally improves cardiovascular survival, or that gliclazide MR alone caused the observed benefit.

Safety signal

More intensive treatment increased severe hypoglycemia and modestly increased hospitalization; treatment burden was substantially higher.

Clinical translation boundary

Any present-day treatment recommendation requires separate verification against current guidelines and evidence for contemporary therapies; that verification is outside this source-only appraisal.
