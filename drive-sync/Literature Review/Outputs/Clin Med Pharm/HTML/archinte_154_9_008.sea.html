One-page synthesis

Core thesis: A standardized, bleeding-risk-stratified UFH protocol produced faster achievement of therapeutic APTT than nonprotocol physician management, largely by using higher initial infusion rates in low-bleeding-risk patients, earlier APTT checks, and mandatory dose escalation for subtherapeutic results.

The protocol cohort received a 5000-U IV bolus and either 1680 U/h (no identifiable bleeding risk) or 1240 U/h (high bleeding risk), followed by explicit APTT-based titration. Nonprotocol patients were managed by physicians without the study nomogram. Kaplan-Meier analysis favored the protocol for time to therapeutic APTT (P=.025). Initial therapeutic APTT occurred in 75% vs 60%, and by 24 hours at least one APTT >55 seconds had occurred in 95% vs 79%. Among low-risk protocol patients receiving 1680 U/h, 10/11 (91%) had an initially therapeutic APTT.

Process differences were substantial: protocol patients had their first APTT earlier (4.6 vs 6.3 h) and had four measurements in the first 24 hours versus 2.5 per patient without the protocol. Nonprotocol care produced 68 subtherapeutic APTT instances in 24 hours, including 11 with no response; subsequent APTT remained subtherapeutic after nonresponse. Major bleeding was 5% vs 4%, but the study was too small to establish safety equivalence, and recurrent VTE was too sparse to show clinical benefit. The evidence therefore supports the historical value of protocolization more strongly than it supports the exact dose schedule or a patient-centered outcome benefit.

Section condensation

Background and objective

The paper frames delayed therapeutic APTT as a recurrent problem in UFH treatment of VTE and attributes delay to variable heparin kinetics plus practice-process failures: low initial infusion rates, delayed first APTT measurement, and inadequate dose increases after low APTT results. The authors hypothesized that a standardized protocol with a higher initial infusion for patients without identifiable bleeding risk would shorten time to therapeutic APTT without increasing major bleeding.

Patients and methods

The source population was 109 consecutive patients with acute symptomatic proximal DVT diagnosed by duplex noncompressibility or ascending venography between January 1990 and April 1991. The analyzed UFH cohorts were 20 protocol patients from a concurrent LMWH trial and 48 patients excluded from that trial for specified reasons who received physician-managed UFH. An additional 23 patients were excluded from the latter cohort for conditions that could alter heparin management or effect.

Protocol patients were stratified by bleeding risk. All received 5000 U IV; initial infusion was 1680 U/h without identifiable bleeding risk and 1240 U/h with bleeding risk. APTT was checked within 4-6 hours and titrated by Table 1. The primary outcome was time to first APTT >55 seconds (>1.5× upper limit of normal), analyzed with Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox's F test. Patients were followed 3 months for recurrent VTE, bleeding, and death; these clinical outcomes were interpreted by physicians blinded to treatment method.

Results

Baseline characteristics were reported as not significantly different. Protocol delivery differed materially from nonprotocol care: mean initial infusion 1482±50 vs 1041±21 U/h (P<.001), total first-24-hour dose 36,538±1,400 vs 32,004±1,242 U (P=.03), earlier first APTT, and more intensive first-day monitoring. Warfarin initiation also differed: all protocol patients started between 24-48 hours, versus 9/48 nonprotocol patients within 48 hours (P<.001).

The protocol reached therapeutic APTT faster by time-to-event analysis (P=.025). Initial therapeutic APTT was 75% vs 60%; among low-risk protocol patients receiving 1680 U/h it was 91%. At 24 hours, 95% vs 79% had at least one APTT >55 seconds. Major bleeding was 1/20 vs 2/48; minor bleeding 1/20 vs 1/48; recurrent VTE 0/20 vs 2/48; deaths 3/20 vs 1/48. The clinical-event counts were too small for firm comparative conclusions.

Comment

The authors interpret the benefit as a bundled effect of higher initial dosing for low-bleeding-risk patients, prompt APTT measurement, graded responses to low APTT, and frequent early monitoring. They emphasize that nonprotocol physicians sometimes failed to increase heparin after subtherapeutic APTT and that such nonresponses were followed by persistent subtherapeutic results.

They also acknowledge potential selection/contamination bias from concurrent cohorts and insufficient power for bleeding outcomes. They note that four APTTs in the first 24 hours may be impractical or unnecessary in patients whose first measurement is already therapeutic.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin-protocol deep dive; do not use as a current dosing standard.
Best use: historical evidence for why protocolized UFH dosing/monitoring can outperform discretionary management and for identifying process failure modes.
Do not use for: direct adoption of the 1240/1680-U/h dosing thresholds or claims of modern safety/clinical superiority.

Clinical validity

Strengths: objective DVT diagnosis; prospective cohort identification; explicit protocol and endpoints; time-to-event analysis for the primary outcome; blinded interpretation of recurrent VTE/bleeding/death; granular process measures showing where nonprotocol care failed.
Major limitations: nonrandomized cohort construction; small sample; surrogate primary endpoint; one-tailed tests in Table 4; different warfarin timing; clinical outcomes underpowered; single-center historical APTT assay/threshold.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Protocolization
The strongest evidence in this paper is about reliable process execution: adequate starting dose, early measurement, mandatory response, and repeat monitoring.
Safety boundary
The observed 5% vs 4% major-bleeding rates do not establish safety equivalence; the study explicitly lacked power to rule out a clinically relevant difference.
Monitoring design
The paper separates first-day monitoring intensity from total monitoring burden: protocol patients had more early APTTs but fewer total APTTs across the course.
Modern reuse
Reuse the workflow logic and failure-mode analysis, not the historical dose/APTT thresholds, unless those thresholds are independently validated for the current assay and population.
