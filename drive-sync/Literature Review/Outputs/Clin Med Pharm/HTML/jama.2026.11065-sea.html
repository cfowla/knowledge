One-page synthesis

Core result: TIVA did not improve the primary outcome. Mean days alive and at home at 30 days were 22.5 with TIVA and 22.4 with inhalational anesthesia; adjusted IRR 1.00 (95% CI 0.99–1.02; P=.68). The source-defined minimal clinically important difference was 3 days.

VITAL randomized 2,508 adults aged 50 years or older undergoing elective major noncardiac surgery to propofol-based total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) or volatile inhalational anesthesia. Treatment adherence was high (95.8%). Major secondary outcomes—days alive and at home at 90 days, mortality at 30 days, 90 days and 6 months, day-3 QoR-15, delirium, and overall postoperative complications—also did not show statistically significant between-group differences.

Exploratory day-1 patient-reported symptoms favored TIVA for thirst (73.8% vs 78.2%), hoarseness (47.6% vs 54.2%), and nausea/vomiting (26.8% vs 36.1%). These secondary comparisons were not multiplicity-adjusted and should be treated as exploratory. Two certain/probable awareness cases occurred, both in the TIVA arm; the study explicitly notes that event rarity prevents definitive comparative conclusions.

Interpretive boundary: the primary trial was a superiority analysis, not a formal equivalence trial. Its narrow DAH30 effect estimate supports no clinically important average superiority of TIVA for that outcome, but “no significant difference” should not be generalized into formal equivalence across every clinical and safety endpoint. The supplement also shows the 90-day mortality noninferiority objective was underpowered because mortality was much lower than anticipated.

Methods

Question and population

The trial asked whether propofol-based TIVA improves patient-centered recovery and major outcomes compared with volatile inhalational maintenance anesthesia. Eligible participants were at least 50 years old, undergoing elective major noncardiac surgery under general anesthesia; contraindication to either technique and expected survival under 30 days were key exclusions.

The cohort was clinically broad within elective surgery but not evenly distributed across surgical contexts: 75.4% underwent cancer surgery, 72.1% intra-abdominal surgery, 98.1% had surgery expected to last ≥2 hours, and approximately 80% were classified as well (Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale 1–3).

Randomization and anesthetic strategies

Randomization was 1:1 on the day of surgery with computerized minimization by surgical specialty, expected duration, cancer status, and frailty. Clinicians were unblinded; patients were not explicitly told allocation. The randomized intervention concerned the intended primary maintenance mode—propofol infusion for TIVA versus a volatile inhalational agent—with other perioperative management left to clinicians.

Adherence was 95.8%. Depth-of-anesthesia monitoring differed substantially (90.6% TIVA vs 49.5% inhalational), whereas regional anesthesia was used similarly and very frequently (78.2% vs 78.3%). These are important cointervention/context variables for interpretation.

Outcomes and statistics

The primary endpoint was days alive and at home at 30 days (DAH30), with death within 30 days scored as zero. The reported minimal clinically important difference was 3 days. Secondary outcomes included DAH90, mortality, QoR-15, Bauer satisfaction/discomfort items, day-3 delirium by 4AT, awareness, and postoperative complications.

The primary analysis used zero-inflated negative binomial regression adjusted for minimization variables. The planned sample of 2,500 provided 90% power to detect a 1-day DAH30 difference under the source assumptions. Secondary analyses were not multiplicity-adjusted. DAH90 used negative binomial regression after the zero-inflated model failed to converge. Per-protocol and prespecified subgroup analyses were also performed.

Table 1 — baseline characteristics

Arms were well balanced for age, sex, ethnicity, surgical specialty, expected duration, cancer surgery, procedural mode, frailty, and baseline EQ-5D. The cohort was dominated by intra-abdominal, cancer, ≥2-hour, and relatively fit elective cases.

Table 2 — intraoperative management

The randomized maintenance modes were strongly separated: propofol infusion was used in 96.3% of TIVA vs 3.0% of inhalational cases; inhalational anesthetic in 2.2% vs 95.7%. Opioid infusion and depth-monitoring use differed between arms, while regional anesthesia use and anesthesia duration were similar.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: high-quality comparative-effectiveness evidence for anesthetic maintenance choice in adults ≥50 undergoing elective major noncardiac surgery. Do not use it as proof that TIVA and volatile anesthesia are formally equivalent for every endpoint or rare adverse event.

Main strengths

Random allocation, a clinically meaningful patient-centered primary endpoint, large multicenter enrollment, strong treatment separation/adherence, substantial follow-up, transparent sensitivity analysis, and detailed protocol/SAP/supplemental reporting.

Main limitations and inference boundaries

Formal equivalence: The discussion uses “clinical equivalence,” but the primary DAH30 analysis was designed for superiority. A null superiority result is not automatically a formal equivalence conclusion. The narrow primary-outcome CI is reassuring for DAH30, but this logic cannot be indiscriminately extended to mortality, awareness, or other rare events.
  • Open-label design can influence subjective outcomes.
  • Secondary comparisons were not multiplicity-adjusted.
  • 90-day mortality was too uncommon to preserve the planned noninferiority power.
  • Two awareness events occurred in TIVA; event rarity prevents causal/comparative inference.
  • Elective UK NHS population and high regional-anesthesia use constrain transferability.
  • No long-term oncologic endpoint and no cost-effectiveness result.
  • Appraisal inference: SAP Version 1.0 is dated December 11, 2024, after the article-reported final follow-up in October 2024. This weakens the strongest possible claim that all final analytical details were locked before outcome collection ended, but the supplied sources do not show that analyses were data-driven or altered in response to results.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What this can changeFor similar elective major noncardiac cases, there is no evidence that choosing propofol TIVA instead of volatile anesthesia improves DAH30 or major longer-horizon recovery outcomes. Technique choice can therefore incorporate other patient, workflow, environmental, drug-supply, monitoring, and institutional considerations without claiming a major recovery advantage from TIVA.
Potential patient-experience advantageTIVA had lower reported thirst, hoarseness, and nausea/vomiting on day 1, but these are exploratory findings and should not be promoted as definitive comparative benefits without multiplicity-aware confirmation.
SafetyOverall complication and delirium rates were similar. Rare outcomes remain unresolved: awareness and mortality comparisons are not adequately precise to prove safety equivalence.
Do not extrapolateEmergency surgery, highly frail/cognitively impaired populations, non-UK systems with different regional-anesthesia practices, long-term cancer survival, and cost-effectiveness.
