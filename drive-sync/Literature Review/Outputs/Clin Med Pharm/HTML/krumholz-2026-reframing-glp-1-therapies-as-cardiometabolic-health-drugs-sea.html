One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Krumholz argues that GLP-1 receptor agonists have outgrown a weight-loss-only frame and should increasingly be considered cardiometabolic health drugs, with success judged by cardiovascular and broader health outcomes rather than pounds lost.

The editorial starts from a cardiovascular prevention problem: U.S. cardiovascular mortality improvements have plateaued despite sharply rising spending, while obesity and cardiometabolic disease have expanded. It uses the contemporaneous SURMOUNT-1 post hoc biomarker analysis as an example of tirzepatide affecting several cardiovascular-risk pathways, then broadens to cited cardiovascular-outcomes and mortality evidence. Importantly, it does not present the biomarker study as an outcomes trial; it notes that most biomarker changes tracked with weight and waist reduction and that some mortality signals remain unexplained.

The main value is not a new effect estimate but a change in clinical frame. Krumholz argues that a weight-centered narrative narrows the field's attention, reinforces stigma, and can delay appropriate conversations. He proposes outcome-centered, stigma-sensitive shared decision-making and a research agenda that tests whether cardiometabolic benefit can be preserved with lower-dose or reduced-frequency exposure. That latter idea is explicitly an evidence gap, not a demonstrated dosing strategy.

Section condensation

Opening framing

Krumholz describes cardiovascular disease as a persistent leading cause of death and disability and characterizes recent U.S. progress as a plateau. He cites a 34.7% fall in age-adjusted death rates from 2000 to 2011 followed by little further improvement through 2022, apart from the pandemic spike, while direct cardiovascular spending increased from roughly $70 billion to $220 billion.

He offers rising obesity and cardiometabolic disease as a likely major reason progress has stalled. This is an editorial causal hypothesis rather than an analysis performed in this paper. From that premise, he introduces the central reframing: GLP-1 receptor agonists should be viewed as cardiometabolic health drugs, not merely medications for excess weight.

Cardiometabolic Drugs

The editorial highlights a post hoc SURMOUNT-1 biomarker analysis in people with obesity without diabetes treated with tirzepatide for 72 weeks. Krumholz summarizes improvement across inflammatory, metabolic/adiposity, endothelial, and thrombotic pathways; he specifically notes that high-sensitivity C-reactive protein fell by roughly one-half at the highest dose, with parallel interleukin-6 reductions and changes in insulin resistance, leptin, hepatic enzymes, adiponectin, endothelial function, and thrombotic risk.

He also states the necessary limit: this was exploratory biomarker evidence, not an outcomes analysis, and most changes correlated with weight and waist reduction. He then points to cited clinical-outcome evidence suggesting reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality and to a meta-analytic signal for noncardiovascular mortality. He cautions that the noncardiovascular signal is not fully understood and was partly related to fewer infectious deaths during the pandemic. His conclusion is that outcomes, not the number of pounds lost, should be the meaningful competitive endpoint.

What Should We Call Them

Krumholz argues that disease-based drug-class names shape clinical thinking. GLP-1 therapies moved from being described as glucose-lowering drugs to antiobesity drugs; he proposes that “cardiometabolic health drugs” now better represents the underlying biology being treated, with weight loss as one manifestation rather than the entire purpose.

He does not claim cardiometabolic disease is the only domain of benefit. The article points to benefits beyond cardiology, including sleep apnea and joint-related conditions, while acknowledging disappointments such as recent Alzheimer disease trials. The implication is organizational as much as semantic: cardiology should treat obesity and cardiometabolic disease as part of its own core responsibility.

The Barrier We Must Name

The editorial centers weight stigma as an implementation barrier. It notes that obesity is unusually stigmatized, that this can affect clinical encounters, and that only about 1 in 5 primary-care visits involving patients with obesity includes weight-related advice. It further notes declining counseling despite improved guidelines and substantial gaps in clinician-patient discussion of weight.

Krumholz argues that avoiding obesity conversations is not a neutral response to stigma. His preferred approach is to preserve patient dignity while directly treating cardiometabolic disease, continuing lifestyle, nutrition, and physical-activity support and offering medication as another option. This is a communication and care-framing recommendation, not evidence that one specific conversation strategy has been proven superior.

The Work Ahead

The article frames language as an implementation problem that is nearly as important as evidence generation. Krumholz calls for language that makes patients aware of cardiometabolic treatment options without reproducing weight stigma, and he emphasizes informed, patient-owned decisions that include both expected benefits and potential adverse effects.

For research, he asks whether lower-dose, reduced-frequency, or other exposure strategies might retain cardiometabolic benefit at lower cost and with broader eligibility if benefit is at least partly decoupled from weight loss. He explicitly states that the minimum exposure required to optimize clinical outcomes is not known. The editorial closes by arguing that population-level impact will depend on therapies reaching people who need them and on clinicians adopting a cardiometabolic rather than weight-loss-only frame.

Main strengths

  • Clear distinction between weight loss as a visible phenotype and cardiometabolic outcomes as the clinically important target.
  • Explicitly cautions that the highlighted SURMOUNT-1 analysis is exploratory biomarker evidence rather than outcomes evidence.
  • Connects pharmacotherapy evidence to communication, stigma, access, and implementation rather than treating efficacy as sufficient for impact.
  • Names a concrete evidence gap: minimum exposure needed to preserve clinical outcome benefit.

Main limitations

  • Narrative editorial with selected citations; no systematic literature search, evidence grading, or quantitative synthesis.
  • Several broad efficacy statements are summaries of cited studies, not results generated by this source.
  • The lower-dose/reduced-frequency idea is hypothesis-generating and should not be translated into a dosing recommendation.
  • Safety, contraindications, adverse-event burden, treatment discontinuation, cost-effectiveness, and access disparities are not analyzed in enough detail to support a full benefit-risk framework.
  • The communication strategy is persuasive but untested in this article; there is no evidence here that the proposed terminology improves uptake, stigma, adherence, or outcomes.
Do not use for: selecting an individual GLP-1 agent, choosing a dose, reducing dosing frequency, determining coverage/eligibility, or replacing primary trial/guideline/label review.
