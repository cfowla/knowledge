One-page synthesis

Core finding. In patients stabilized after a recent acute coronary syndrome, atorvastatin 80 mg produced substantially lower LDL cholesterol than pravastatin 40 mg (median 62 vs 95 mg/dL) and reduced the two-year Kaplan–Meier rate of the composite primary endpoint from 26.3% to 22.4%. The paper reported a 16% relative reduction in hazard (95% CI, 5%–26%; P=0.005).

The trial was designed around a noninferiority question—whether standard pravastatin therapy was noninferior to intensive atorvastatin therapy—but failed the prespecified equivalence criterion and then demonstrated superiority of the intensive regimen. The clinical curves separated early: primary-endpoint event rates were 2.2% vs 1.9% at 30 days, 7.7% vs 6.3% at 90 days, and 14.1% vs 12.2% at 180 days (pravastatin vs atorvastatin). The composite advantage was supported by significant reductions in revascularization and recurrent unstable angina; all-cause mortality and death-or-MI trends favored atorvastatin but did not reach conventional statistical significance.

The main clinical tradeoff was biochemical hepatic toxicity: ALT >3× ULN occurred in 1.1% with pravastatin versus 3.3% with atorvastatin (P<0.001), while muscle-related discontinuation was similar and no rhabdomyolysis occurred. The authors appropriately warned that their carefully selected, monitored population may tolerate high-dose statin therapy better than less-selected practice populations. A 2006 correction changed several Figure 2 numbers-at-risk values but did not report a change to the event curves, two-year endpoint rates, or treatment-effect estimate.

Methods

Clinical question and design

The trial asked whether pravastatin 40 mg daily, intended to achieve LDL around 100 mg/dL, was noninferior to atorvastatin 80 mg daily, intended to achieve LDL around 70 mg/dL, for preventing death or major cardiovascular events after ACS. Patients were randomized 1:1 in double-blind, double-dummy fashion. The statin comparison was one factor of a 2×2 factorial design with monthly gatifloxacin versus placebo; no interaction with the statin effect was detected for the primary endpoint (interaction P=0.90).

The study was multicenter and international. It enrolled 4162 adults between November 2000 and December 2001 at 349 sites in eight countries. Follow-up was event-driven and lasted 18–36 months (mean 24); the trial continued until 925 endpoint events were reported.

Eligibility and population boundaries

Participants had to be at least 18 years old, hospitalized within the preceding 10 days for MI (with or without ST elevation) or high-risk unstable angina, clinically stable, and enrolled after planned PCI. Total cholesterol had to be ≤240 mg/dL, or ≤200 mg/dL at screening for patients already on long-term lipid-lowering therapy at the index event.

Important exclusions included expected survival <2 years; atorvastatin-equivalent 80-mg statin exposure at the index event; lipid therapy that could not be stopped; recent/anticipated strong CYP3A4 inhibitors; selected recent or planned revascularization; QT-prolonging factors; serious hepatobiliary disease; unexplained CK >3× ULN unrelated to MI; and creatinine >2.0 mg/dL. These restrictions materially narrow safety/generalizability.

Endpoints and statistics

The primary endpoint was time to first all-cause death, MI, documented unstable angina requiring rehospitalization, revascularization at least 30 days after randomization, or stroke. MI and unstable-angina definitions were prespecified. Secondary endpoints included CHD death/nonfatal MI/revascularization, CHD death/nonfatal MI, and individual primary components.

The prespecified noninferiority boundary was the upper limit of the one-sided 95% CI for two-year relative risk <1.17 (corresponding to HR 1.198 over follow-up). With an assumed 22% two-year atorvastatin event rate and equivalent efficacy, ~2000 patients/group yielded 87% power. Analyses were intention-to-treat using Cox proportional-hazards models; after noninferiority was not demonstrated, superiority was assessed with two-sided confidence intervals.

Results

Baseline and concomitant therapy

Randomization yielded 2063 pravastatin and 2099 atorvastatin patients. Groups were broadly balanced, with peripheral arterial disease more common in the pravastatin group (6.6% vs 5.0%; P=0.03). Mean age was ~58 years; 22% were women; ~18% had diabetes; the qualifying event was split roughly into thirds among unstable angina, NSTEMI, and STEMI. About 69% underwent PCI for the index ACS and 25% were already receiving a statin.

Concomitant evidence-based therapy was common for the era: aspirin 93%, beta-blockers 85%, ACE inhibitors 69%, and clopidogrel/ticlopidine 72% initially (20% at one year). This supports applicability to a relatively intensively treated ACS cohort, while still reflecting early-2000s care.

Lipid and inflammatory-marker separation

Median LDL was 106 mg/dL in both groups at randomization. During follow-up, median LDL was 95 mg/dL with pravastatin and 62 mg/dL with atorvastatin (P<0.001). In statin-naive patients, LDL fell 22% vs 51% at 30 days; in previously treated patients it was essentially unchanged with pravastatin and fell an additional 32% with atorvastatin (both P<0.001).

Median HDL increased 8.1% with pravastatin and 6.5% with atorvastatin (P<0.001). Median CRP fell from 12.3 mg/L in both groups to 2.1 vs 1.3 mg/L, respectively (P<0.001). The trial therefore established a large exposure separation in LDL and a smaller differential in inflammatory-marker suppression.

Primary and secondary clinical outcomes

Two-year primary-event rates were 26.3% with pravastatin and 22.4% with atorvastatin, with a reported 16% relative hazard reduction (95% CI 5%–26%; P=0.005). Benefit appeared by 30 days and remained directionally consistent through follow-up. The secondary CHD death/MI/revascularization endpoint was 22.3% vs 19.7% (14% reduction; P=0.029), and death/MI/urgent revascularization was reduced by 25% (P<0.001).

Among individual outcomes, revascularization was reduced 14% (18.8% vs 16.3%; P=0.04) and recurrent unstable angina 29% (5.1% vs 3.8%; P=0.02). All-cause mortality (3.2% vs 2.2%; 28% reduction; P=0.07) and death-or-MI (10.0% vs 8.3%; 18% reduction; P=0.06) favored intensive therapy but were not statistically significant. Stroke was infrequent and not significantly different.

Subgroups

The paper described the treatment effect as consistent across prespecified subgroups including sex, age, diabetes, smoking, prior statin use, and ACS presentation. The only reported significant interaction was baseline LDL ≥125 vs <125 mg/dL (P for interaction=0.02).

In the ≥125-mg/dL subgroup, two-year primary-event rates were 28.2% with pravastatin and 20.1% with atorvastatin, with a reported 34% hazard reduction. In the <125-mg/dL subgroup, rates were 25.6% vs 23.5%, with a 7% reported reduction. This interaction is hypothesis-informing but should not override the randomized overall effect, especially because multiple subgroup comparisons were examined.

Both regimens were generally tolerated, but high-dose atorvastatin produced more frequent marked ALT elevation. The study's exclusion and monitoring strategy is integral to interpreting safety; the absence of rhabdomyolysis in ~4,000 selected participants does not establish zero risk in broader practice.

Interpretation and limitations

What the authors argued

The authors interpreted the 33-mg/dL median LDL separation and the 16% relative hazard reduction as evidence that much of the incremental benefit was attributable to more intensive LDL lowering, while acknowledging that differential non-lipid or pleiotropic statin effects could not be excluded. They emphasized the early emergence of benefit and its continuation through average two-year follow-up.

They further argued that, after ACS, the LDL target might be lower than the then-current goal of <100 mg/dL because the intensive arm achieved a median LDL of 62 mg/dL with better clinical outcomes.

Key appraisal limitations

  • Intensity and molecule are confounded: atorvastatin 80 mg was compared with pravastatin 40 mg, so the trial does not isolate dose intensity from molecule-specific effects.
  • Composite endpoint: the primary benefit includes revascularization and unstable-angina rehospitalization; mortality and death-or-MI were favorable but not conventionally significant.
  • Selected safety population: strong CYP3A4 inhibitor use, significant renal/hepatic abnormalities, and other higher-risk features were excluded.
  • Era effects: early-2000s ACS management differs from contemporary PCI, antithrombotic, and nonstatin lipid-lowering practice, although the randomized statin comparison remains internally valid.
  • Subgroups: the LDL-threshold interaction is one of multiple subgroup analyses and should be interpreted cautiously.
  • Sponsor involvement: Bristol-Myers Squibb and Sankyo funded the study; Bristol-Myers Squibb employees were authors and sponsor/TIMI/data-center personnel jointly analyzed data. The paper also states investigators designed the trial and had free and complete access to data.
  • Correction: Figure 2 risk-set numbers were corrected in 2006. The correction is material for accurate survival-curve documentation but did not state that clinical-effect estimates changed.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a landmark active-comparator RCT establishing that more intensive statin therapy soon after ACS improves a broad ischemic composite compared with moderate-intensity therapy. It remains highly useful for understanding the evidentiary foundation of post-ACS high-intensity statin practice, but it should not be used alone to define contemporary LDL thresholds or nonstatin sequencing.

Practice translation

Source-derived historical translation

PROVE IT–TIMI 22 supports early initiation and continued use of an intensive statin regimen after ACS, demonstrating both larger LDL reduction and fewer composite cardiovascular events than a moderate regimen. It also establishes the need to monitor hepatic tolerability and to consider interaction/comorbidity exclusions when generalizing the safety experience.

Current-practice verification — external, not source-derived

The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI ACS guideline recommends high-intensity statin therapy for all ACS patients. It also recommends adding a nonstatin agent for patients on maximally tolerated statin therapy with LDL-C ≥70 mg/dL and states that further intensification is reasonable when LDL-C is 55 to <70 mg/dL in this high-risk population. Thus, the original trial's finding that benefit extended below the historical 100-mg/dL target is consistent with the direction of contemporary ACS lipid management, but the trial itself did not test today's nonstatin strategies.
