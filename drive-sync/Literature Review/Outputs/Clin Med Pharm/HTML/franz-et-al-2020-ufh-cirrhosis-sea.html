One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this 51-patient retrospective cohort, increasing cirrhosis severity was associated with a lower probability and slower attainment of two consecutive therapeutic AXA levels during UFH treatment. More severe disease also showed directionally lower initial AXA values, greater dose escalation, and more bleeding, but several of those dose and safety differences did not reach statistical significance.

The most robust study signal is therapeutic AXA attainment: all compensated patients achieved two consecutive therapeutic AXA levels versus 64.3% of decompensated patients (P=0.001). By CTP class, attainment was 100% in class A, 84.6% in class B, and 42.9% in class C (P=0.017). Time-to-attainment curves also separated by compensation status (P=0.004) and CTP class (P=0.037). Initial AXA was numerically lower with greater severity, but the differences narrowly missed conventional statistical significance.

The paper's more practice-sensitive interpretation - that severe cirrhosis may require higher UFH doses to achieve standard AXA targets - is plausible within the observed data but less definitive than the attainment findings. Mean therapeutic infusion rates among those achieving target were not statistically different by compensation status (17.06 vs 18.02 U/kg/h; P=0.411) or CTP class (16.09, 17.96, and 18.77 U/kg/h; P=0.429). Decompensated patients did reach higher maximum rates (20.6 vs 18.0 U/kg/h), with nonachievers averaging 24.6 U/kg/h, but these descriptive data do not establish an optimal higher dose.

Safety signal is concerning but imprecise. Bleeding occurred in 29.4% overall and was numerically more frequent in decompensated disease and CTP class C, yet between-group tests were nonsignificant. The study pooled minor bleeding (including oozing/epistaxis) with more serious events and could not reliably attribute adverse events to UFH. Disease severity itself is a major competing explanation.
Best use. Treat the study as a mechanistic/monitoring caution: persistently low AXA in advanced cirrhosis may not have the same interpretation as in patients with preserved hepatic synthetic function. The paper supports further validation of monitoring strategies; it does not support automatic dose escalation or a new therapeutic range.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame cirrhosis as a rebalanced but unstable hemostatic state with simultaneous bleeding and thrombotic risk. Conventional tests such as PT/INR and aPTT are abnormal at baseline, while heparin-dependent AXA measurements may be depressed in cirrhosis because circulating antithrombin is reduced. The resulting clinical problem is that repeated low AXA values could drive either premature anticoagulant switching or overly aggressive UFH escalation.

The stated objective was to characterize therapeutic UFH dosing and safety across cirrhosis severity and determine whether dose requirements to achieve therapeutic AXA differ with disease severity.

Methods

Adults treated at a single academic center from 2014-2017 were included if cirrhosis was confirmed by imaging or biopsy, VTE was proven or strongly suspected, UFH continued for at least 48 hours, and AXA values were recorded after dose changes and at least daily. Patients younger than 18 years, pregnant patients, and patients with known coagulopathies expected to alter AXA monitoring were excluded. Decompensation was assigned from physician documentation/evidence including ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or esophageal varices.

UFH was adjusted by pharmacists and/or nurses using an AXA target of 0.3-0.7 IU/mL. Bolus use was discretionary to the ordering physician within the nomogram. Therapeutic dosing required two consecutive AXA values in range, 6 hours apart. Safety was assessed by chart review for 30 days. No power calculation was performed. Statistical methods varied by outcome distribution/grouping and included t tests, Mann-Whitney U, chi-square, Fisher exact, Kruskal-Wallis, one-way ANOVA, and Kaplan-Meier/log-rank analysis; nonachievers were assigned a 168-hour endpoint and censored there.

Results

Seventy-four patients were screened and 51 included: 23 compensated and 28 decompensated. Mean age was 56.1 years, 45.1% were female, and initial dosing weight averaged 90.9 kg. The groups were broadly similar in age and weight. CTP score differed as expected (6.7 compensated vs 9.0 decompensated; P<0.001); MELD was numerically higher with decompensation but not significant (15.8 vs 13.6; P=0.352). CTP class could be calculated for only 42 patients because prior warfarin affected INR in some patients.

The strongest efficacy differences were the likelihood and speed of reaching two consecutive therapeutic AXA levels. Compensated patients achieved the target in 100% of cases compared with 64.3% of decompensated patients (P=0.001). CTP A/B/C attainment was 100%, 84.6%, and 42.9% (P=0.017). Initial AXA trended down with severity (compensated 0.47 vs decompensated 0.34 IU/mL, P=0.053; CTP A/B/C 0.54/0.37/0.28, P=0.069). Mean therapeutic infusion rate did not significantly differ by compensation status or CTP class. Bleeding and death were numerically greater with advanced disease but not statistically significant.

Discussion

The authors interpret the findings as clinically consistent with prior work suggesting reduced measured AXA response to antithrombin-dependent anticoagulants in cirrhosis, especially as severity increases. They emphasize that repeated subtherapeutic AXA values were common despite some initially therapeutic values and that patients with advanced disease sometimes required markedly higher UFH rates, including 18-33.5 U/kg/h among nonachievers.

They also acknowledge substantial uncertainty: bleeding was common but heterogeneously defined, adverse-event causality was difficult to assign, the sample was small and likely underpowered for most outcomes, the nomogram was institution-specific, and there was no noncirrhotic control group. The authors state a preference for AXA over aPTT in cirrhosis, but that preference is based partly on prior literature; this study did not directly test AXA against aPTT.

Conclusion and relevance

The authors conclude that decompensated and CTP class C patients may be less likely to achieve therapeutic AXA and may require higher UFH doses to reach conventional targets, with a possible tradeoff of greater bleeding. They propose that standard-population UFH nomograms and AXA goals may be suboptimal in advanced cirrhosis and call for larger studies to define a better monitoring/dosing strategy.

Those conclusions are best read as hypothesis-generating. The current study demonstrates a severity-linked monitoring/attainment signal more convincingly than it demonstrates a safe or effective alternative dosing approach.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a focused UFH/cirrhosis review; do not use alone to change practice. The paper is short, clinically direct, and exposes a meaningful monitoring problem, but its observational design and small sample do not establish a safer alternative target or dosing strategy.

Validity and evidence interpretation

Strengths
  • Clear cirrhosis confirmation criteria and severity stratification.
  • Defined AXA target and requirement for two consecutive therapeutic values.
  • Direct capture of infusion rates, dose escalation, target attainment, and 30-day safety.
  • Severity signal replicated using two classification approaches: compensation status and CTP class.
Major limitations
  • Retrospective, single-center cohort with only 51 patients and no power calculation.
  • No noncirrhotic comparator; no direct AXA-vs-aPTT comparison.
  • Institution-specific nomogram and 1,800 U/h starting cap limit transferability.
  • Proven and suspected VTE were both eligible; confounding by illness severity is substantial.
  • Bleeding definition mixed minor and serious events, and causality could not be assigned.
  • CTP class unavailable in 9 patients because prior warfarin affected INR.
Internal outcome-labeling inconsistency. Methods define the primary efficacy outcome as the mean UFH infusion rate producing two consecutive therapeutic AXA levels. In Results, the authors refer to the statistically significant difference in therapeutic-target attainment as the "primary outcome." The therapeutic infusion-rate comparisons themselves were not statistically significant. This does not invalidate the observed attainment signal, but it weakens precision in how the central endpoint is reported.
Claim-to-data calibration. The source most strongly supports: "advanced cirrhosis is associated with lower/fewer/slower therapeutic AXA measurements under this nomogram." It less strongly supports: "advanced cirrhosis requires higher UFH doses." It does not establish: "higher UFH doses are clinically appropriate or safe" or "a new AXA target should be used."

Implementation and practice takeaways

What the paper can changeIt should increase caution when interpreting persistently low AXA values in advanced cirrhosis and prompt review of cirrhosis severity, antithrombin-dependent assay behavior, bleeding risk, and the full clinical anticoagulation picture.
What it should not changeDo not infer that CTP C patients should automatically receive higher UFH rates, that standard AXA 0.3-0.7 IU/mL is wrong, or that a cirrhosis-specific nomogram is validated.
Monitoring implicationThe study supports the possibility that standard AXA targets may be difficult to attain in severe cirrhosis. The author's preference for AXA over aPTT is not directly tested here.
Safety implicationBleeding was common and numerically highest in CTP C, but event definitions and attribution prevent a clean UFH dose-response conclusion.
Research priorityProspective multicenter comparison of monitoring strategies, with antithrombin measurements, standardized bleeding definitions, clinical efficacy endpoints, and explicit dose-response analysis.
Medication-use-system valuePotential trigger for pharmacist escalation or individualized review when severe cirrhosis plus persistent subtherapeutic AXA leads to repeated boluses/escalation beyond usual institutional ranges.
