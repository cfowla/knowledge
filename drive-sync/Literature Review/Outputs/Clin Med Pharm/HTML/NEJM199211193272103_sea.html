One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In patients with acute proximal DVT treated with acenocoumarol, omitting initial therapeutic heparin produced substantially more thrombus progression and more symptomatic recurrent/extended VTE. The trial was stopped early for excess symptomatic events in the acenocoumarol-alone arm.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors framed the study around uncertainty over whether high-dose oral anticoagulation alone could replace initial heparin in acute proximal DVT. They randomized symptomatic outpatients to continuous IV heparin plus acenocoumarol or placebo infusion plus acenocoumarol and prespecified symptomatic extension/recurrent VTE over six months as the principal endpoint.

The trial was stopped early because symptomatic events accumulated in the acenocoumarol-alone arm. Symptomatic events were 20% vs 6.7% (P=0.058), asymptomatic imaging progression was 39.6% vs 8.2% (P<0.001), and major bleeding was uncommon in both arms. The authors concluded that full-dose heparin is required when initiating acenocoumarol for proximal DVT.

Introduction / background

At the time, initial IV heparin followed by oral anticoagulation was traditional, but the authors argued that controlled evidence directly establishing the need for heparin was limited. Some European practice used acenocoumarol alone in selected outpatients on the hypothesis that rapid high-dose VKA initiation could obviate heparin and avoid hospitalization.

The trial therefore addressed genuine clinical equipoise: whether therapeutic heparin added meaningful efficacy and acceptable safety when acenocoumarol was started at the same time. The background also notes earlier randomized evidence that heparin duration could be shortened when oral anticoagulation began concurrently.

Methods

Consecutive symptomatic outpatients with venography-confirmed proximal DVT were eligible. Major exclusions included previous DVT, suspected PE at referral, bilateral thrombosis/phlegmasia, ongoing anticoagulation, pregnancy, age <18 or >85 years, life expectancy <6 months, known coagulation-inhibitor deficiency, or lack of consent. The double-blind comparison used therapeutic IV UFH vs visually identical placebo, with blinded outcome interpretation.

Heparin was loaded at 5000 units then infused at 1250 units/hour and adjusted daily to aPTT 60-90 seconds (approximately 2-3× normal). All patients received acenocoumarol 6 mg day 1 and 4 mg day 2, then dose-adjusted to INR 2.0-3.0 for 12 weeks. Study infusion continued at least 7 days and stopped after two consecutive therapeutic prothrombin-time measurements. Objective recurrent-VTE testing and repeat venography/lung scanning were prespecified. Major bleeding required overt bleeding plus ≥20 g/L hemoglobin fall, ≥2-unit transfusion, intracranial bleeding, or retroperitoneal bleeding.

Results

At termination, 120 patients had been enrolled, 60 per arm. Baseline characteristics were similar. The primary symptomatic extension/recurrent-VTE endpoint occurred in 4/60 (6.7%) with heparin + acenocoumarol and 12/60 (20%) with acenocoumarol alone (P=0.058). In the acenocoumarol-only arm, three of the 12 confirmed symptomatic events occurred in the first week and six occurred during the 12-week secondary-prophylaxis period.

Among patients evaluable for protocol repeat imaging after one week, asymptomatic thromboembolic worsening occurred in 4/49 (8.2%) vs 21/53 (39.6%), P<0.001. Major bleeding occurred in 2 vs 3 patients and minor bleeding in 3 vs 6. One combined-therapy patient died by suicide; two acenocoumarol-alone patients had cancer-related deaths. Mean acenocoumarol doses and INR trajectories were similar during week 1, with INR 2.0 reached after about 3-4 days in both groups.

Discussion / conclusion

The authors interpreted the excess symptomatic events plus the five-fold difference in asymptomatic imaging worsening as evidence that acenocoumarol alone failed to suppress the acute thrombotic process. They acknowledged that the primary symptomatic comparison only approached conventional statistical significance, but argued that concordant objective imaging progression strongly supported a real treatment difference.

They concluded that full-dose heparin was required for the initial treatment of proximal DVT and could be safely started simultaneously with acenocoumarol. Mechanistically, they proposed that inadequate anticoagulation early in treatment could permit persistent thrombus growth and later recurrence.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. For a heparin/VTE deep dive, this is a compact landmark trial directly testing the need for initial parenteral anticoagulation when a VKA is used. Its historical importance remains high, but its drug-selection implications are narrower in the DOAC era.
Relevance
10/10

Basis: direct randomized evidence on the role of heparin in acute proximal DVT.

Limiter: acenocoumarol/VKA context rather than modern DOAC-first management.

Would change: lower only if the review question excludes historical VKA-era evidence.

9/10

Basis: directly tested a then-used oral-anticoagulant-only strategy under blinded randomized conditions.

Limiter: the mechanistic rationale for immediate anticoagulation was not new.

Would change: lower when judged purely by present-day novelty rather than historical contribution.

Method strength
7/10

Basis: randomized double-blind design, placebo infusion, objective testing, blinded interpretation, intention-to-treat primary analysis.

Limiter: early stop at 120 vs planned ≥250 and no confidence intervals for key effects.

Would change: higher with full enrollment, concealed randomization details, prespecified stopping boundaries, and interval estimates.

Evidence strength
7/10

Basis: concordant symptomatic and objective imaging outcomes with a large treatment separation.

Limiter: primary P=0.058 and small event counts.

External validity
5/10

Basis: clinically recognizable first-episode proximal DVT population.

Limiter: two-center Dutch outpatient sample, numerous exclusions, fixed historical UFH regimen, no suspected PE, and VKA-specific comparison.

Would change: higher with broader populations, modern agents, and multicenter international replication.

Implementation value
8/10

Basis: supports the durable rule that VKA treatment of acute VTE requires immediate parenteral anticoagulation overlap.

Limiter: does not define present-day preferred anticoagulant or modern UFH nomograms.

Would change: lower for DOAC-only pathways; higher for historical rationale or VKA-specific CDS logic.

Main strengths: direct randomized question; blinding preserved with placebo infusion and fictitious aPTTs; objective venographic/lung-scan follow-up; blinded diagnostic interpretation; clinically important symptomatic endpoint; safety reporting.
Main limitations: early termination; underenrollment relative to planned sample; primary comparison narrowly missed conventional significance; no confidence intervals; small event counts; historical fixed-dose UFH strategy; acenocoumarol rather than warfarin/DOACs; restricted outpatient population; imaging progression is a surrogate secondary endpoint.

Implementation / practice translation

What the source itself supports

  • Acenocoumarol alone should not be treated as adequate initial anticoagulation for acute proximal DVT.
  • When a VKA is used, therapeutic parenteral anticoagulation should begin immediately rather than waiting for the VKA effect.
  • Starting the VKA at the same time as heparin did not appear to increase major bleeding in this small trial and did not materially alter the early INR trajectory.

External/current verification — separate from the 1992 source

CHEST 2024 compendium: current collected CHEST guidance retains the VKA-specific principle demonstrated by this trial: for acute VTE treated with a VKA, initial parenteral anticoagulation is recommended rather than none; VKA should be started early (for example, the same day), with parenteral anticoagulation continued for at least 5 days and until INR ≥2.0 for at least 24 hours. The same compendium recommends apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, or rivaroxaban over VKA for treatment-phase anticoagulation in acute VTE and notes that DOAC initiation differs by agent.

Translation: this paper remains highly useful for understanding why VKA therapy requires overlap. It should not be generalized into a rule that all oral anticoagulants require heparin. Apixaban and rivaroxaban use oral loading/intensification regimens, whereas dabigatran and edoxaban require an initial parenteral course; current selection depends on the patient's clinical context and current guidance.

For CDS/evidence-graph use: model the finding as a VKA-specific initiation rule with provenance to this trial, not as a universal “oral anticoagulant + heparin” rule. Keep historical study dosing distinct from contemporary UFH dosing protocols.
