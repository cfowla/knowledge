One-page synthesis

Core thesis. CDI should not be framed solely as a hospital-acquired infection. The authors use a 45-year-old woman without recent hospitalization or antibiotic exposure to illustrate community-acquired disease and to argue that outpatient clinicians should consider CDI even when classic risk factors are absent.

Section condensation

Opening background

The article describes CDI as a fecal-oral, potentially severe infection historically associated with antibiotics, hospitalization, and older age. It defines community-acquired CDI as symptom onset within 48 hours of admission or more than 12 weeks after discharge and frames the report as evidence of a shift away from an exclusively hospital-acquired model.

These epidemiologic statements are literature-derived background. They contextualize the case but were not generated by the index patient and therefore should not be treated as primary-study results from this article.

Case

A previously well 45-year-old woman presented to primary care with 3 days of watery, foul-smelling diarrhea, 4-5 nonbloody stools daily, and no fever. She denied travel, sick contacts, unusual food, recent hospitalization, and antibiotic exposure. Viral gastroenteritis was initially presumed and supportive hydration was advised.

She returned four days later with persistent diarrhea and new lower abdominal pain. GDH antigen and C. difficile toxin immunoassays were both positive. She was treated with metronidazole for 10 days and fully recovered. The report does not provide metronidazole dose, route, frequency, laboratory severity markers, strain typing, recurrence follow-up duration, or a comparator.

Discussion

The authors summarize population-based and surveillance studies suggesting a meaningful community burden, including disease among younger patients and those without recent antibiotic exposure. Proposed acquisition pathways include asymptomatic carriers, contact with young children, outpatient health-care exposure, and circulating virulent strains. The article also notes that community-acquired cases can still lead to hospitalization, severe disease, treatment failure, and recurrence.

The discussion additionally reviews 2017-era diagnostic and therapeutic practice: GDH/toxin testing with PCR for discordance, severity-stratified metronidazole versus vancomycin, fidaxomicin as a lower-recurrence but higher-cost option, and mixed evidence for probiotics. These are secondary summaries of cited literature, not results of the case report itself.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that CDI is not purely hospital acquired and can affect patients previously considered low risk. Their practical message is to maintain diagnostic suspicion in patients with diarrhea lasting more than 24 hours even without classic risk factors.

The diagnostic-awareness message remains conceptually useful, but the specific treatment and prevention discussion requires current guideline verification before clinical use.

Current-practice verification

Treatment is outdated. The 2021 IDSA/SHEA focused update suggests fidaxomicin rather than a standard course of vancomycin for an initial CDI episode (conditional, moderate-certainty evidence), while vancomycin remains acceptable. Metronidazole is an alternative only for nonsevere CDI when fidaxomicin and vancomycin are unavailable. This makes the report's successful 10-day metronidazole case a historical observation, not a preferred contemporary regimen.
Testing threshold remains broadly aligned. IDSA/SHEA recommends testing patients with unexplained, new-onset ≥3 unformed stools in 24 hours. Diagnostic testing may use NAAT alone when strict stool-submission criteria exist, or a multistep approach such as GDH plus toxin with NAAT arbitration. The article's GDH/toxin approach fits within a multistep strategy but should not be treated as the only valid algorithm.
Probiotic conclusion should not drive practice. IDSA/SHEA states that evidence is insufficient to recommend probiotics for primary CDI prevention outside clinical trials. The article's age-stratified interpretation of probiotic benefit is therefore too strong for current stand-alone guidance.

Verification sources: IDSA/SHEA 2017 CDI guideline update (published 2018) for diagnosis and probiotics; IDSA/SHEA 2021 focused update for fidaxomicin/vancomycin treatment recommendations.

Implementation / practice takeaways
