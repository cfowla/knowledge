One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Replacing a fixed 10,000-U initial heparin bolus with an empirical BSA-scaled bolus substantially increased early ACT target attainment in this small matched PTCA cohort.

The BSA formula was [BSA (m²) / 1.3 m²] × 10,000 U. Mean 10-minute arterial ACT was 407 ± 57 s with BSA-guided dosing versus 309 ± 53 s with the fixed bolus (p<0.01). ACT >400 s occurred in 13/27 (48.1%) versus 2/27 (7.4%), an absolute difference of 0.41 (95% CI 0.19-0.62). ACT >350 s occurred in 25/27 (92.6%) versus 6/27 (22.2%), an absolute difference of 0.70 (95% CI 0.52-0.89; p<0.001).

The result is pharmacodynamically clear but clinically narrow. There was no randomization described, only 54 patients, no patient-centered efficacy or bleeding outcome, and the formula was empirically derived. ACT interpretation was explicitly device- and sample-source-dependent: venous values tend to exceed arterial values, and the paper notes that at ACT >300 s the Hemochron may read at least 50 s higher than HemoTec. These constraints sharply limit direct translation to modern PCI practice.

Section condensation

Summary / abstract

The article frames fixed 10,000-U bolus dosing as frequently inadequate for reaching Hemochron ACT targets of 350-400 s. It compares 27 BSA-adjusted patients against 27 matched fixed-dose controls and reports substantially higher target attainment with BSA adjustment.

The authors conclude BSA adjustment is more effective for initial arterial ACT target attainment, while restricting that conclusion to the Hemochron device and arterial samples.

Introduction

Traditional PTCA heparin dosing used a fixed bolus followed by reactive supplementation. The authors argue that patient size is biologically relevant and note prior work suggesting BSA correlated better with total heparin requirements than weight, height, or age in PTCA.

The prespecified practical question was whether fixed or BSA-guided dosing better produced an initial ACT of 350 or 400 s.

Methods

Twenty-seven BSA-guided patients were compared with 27 age-/gender-matched fixed-dose controls. Patients receiving heparin before PTCA were excluded. The adjusted dose was [BSA(m²)/1.3 m²] × 10,000 U. Arterial ACT was measured with a Hemochron Model 401 before dosing and 10 minutes afterward; samples came from the femoral arterial sheath after a discard and saline flush.

Mean ± SD values were compared with a paired Student t test, justified by age/gender matching, while target-attainment proportions were compared with a z test. Tests were two-tailed and p<0.05 defined significance.

Results

Groups were similar in mean BSA (1.92 ± 0.18 vs 1.87 ± 0.24 m²) and baseline ACT (128 ± 29 vs 118 ± 26 s). At 10 minutes, mean ACT was 407 ± 57 versus 309 ± 53 s (p<0.01).

For ACT >400 s, success was 48.1% versus 7.4% (absolute difference 0.41, 95% CI 0.19-0.62; p<0.01). For ACT >350 s, success was 92.6% versus 22.2% (absolute difference 0.70, 95% CI 0.52-0.89; p<0.001).

Discussion

The authors interpret the BSA method as a marked improvement over a fixed bolus, particularly for the 350-s threshold. They acknowledge that many patients still require subsequent dose adjustment to reach 400 s. The formula itself was empirical: observations in patients around 1.3 m² suggested 10,000 U produced about 400 s, and scaling was then assumed to follow body size. A BSA of 1.7 m² yields roughly 13,000 U under the formula.

Important measurement caveats are explicit. Venous ACT is slightly higher than arterial ACT, and Hemochron and HemoTec are not numerically interchangeable at high ACT values. The paper therefore warns against transplanting thresholds across specimen types or devices.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that BSA-adjusted initial heparin dosing is more effective than a fixed 10,000-U bolus for attaining arterial Hemochron ACT targets of 350 and 400 s before PTCA. They again caution that target values require reconsideration with venous sampling or another ACT machine.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High value as historical, directly relevant evidence that fixed-dose UFH can produce size-dependent ACT underexposure; low value as a current dosing protocol.
8/10
Relevance
Directly addresses UFH initial dosing and ACT target attainment. Limiter: PTCA-era context rather than current PCI practice.
4/10
Novelty
Historically useful individualized-dosing concept, but not novel now. Would rise only if linked to a modern validation framework.
4/10
Method strength
Standardized ACT measurement and matched controls, but small, nonrandomized, single-center comparison with limited confounding control.
5/10
Evidence strength
Large, internally consistent surrogate effect with CIs and p values. Limited by n=54 and absence of clinical outcomes.
2/10
External validity
Single-center 1997 PTCA, arterial Hemochron 401, empirical BSA equation. Generalization to other devices, samples, and contemporary PCI is weak.
2/10
Implementation value
The equation is reproducible, but the evidence is insufficient to adopt it as a contemporary protocol without modern comparative validation and safety outcomes.

Validity

  • Design: matched comparative study; the text contrasts a previous fixed-dose approach with a recently adopted BSA approach rather than describing random allocation.
  • Bias / confounding: matching was only on age and gender; other determinants of heparin response may differ.
  • Statistics: proportions and confidence intervals support a large target-attainment signal. The rationale for treating matched groups as dependent for a paired t test is not fully persuasive because these were distinct patients.
  • Endpoint: 10-minute ACT is a pharmacodynamic surrogate; thrombosis, ischemia, bleeding, transfusion, or procedural clinical outcomes were not reported.
  • Measurement: ACT is explicitly affected by sample source and device, limiting threshold portability.
Do not use for: selecting a current UFH bolus regimen for PCI, defining contemporary ACT targets, or inferring improved bleeding/thrombotic outcomes. The paper only establishes better short-term ACT target attainment under its specific historical conditions.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Useful signal
A fixed 10,000-U bolus produced substantially lower 10-minute ACT than a size-adjusted strategy, supporting the general rationale for patient-size-aware UFH dosing.
Measurement lesson
ACT targets are not device-agnostic or specimen-agnostic. Protocols need to specify device, sample source, timing, and target.
Protocol-design lesson
Empirical dosing equations require external validation, safety endpoints, and comparison with accepted alternatives before operational adoption.
Best use
Historical evidence-gap and mechanistic context for a heparin deep dive; useful as a citation supporting why individualized dosing emerged.
