One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The investigators built a low-cost in-house chromogenic anti-factor Xa assay with strong analytical performance versus a commercial platform, then used it to show substantial categorical discordance between APTT and anti-factor Xa in UFH-treated patients and to explore factors associated with that discordance.

The analytical portion is the strongest part of the paper. The assay used bovine factor Xa and S-2732 with optimized reaction conditions; reported precision remained within CLSI acceptance criteria, LoB/LoD/LoQ were 0.073/0.080/0.10 IU/mL, linearity was excellent through 1.35 IU/mL, carryover was 0.57%, and method comparison produced Pearson r = 0.986 with R² = 0.972. The authors also report ≤5-minute turnaround and preliminary reagent cost near US$2/test. These findings make the paper potentially useful to laboratories considering in-house anti-Xa capability, although between-site reproducibility and real-world lot/reagent robustness were not externally tested.

The clinical/predictive portion is less secure. The main text establishes a laboratory-specific APTT range of 79–127 s against anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 IU/mL, reports only moderate APTT–anti-Xa association (Spearman ρ = 0.678), and reports 58.2% categorical discordance. Random forest and logistic regression identify fibrinogen, antithrombin III, eGFR, triglycerides, and CRP as shared predictors, but the modeling has roughly 64 events for 10 candidate predictors, internal cross-validation only, no external cohort, and no clinical outcome linkage. Several central figures disagree with the prose on sample size, ROC thresholds/AUCs, concordance counts, and proposed algorithm logic; these inconsistencies materially limit confidence in exact clinical thresholds.

Bottom line. High relevance for a heparin monitoring literature cluster and useful for assay-development concepts. Treat the exact APTT thresholds, discordance matrix, predictive model, and Figure 14 monitoring algorithm as unresolved until the source discrepancies are clarified or independently validated.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames three linked goals: develop and validate an in-house anti-Xa assay, calibrate a laboratory-specific APTT therapeutic range to anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 IU/mL, and identify predictors of APTT/anti-Xa discordance. It reports 190 samples from 110 UFH-treated patients, assay correlation with a commercial platform (r = 0.986; R² = 0.972), a final APTT range of 79–127 s, 58.2% discordance, and five common predictors across random forest/logistic approaches.

The abstract’s clinical implication is tempered later in the paper: the predictive models are explicitly hypothesis-generating, not externally validated, and not linked to bleeding/thrombosis outcomes.

1 Introduction

The introduction contrasts APTT’s susceptibility to reagent sensitivity, coagulation-factor levels, inflammation, lupus anticoagulants, and pre-analytic variation with the greater mechanistic specificity of chromogenic anti-Xa assays. Cost is presented as a barrier to broad anti-Xa adoption. Prior discordance rates of roughly 30%–50% are cited, with candidate contributors including antithrombin deficiency, factor VIII, acute-phase responses, and renal dysfunction.

The rationale for machine learning is the possibility of nonlinear relationships among multiple predictors. The paper’s three objectives are restated clearly: assay development, local APTT calibration, and exploratory prediction of monitoring discordance.

2.1 Study design and sample collection

The single-center study was ethics-approved and prospectively collected specimens from adults on continuous IV UFH. Inclusion required age ≥18 years, ≥24 hours of IV UFH, and paired APTT/anti-Xa testing within 4 hours of blood collection. Important exclusions included other anticoagulant classes, Child-Pugh C liver dysfunction, inherited coagulation disorders, markedly compromised specimens, and incomplete laboratory data. Samples were collected after a bolus or at defined infusion steady state.

Plasma handling was standardized, and normal pooled plasma came from 280 healthy volunteers. The paper reports 190 specimens overall. However, its allocation language is internally problematic: it describes 80 derivation specimens from 80 different patients plus 110 modeling specimens from 110 different patients “independent” of derivation, which cannot be reconciled with only 110 total enrolled patients without additional explanation.

2.2–2.4 Instruments, assay development, and analytical validation

The in-house system ran on a ZONCI XL-3200c analyzer using bovine factor Xa, S-2732 chromogenic substrate, buffer/stabilizer components, and a two-stage chromogenic principle. Commercial comparison and other coagulation tests used Stago instrumentation. Optimization varied reagent concentrations, pH, stabilizers, volume ratios, and reaction timing.

Analytical validation followed cited CLSI procedures for precision, sensitivity, linearity, method comparison, and stability. Prespecified precision acceptance was within-run CV <5% and between-run CV <10%; LoQ required MAD ≤30%, probability of detection ≥95%, and total error ≤40%.

2.5 APTT therapeutic-range establishment

The laboratory’s stated APTT reference range was 28–43.5 s. Eighty specimens were assigned to derive an APTT therapeutic range; the remaining 110 were used for concordance and predictive modeling. Linear regression mapped anti-Xa 0.3 and 0.7 IU/mL to APTT values, while two ROC analyses sought cutoffs optimized by the Youden index.

Concordance was defined as agreement across three therapeutic categories. This design appropriately separates threshold derivation from subsequent evaluation at the specimen level, but the patient-level independence of the two sets is unclear because of the cohort-count inconsistency noted above.

2.6 Predictive modeling

Ten predictors were collected within 24 hours of monitoring: age, sex, BMI, fibrinogen, antithrombin III, D-dimer, CRP, eGFR, triglycerides, and total cholesterol. Random forest used bootstrap/class balancing and permutation importance; logistic regression used all 10 variables with backward elimination while retaining fibrinogen and antithrombin III. Five-fold cross-validation was used.

The authors themselves identify the main modeling constraint: 64 discordant events for 10 predictors gives approximately 6.4 events per variable, below the cited rule-of-thumb minimum of 10. Thus the models are explicitly exploratory and require external validation.

3.1–3.2 Assay optimization and analytical performance

The optimized assay used factor Xa 0.9 IU/mL, S-2732 0.43 mmol/L, and a 5:40:100 µL sample:R1:R2 volume ratio. Incubation was selected at 90 s and detection stabilized after ~55 s. Reported calibration R² values were 0.99883 for the in-house curve and 0.99854 for the commercial system. Precision CVs ranged 1.15%–4.32% intra-assay and 1.25%–4.03% inter-assay.

Analytical sensitivity results were LoB 0.073 IU/mL, LoD 0.080 IU/mL, and LoQ 0.10 IU/mL. Linearity from 0.10–1.35 IU/mL had R² 0.99923. Carryover was 0.57%, and 14-day 37 °C thermal-stress testing showed relative MAD ≤2.86%. These are favorable analytic results, although the displayed precision table and Figure 4 contain internal formatting/equation inconsistencies.

3.3 Method comparison

The main text reports 80 specimens tested by both in-house and STA-Liquid systems, no significant paired difference, Pearson r = 0.986, R² = 0.972, and 93.75% of samples within Bland–Altman limits. These results support strong analytical comparability at this site.

Exact reproducibility is weakened by presentation conflicts: Figure 8 labels n = 120 rather than 80, and Figure 9’s mean bias differs from the value later quoted in Discussion.

3.4 APTT therapeutic range calibrated against anti-factor Xa

APTT and anti-Xa were non-normal and showed moderate Spearman correlation (ρ = 0.678; p < 0.001). Regression Y = 132.7X + 44.1 (R² = 0.46) predicted 84–137 s for anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 IU/mL. The main text reports ROC cutoffs of 78.9 s (AUC 0.723, sensitivity 70.6%, specificity 71.6%) and 126.6 s (AUC 0.815, sensitivity 83.3%, specificity 80.0%), leading to a final 79–127 s range.

The authors report that their ROC-optimized range reduced misclassification from 42.7% with the empirical 54–90 s range to 25.5%. However, Figure 11 presents different ROC cutoffs and AUCs, and Figure 10 gives n = 110 despite text indicating the n = 80 derivation set. Exact threshold estimates therefore need clarification before operational use.

3.5 Classification concordance

The text reports 46/110 (41.8%) concordant and 64/110 (58.2%) discordant paired classifications, with 8 subtherapeutic, 32 therapeutic, and 6 supratherapeutic concordant pairs. Table 2 shows numerically higher discordance in post-cardiac surgery (73.3%) and hemodialysis/CRRT (75.0%) than ACS/VTE groups, but the subgroup comparison was not significant (p = 0.156).

Figure 12 does not numerically reconcile with the prose: its displayed diagonal totals 57 rather than 46. That conflict affects one of the paper’s central findings and is a major reason not to reuse the exact category matrix without clarification.

3.6 Predictive models

Random forest achieved mean AUC 0.79 (95% CI 0.71–0.87), accuracy 78.8%, sensitivity 64.3%, and specificity 89.5%; permutation importance ranked eGFR, fibrinogen, AT-III, triglycerides, and CRP among the top variables. Logistic regression achieved AUC 0.726 (95% CI 0.638–0.814), accuracy 68.2%, sensitivity 73.1%, and specificity 63.8%.

Logistic regression identified fibrinogen (OR 3.67), triglycerides (OR 1.50), eGFR (OR 1.03), CRP (OR 1.07), and AT-III (OR 1.015) as independent predictors, with acceptable reported VIF and Hosmer–Lemeshow fit. These estimates remain unstable for clinical implementation because the model is small, selected from 10 candidates, and externally unvalidated.

4 Discussion

The authors position the assay as a lower-cost, rapid alternative to commercial anti-Xa testing and argue that direct anti-Xa measurement may be preferable when APTT is confounded. They report ≤5-minute turnaround and roughly US$2 reagent cost per test. They interpret 58.2% discordance as evidence of APTT limitations, especially in high-risk populations.

The discussion also proposes a selective monitoring framework based on the five model variables. Importantly, the authors repeatedly state that the framework is preliminary and should not guide practice without prospective outcome validation. Figure 14 contains conflicting threshold/branch logic, reinforcing that caution.

5 Conclusion and disclosures

The conclusion states that the in-house anti-Xa assay had analytical performance comparable to commercial systems, that APTT/anti-Xa categorical agreement was poor, and that a local APTT range of 79–127 s was derived. The authors emphasize that predictive findings are hypothesis-generating because of the single-center design, small sample, no external validation, and absence of clinical endpoint data.

ZONCI Technology Development Co., Ltd. funded the work; one author was employed by ZONCI. The sponsor was reported to have participated in study design but not data collection, analysis, interpretation, or writing. The article states that original contributions are in the article/supplementary material and that generative AI was not used to create the manuscript.

Verdict: Read first — for heparin monitoring/assay-development context, not as a practice standard.

Strengths

  • Clear analytical-development objective with concrete reagent conditions and validation targets.
  • Direct comparison to a commercial anti-Xa platform and multiple analytical performance domains.
  • Explicit acknowledgment of overfitting risk and the need for external validation.
  • Addresses a clinically important problem: therapeutic-category discordance between APTT and anti-Xa.

Principal limitations

  • Single-center, small clinical dataset; only 64 discordant events for 10 candidate predictors.
  • No bleeding, thrombosis, mortality, or dosing-outcome linkage.
  • No external validation for either the prediction model or the proposed monitoring framework.
  • Multiple internal figure/text inconsistencies affecting denominators, ROC thresholds, concordance counts, regression equations, and algorithm thresholds.
  • Local APTT range is intrinsically reagent/laboratory-specific and cannot be generalized directly.
  • Industry funding with sponsor involvement in study design; one author employed by sponsor.

Confidence in appraisal: high. The full PDF text and all main-text visuals/tables were inspected. Confidence in the paper’s exact clinical threshold values is lower because of the source-level contradictions documented above.

Do not operationalizeDo not implement the 79–127 s range, Figure 14 algorithm, or predictor cutoffs directly from this paper without resolving source discrepancies and validating locally/prospectively.
