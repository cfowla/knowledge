Scope warning. This is a small, nonrandomized, retrospective comparison with a selected CDT group. It can describe this center's historical experience and anticoagulation/CDT protocol details, but it does not establish therapeutic equivalence or current standard of care.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In 149 patients with acute lower-extremity DVT, the study found no statistically significant difference between anticoagulation alone (ACA; n=120) and CDT plus anticoagulation (n=29) in lesion-level thrombus removal or recurrence-free survival. Four transfusion-requiring puncture-site bleeds occurred after CDT. The authors concluded that ACA retained a crucial treatment role.

acute DVTUFHwarfarinCDTurokinaseaPTT monitoringretrospective

What the study actually tested. Patients were not randomized. ACA used IV unfractionated heparin with a target aPTT 1.5-2.5 times control, then warfarin with INR 2.0-3.0 for at least 6 months. The CDT protocol selected patients with proximal DVT, symptoms ≤14 days, good functional status, life expectancy ≥2 years, and low bleeding risk; it included enoxaparin 1 mg/kg on diagnosis, catheter urokinase 600-1,200 U/kg/h for 24-48 hours, and concurrent heparin through the access sheath with aPTT checked every 4 hours and maintained at 1.5-2.0 times control.

Main results. Overall thrombus removal was 91.1% (256/281 lesions) with ACA versus 87.1% (74/85) with CDT (P=0.273). Recurrence-free rates at 3 years were 63.8% versus 74.3%, but the full curves did not differ by log-rank test (P=0.594). The study did not measure post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), a major patient-centered rationale for thrombus-removal strategies. There was also a baseline malignancy imbalance (7.5% ACA vs 24.1% CDT; P=0.017).

Interpretive constraint: “No significant difference” is not evidence of equivalence or noninferiority. The study was small, especially in the CDT arm, had acknowledged selection bias, did not adjust the key comparisons for baseline imbalance, and treated multiple lesions per patient as lesion-level observations without reporting a clustering-aware analysis.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames anticoagulation as primary treatment and CDT as an additional strategy, then reports a retrospective comparison of 120 ACA and 29 CDT-plus-anticoagulation patients. It emphasizes thrombus distribution, lesion-level thrombus removal, and recurrence-free follow-up.

The abstract reports 91.1% versus 87.0% overall thrombus removal (P=0.273) and no significant recurrence-free difference (P=0.594), concluding that ACA remains important. Results text later gives the CDT removal value as 87.1% (74/85), a minor internal rounding discrepancy.

Introduction

The introduction summarizes DVT/VTE burden and guideline-era rationale for anticoagulation, while describing CDT as a method for rapid, localized thrombolysis. It also notes prior concerns about bleeding, mortality, length of stay, and cost with CDT.

The study question is therefore comparative rather than mechanistic: whether this center's acute DVT patients treated with ACA differed from those receiving CDT plus anticoagulation in thrombus removal, recurrence, and complications.

Materials and Methods

The study was a retrospective single-center registry review of 149 patients from 2011-2015. Inclusion required age >20 years, first-episode acute DVT with swelling and symptoms <14 days, and life expectancy >2 years; major bleeding risk conditions and several other clinical states were excluded. CT venography was used for diagnosis and follow-up, generally every 3-6 months clinically, with follow-up CT within at least the first 6 months and when patency was questioned. Ten patients with IVC thrombus received temporary IVC filters, all removed within 1 month.

ACA consisted of IV UFH targeting aPTT 1.5-2.5× control followed by warfarin INR 2.0-3.0 for ≥6 months. CDT candidates had proximal DVT, symptoms ≤14 days, good function, ≥2-year life expectancy, and low bleeding risk. The CDT subsection reports enoxaparin 1 mg/kg on diagnosis, popliteal venous access, a multisidehole infusion catheter, urokinase 600-1,200 U/kg/h for 24-48 hours, concurrent sheath heparin with aPTT every 4 hours targeting 1.5-2.0× control, and repeat venography after 24-48 hours. Analyses used chi-square/Fisher exact, independent t-tests, and log-rank testing with P<0.05.

Results

The groups were 120 ACA and 29 CDT patients. Most baseline variables were not statistically different, but malignancy was more common in CDT patients (24.1% vs 7.5%; P=0.017). Across 281 ACA lesions and 85 CDT lesions, femoral and popliteal veins made up most recorded lesions. Overall thrombus removal was 91.1% vs 87.1% (P=0.273); no lesion-specific comparison reached P<0.05, with the largest nominal difference in popliteal removal (91.0% ACA vs 77.8% CDT; P=0.089).

Recurrence-free rates favored ACA early but CDT numerically at 3 years: ACA 100.0%, 98.1%, 85.5%, 79.4%, and 63.8% at 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, 2 years, and 3 years; CDT 93.1%, 89.4%, 81.1%, 74.3%, and 74.3%. The log-rank P-value was 0.594. There were no in-hospital deaths. Four CDT patients had puncture-site bleeding requiring transfusion and bleeding control; the authors also state there was no cerebral or gastrointestinal bleeding.

Discussion and conclusion

The discussion interprets the findings as showing anticoagulation was not inferior to CDT, but the study was not designed or powered as a noninferiority trial. It places the findings alongside prior literature on PTS, recurrence, bleeding, and evolving oral anticoagulants.

The authors explicitly acknowledge retrospective design, selection bias, a much smaller CDT group, and inability to assess PTS. Their final conclusion is narrower than some discussion language: lesion-level thrombus removal and recurrence-free rates did not significantly differ, and ACA retained a crucial role.

Conflicts of Interest

The authors state that they had nothing to disclose. The PDF does not present a separate funding statement or data-availability statement.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best use is historical protocol context for UFH/aPTT and CDT workflow plus a cautionary example of weak comparative observational evidence. Do not use this paper alone to choose CDT versus anticoagulation, infer equivalence, or define current anticoagulant selection.

Clinical question / PICO

P: Adults >20 years with first-episode acute lower-extremity DVT and symptoms <14 days; n=149.
I: CDT plus anticoagulation; n=29. Selected proximal DVT, good function, low bleeding risk, life expectancy >=2 years.
C: Anticoagulation alone; n=120. IV UFH -> warfarin.
O: CT-venography thrombus removal; recurrence-free rate to 3 years; in-hospital complications.
Not measured: post-thrombotic syndrome.
7/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly relevant to acute DVT anticoagulation and includes explicit UFH/aPTT and CDT-heparin protocol details.

Evidence basis: Treatment regimens, lesion outcomes, recurrence, and bleeding are all reported.

Principal limit: Primary question is CDT strategy rather than heparin pharmacology or dosing optimization.

Would change with: Higher if the paper directly compared heparin regimens, exposure-response, or monitoring targets.

4/10

Novelty

Rationale: The study adds a single-center real-world comparison but does not introduce a new treatment mechanism or rigorous causal design.

Evidence basis: Retrospective institutional registry comparison with protocol description.

Principal limit: The paper itself cites prior comparative CDT and anticoagulation evidence.

Would change with: Higher with a prospectively specified question, novel dosing strategy, or new endpoint.

3/10

Method strength

Rationale: The design is vulnerable to confounding and selection bias, with a very small CDT arm and no reported adjusted comparative model.

Evidence basis: n=120 vs 29; malignancy 7.5% vs 24.1% (P=0.017); authors acknowledge selection bias.

Principal limit: Nonrandomized allocation and lesion-level analyses with multiple lesions per patient.

Would change with: Higher with randomization or robust propensity/multivariable adjustment, clustering-aware analyses, and prospective follow-up.

3/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: The source supports descriptive rates but not therapeutic equivalence or noninferiority.

Evidence basis: Overall removal P=0.273; recurrence log-rank P=0.594; four transfusion-requiring CDT bleeds.

Principal limit: Wide uncertainty is not quantified with confidence intervals; PTS was not measured.

Would change with: Higher with adequately powered patient-centered endpoints, effect estimates with uncertainty, and prespecified safety definitions.

4/10

External validity

Rationale: Generalizability is limited by single-center selection, historical warfarin-era management, and a narrowly selected CDT population.

Evidence basis: One Korean center, 2011-2015 registry, explicit CDT eligibility filters.

Principal limit: No external validation or multicenter replication within the study.

Would change with: Higher with contemporary multicenter cohorts spanning broader patient and anticoagulant profiles.

5/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The procedural and anticoagulation details are specific enough to reconstruct the historical workflow, but the comparative findings should not drive practice.

Evidence basis: UFH, warfarin, enoxaparin, urokinase, aPTT targets, and catheter steps are described.

Principal limit: Current practice has evolved and the study did not test PTS or modern oral-anticoagulant pathways.

Would change with: Higher with current guideline alignment, standardized bleeding definitions, operational monitoring data, and contemporary anticoagulants.

Major strengths

  • Full treatment protocols are unusually concrete for a short comparative paper, including aPTT targets and thrombolytic dosing.
  • Three-year recurrence-free follow-up is reported.
  • The authors explicitly acknowledge retrospective design, selection bias, sample-size imbalance, and inability to evaluate PTS.
  • All three tables and the recurrence figure can be reconciled with the narrative.

Major limitations

  • Nonrandom treatment assignment and explicit selection of lower-bleeding-risk, good-functional-status CDT candidates.
  • Small CDT arm (n=29), limiting power and precision.
  • Baseline malignancy imbalance (24.1% CDT vs 7.5% ACA; P=0.017) with no reported adjusted outcome analysis.
  • Lesion-level denominators (281 and 85) exceed patient counts, yet no patient-level clustering adjustment is described.
  • PTS was not measured despite being central to the rationale for early thrombus removal.
  • Transfusion-requiring puncture-site bleeding is reported, but the source does not define its major-bleeding classification.
  • Internal numerical inconsistencies exist for age P-value, CDT overall-removal rounding, and calf-vein prose order.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build/useful detail from the source: For historical protocol reconstruction, ACA used IV UFH to aPTT 1.5-2.5× control before warfarin INR 2-3, while CDT used concurrent sheath heparin with aPTT 1.5-2.0× control checked every 4 hours plus urokinase 600-1,200 U/kg/h for 24-48 hours.
Do not overclaim: A nonsignificant P-value in this underpowered observational study does not demonstrate equivalence, noninferiority, or that CDT provides no patient-centered benefit.

Current-practice verification (external; not source evidence)

Current NICE NG158 guidance, as available in 2026, says to consider catheter-directed thrombolysis for symptomatic iliofemoral DVT when symptoms are <14 days, functional status is good, life expectancy is at least 1 year, and bleeding risk is low. The paper's CDT selection resembles this framework but uses the broader label “proximal” DVT and a ≥2-year life-expectancy threshold. NICE also advises against routine IVC filters for proximal DVT/PE unless specific indications such as contraindication to anticoagulation or PE during anticoagulation apply, which differs from this study's historical insertion of filters in 10 patients with IVC thrombus.

A 2025 European Society of Vascular Medicine guideline confirms that catheter-based DVT interventions remain an area requiring selective indications, expert decision-making, and experienced centers. This reinforces using the 2019 study as contextual evidence rather than a stand-alone practice standard.

External sources used only for currency check: NICE guideline NG158, recommendations 1.6.1 and 1.7.1-1.7.3; 2025 ESVM Guidelines on interventional treatment of venous thromboembolism, DOI 10.1024/0301-1526/a001211.
