One-page synthesis

The review was written near the start of Wegovy's obesity indication. It pulls together early subcutaneous semaglutide data from SUSTAIN and STEP and oral semaglutide data from PIONEER. The useful part is the compact trial-by-trial presentation. Across many SUSTAIN and PIONEER studies, semaglutide reduced body weight more than placebo and several active diabetes comparators. The early STEP obesity trials showed a much larger weight-loss signal at 2.4 mg weekly. In STEP 1, Table 5 reports mean weight change of -14.9% with semaglutide 2.4 mg and -2.4% with placebo. The proportions losing more than 5% of body weight were 86.4% and 31.5%.

The review is much weaker as a comparative evidence synthesis. The authors used two search terms, report 127 results, selected the major semaglutide programs, and excluded three SUSTAIN trials for comparator redundancy. They did not report a systematic search strategy, formal screening process, risk-of-bias assessment, or meta-analysis. They also acknowledge that oral versus subcutaneous comparisons are indirect and were not tested with original data. A source inconsistency in Table 4 adds another reason to use the article as a map to the primary studies rather than as a final evidence source.

Verdict: Do not use for current practice decisions. Best use is historical orientation to the early semaglutide trial programs and rapid identification of primary studies worth reading.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract and introduction

The article frames obesity as a major chronic disease and introduces GLP-1 receptor agonists as treatments that can improve glycemia and reduce body weight. It sets out the review's main task: compare weight-loss findings across SUSTAIN, PIONEER, and STEP and discuss Wegovy after its 2021 FDA approval.

The mechanistic discussion centers on appetite and energy intake, including central and peripheral GLP-1 pathways, reduced food intake, and delayed gastric emptying. The article also states that nausea and vomiting explain little of the observed weight loss in SUSTAIN.

Methods

The authors searched NCBI resources using the terms "semaglutide" and "obesity". They report 127 results and focus on SUSTAIN, PIONEER, and STEP because they were original randomized controlled trials conducted before semaglutide approvals. Three of 13 SUSTAIN trials were excluded for redundant comparators. The latest search was July 2021.

The method is closer to a targeted narrative review than a systematic review. The article does not give database-specific full search strings, a study-flow diagram, independent screening, risk-of-bias assessment, evidence grading, or pooled effect estimates.

SUSTAIN

SUSTAIN primarily studied subcutaneous semaglutide in type 2 diabetes. Table 1 shows weight reductions across ten trials, usually larger with semaglutide than placebo or active comparators. Examples include -6.1 kg with 1.0 mg semaglutide versus -1.9 kg with sitagliptin in SUSTAIN 2, and -5.8 kg versus -1.9 kg with liraglutide 1.2 mg in SUSTAIN 10.

Safety is dominated by gastrointestinal events. Table 2 reports GI adverse-event rates such as 40% with semaglutide 1.0 mg versus 24% with sitagliptin in SUSTAIN 2 and 47% versus 28% with canagliflozin in SUSTAIN 8. Hypoglycemia risk varies with background therapy and comparator.

PIONEER

PIONEER studied oral semaglutide in type 2 diabetes. Table 3 shows dose-dependent weight effects in several trials. PIONEER 4 reports -4.4 kg with oral semaglutide 14 mg, -3.1 kg with liraglutide 1.8 mg, and -0.5 kg with placebo. PIONEER 8 reports -3.7 kg with 14 mg oral semaglutide versus -0.4 kg with placebo.

The safety table reports high overall adverse-event rates across arms. The article's narrative says mild to moderate nausea and vomiting were most common and that oral semaglutide did not show an increased hypoglycemia signal. Table 4 has an internal problem: PIONEER 10 values in the column labeled GI-event discontinuation reach 54%, while the narrative says PIONEER GI-event discontinuation ranged from 4.9% to 12%. Those entries should be checked against the primary paper.

STEP

STEP is the most relevant program for obesity treatment. Table 5 reports -14.9% mean weight change with semaglutide 2.4 mg versus -2.4% with placebo in STEP 1. STEP 2 reports -9.6% with 2.4 mg, -7.0% with 1.0 mg, and -3.4% with placebo. STEP 3 reports -16.0% with 2.4 mg plus the trial's behavioral program versus -5.7% with placebo plus intensive behavioral therapy.

STEP 4 supports treatment continuation. Participants who continued semaglutide after the run-in had further mean weight change of -7.9%, while those switched to placebo had +6.9%. GI adverse events remained common. Table 6 reports 74.2% with semaglutide versus 47.9% with placebo in STEP 1.

Comparisons, precautions, limitations, and conclusion

The authors compare semaglutide with other antiobesity medications and discuss route preference, safety, and long-term use. They recognize that cross-program comparisons are indirect. The review also discusses nausea, vomiting, increased lipase, pancreatitis concern, retinopathy complications, gallbladder events, and cardiovascular events.

The conclusion is more confident than the method supports. It calls semaglutide superior to other antidiabetic drugs for weight reduction and cardioprotective, then identifies cost and insurance coverage as practical barriers. The authors explicitly say that direct oral versus subcutaneous comparisons were not available and that their own comparisons should not be treated as definitive.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 7/10

Useful for understanding the early semaglutide obesity evidence base, but the treatment landscape has changed since the 2021 search.

Evidence basis. The review covers the SUSTAIN, PIONEER, and early STEP programs and reports clinically interpretable weight-loss and safety values.

Main limit. The source predates later obesity outcomes, newer Wegovy formulations, new doses, and added indications.

What changes the score. A current systematic review focused on obesity outcomes and current labeling would score higher.

Novelty 4/10

The article compiles existing trial programs and adds no new patient-level data.

Evidence basis. Its main contribution is side-by-side tables and an early comparison of oral and subcutaneous semaglutide evidence.

Main limit. The synthesis is descriptive and relies on already published trials.

What changes the score. A formal comparative synthesis with prespecified methods or new pooled analyses would raise the score.

Method strength 3/10

The search is reproducible only at a basic level and is not a systematic-review method.

Evidence basis. The authors report two search terms, 127 results, selected trial programs, three SUSTAIN exclusions, and a July 2021 search date.

Main limit. No full search strings, study-selection flow, duplicate screening, risk-of-bias assessment, or quantitative synthesis are reported.

What changes the score. A protocol, database-specific search strategy, transparent selection process, risk-of-bias review, and prespecified synthesis would raise the score.

Evidence strength 5/10

The underlying evidence includes many randomized trials, but this review does not independently reanalyze them and makes indirect comparisons.

Evidence basis. The tables preserve trial-level efficacy and safety values across SUSTAIN, PIONEER, and STEP.

Main limit. The authors did not test oral versus subcutaneous differences using original data. Table 4 also contains an internal inconsistency in a safety column.

What changes the score. Direct head-to-head data, formal evidence grading, and reconciled source tables would raise the score.

External validity 5/10

The underlying trials are multinational, but representation and target populations vary across programs.

Evidence basis. The review reports 60%-93% White participation in many programs, limited Black or Asian representation, and a STEP population that was mostly women.

Main limit. Much of SUSTAIN and PIONEER studied type 2 diabetes rather than the broader obesity population targeted by Wegovy.

What changes the score. Broader contemporary obesity cohorts and subgroup analyses would raise the score.

Implementation value 3/10

The review is not a current practice reference in 2026.

Evidence basis. It gives early efficacy, safety, and dosing context for semaglutide and notes cost and insurance barriers.

Main limit. The FDA label has changed substantially since publication, including oral Wegovy tablets, a 7.2 mg injection strength, cardiovascular risk-reduction use, and a MASH indication. Some safety statements in the review should not be read as current labeling.

What changes the score. A current label-aligned review with current comparative therapy, monitoring, access, and cost information would raise the score.

Main methodological problem. The article summarizes strong underlying randomized trials but does not use systematic-review methods. Its confidence sometimes exceeds what an indirect narrative comparison can support.
What the source does well. It gives a compact early map of semaglutide trial programs and preserves many treatment-arm magnitudes that make primary-study triage faster.

Current-status verification for 2026

This section is external verification, not source-derived evidence. The FDA label dated June 18, 2026 lists Wegovy injection and Wegovy tablets. It includes weight reduction and long-term maintenance, cardiovascular risk reduction in adults with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity, and a noncirrhotic MASH indication with F2-F3 fibrosis under accelerated approval. The 2026 label also includes a 7.2 mg Wegovy HD injection strength and 25 mg oral maintenance dosing for adult cardiovascular risk reduction and weight reduction.

That makes several 2021 statements stale. The review describes Wegovy as 2.4 mg subcutaneous semaglutide and treats oral semaglutide mainly as diabetes evidence. It also lists impaired renal function and pancreatitis history in a way that should not be read as current FDA contraindication language. Current labeling should control dosing, contraindications, warnings, and indication decisions.
