One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. This is a strong, practice-relevant pediatric ED randomized trial with an actionable dispensing strategy. The primary dichotomous disease-severity endpoint favored ondansetron, while several secondary/utilization outcomes were neutral or imprecise and actual multidose use was low.

Core finding. Moderate-to-severe gastroenteritis (MVS ≥9) occurred in 5.1% (23/452) of ondansetron versus 12.5% (55/441) of placebo participants, an unadjusted absolute difference of -7.4 percentage points (95% CI -11.2 to -3.7). With imputation and adjustment for site and weight, the OR was 0.50 (95% CI 0.40-0.60); the authors reported NNT 15 (95% CI 9-43).

What changed and what did not. Ondansetron reduced vomiting episode burden (48-hour adjusted rate ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.67-0.87) and vomiting duration (rate ratio 0.74, 95% CI 0.62-0.88). It did not clearly reduce the proportion with any vomiting, repeat IV-fluid use, or caregiver satisfaction. Unscheduled visits numerically favored ondansetron but the CI included no effect (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.44-1.02). Overall adverse-event incidence was similar (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.61-1.61).

Important interpretation. The protocol supplied up to six doses, but actual use was sparse: only 7.8% versus 10.7% received at least three doses among those with dose data, and the discussion reports mean use 0.78 dose, median 0. This makes the study most directly a trial of access to as-needed home ondansetron, not of routinely scheduled multiple-dose exposure.

Section condensation

Background / rationale

Single-dose ED ondansetron already reduces IV fluid use and hospitalization in pediatric gastroenteritis, but postdischarge prescribing had become common despite inconsistent trial and database evidence. The study was designed to address this evidence-practice gap.

The investigators focused on children with recent, frequent vomiting and used an as-needed strategy because many children do not continue vomiting after discharge.

Methods

Six tertiary-care pediatric EDs randomized eligible children 1:1 using block sizes of 4 and 6, stratified by site and weight. Ondansetron/placebo solutions were matched; participants, clinical/trial staff, and analysts were blinded. Follow-up surveys occurred at 24 hours, 48 hours, and day 7.

The analysis was prespecified, intention-to-treat, and used multiple imputation for regression analyses with missing data. Secondary outcomes were adjusted for site and weight; Holm adjustment controlled multiplicity for secondary outcomes and interaction tests. No interim analysis of primary/secondary outcomes was planned.

Results

Of 1,030 randomized children, 989 (96.0%) completed at least one follow-up and 893 (86.7%) completed all surveys. The primary dichotomous MVS outcome favored ondansetron across unadjusted and adjusted analyses. The fully covariate-adjusted supplemental model estimated OR 0.46 (95% CI 0.27-0.78).

Vomiting burden was lower, but not every secondary outcome improved. The continuous MVS difference was -0.47 points (95% CI -0.99 to 0.06). Overall adverse events were similar. A prespecified ≥3-dose safety subgroup had more diarrheal stools with ondansetron (RR 1.36, 95% CI 1.03-1.80).

Discussion / limitations

The authors interpret the trial as supporting a small supply for as-needed home use. They explicitly note that actual dose use was low and argue that this limits unnecessary exposure. No arrhythmias or masked serious alternative diagnoses were observed in the trial.

Source-reported limitations include possible recall bias from aggregating days 3-7 into one survey, cautions inherent to a composite MVS outcome, and dependence of utilization-linked outcomes on Canadian health-care-use patterns.

Conclusion

The source concludes that providing ondansetron after an ED visit to children with recent and frequent gastroenteritis-associated vomiting was beneficial.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 8/10

Rationale: Directly informs ED/postdischarge pediatric antiemetic practice and medication-supply decisions.

Evidence basis: Randomized practice-facing comparison with clinical and utilization outcomes.

Limiting factor: The population is a specific pediatric ED subgroup.

Would raise/lower: Broader replication across care systems and eligibility groups.

Novelty 8/10

Rationale: Directly tests the commonly used but previously weakly supported practice of postdischarge ondansetron access.

Evidence basis: Large multicenter RCT specifically addressing home use after ED treatment.

Limiting factor: Prior smaller trials and observational studies existed.

Would raise/lower: Independent replication or direct comparison of supply strategies.

Method strength 9/10

Rationale: Large double-blind placebo-controlled multicenter RCT with prespecified analysis and strong follow-up.

Evidence basis: 1,030 randomized; blinding, stratification, ITT, multiple imputation, multiplicity control; protocol/SAP available.

Limiting factor: Caregiver-reported follow-up and low actual multidose exposure complicate interpretation.

Would raise/lower: Objective outcome capture and higher intervention exposure without sacrificing pragmatism.

Evidence strength 8/10

Rationale: The dichotomous primary outcome showed a consistent clinically meaningful benefit, but not all secondary/alternate analyses were positive.

Evidence basis: Primary 5.1% vs 12.5%; adjusted OR 0.50; NNT 15; safety similar.

Limiting factor: Continuous MVS CI crossed zero; health-care/IV-fluid outcomes were not clearly different; exploratory estimates lacked multiplicity adjustment.

Would raise/lower: Replication with a patient-centered primary endpoint and confirmatory utilization outcomes.

External validity 7/10

Rationale: Six Canadian pediatric EDs improve multicenter generalizability, but utilization-linked outcomes and exclusions constrain transfer.

Evidence basis: Broad pediatric age range and multiple sites; Supplementary Table S4 addresses representativeness.

Limiting factor: Canadian health-care-use patterns and QT/medication exclusions; all participants had already received ED ondansetron.

Would raise/lower: Replication in U.S./other systems and broader real-world populations.

Implementation value 9/10

Rationale: The trial gives a concrete as-needed dose, maximum dose, interval, and limited-supply strategy that can be operationalized.

Evidence basis: 0.15 mg/kg oral up to 8 mg, q8h as needed for 48 h; authors support a small number of doses.

Limiting factor: It does not test a fixed scheduled multidose regimen or every discharge population.

Would raise/lower: Direct trials comparing two-dose versus larger-supply dispensing strategies.

Main strengths. Randomized double-blind design; multicenter enrollment; 1,030 randomized; prespecified protocol/SAP; high follow-up; clinically interpretable absolute effect; detailed supplement; explicit safety follow-up.
Main limitations. Caregiver-reported follow-up creates recall risk; composite primary outcome includes utilization/treatment components; continuous MVS analysis was not clearly different; actual multidose exposure was low; some potentially practice-important secondary outcomes were imprecise; exploratory CIs were not multiplicity-adjusted.
Independent appraisal inference. Because most children used few or no additional doses, the clearest causal interpretation is that making a small as-needed home supply available improved the dichotomized 7-day severity outcome in this selected population. It would overstate the study to describe it as proof that routinely scheduled multiple ondansetron doses are superior.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Potentially practice-changing
For trial-eligible children already treated with ondansetron in the ED, a small as-needed supply at discharge is evidence-supported.
Dose/interval used
0.15 mg/kg PO, max 8 mg, as needed no more often than q8h, during the first 48 hours.
Do not overgeneralize
Not tested as scheduled therapy; do not erase trial exclusions for QT/arrhythmia risk, interacting medications, bilious/hematemesis presentations, or other excluded states.
Monitoring / counseling
Use should remain symptom-triggered; counsel on diarrhea and return precautions; the study did not find an overall AE excess.

Best use: inform ED discharge protocols, medication-dispensing quantities, and pharmacist/clinician counseling for eligible pediatric AGE patients.

Do not use for: universal outpatient ondansetron prescribing, scheduled multidose regimens, or populations excluded from the trial.
