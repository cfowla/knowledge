One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Cerebral schistosomiasis can mimic an intracranial tumor in migrants or travelers from endemic areas. In these three patients, seizures and mass-like MRI abnormalities led to biopsy, which showed Schistosoma eggs with granulomatous inflammation.

The three patients were Filipino men aged 25, 26, and 29 years. All presented with tonic-clonic seizures. MRI showed multifocal or mass-like T2/FLAIR abnormalities with nodular or irregular enhancement. Histopathology established the diagnosis in each case. Cases 1 and 2 had positive S. mansoni serology, while stool examination was negative in both reported cases with stool testing. The source does not establish species by molecular typing.

All three received praziquantel plus corticosteroid therapy. Case 3 also received phenytoin. Cases 1 and 3 had complete symptom resolution at 3 and 4 months, respectively, with marked MRI improvement reported in Case 3. Case 2 improved but needed repeated corticosteroid courses for recurrent neurological symptoms. These outcomes are uncontrolled and should not be read as treatment-effect estimates.

Best use. Keep neuroschistosomiasis in the differential for seizures with tumor-like brain lesions when epidemiologic exposure fits, and recognize biopsy histopathology and nodular MRI enhancement as the main diagnostic lessons from this report.
Do not use for. Choosing a treatment regimen, deciding whether biopsy can be omitted, estimating the performance of MRI or serology, or assigning a Schistosoma species with certainty.

Section-by-section summary

Abstract and introduction

The authors frame neuroschistosomiasis as a rare, severe manifestation of schistosomiasis caused by eggs lodging in the central nervous system and provoking granulomatous inflammation. They report three patients in Qatar with seizures and tumor-like brain lesions. The abstract states that biopsy confirmed the diagnosis by showing necrotizing granulomas containing Schistosoma eggs.

The introduction provides epidemiologic and treatment background from cited literature, including global schistosomiasis burden, major species, mass drug administration with praziquantel, and an estimated 2-4% frequency of cerebral manifestations among infected persons. These numbers come from cited sources, not from the three-patient series.

Case 1

A 29-year-old Filipino man presented in December 2016 with generalized tonic-clonic seizures. He had lived in Qatar for 4 years and reported lake swimming during a trip to the Philippines 1 year earlier. He had no focal neurologic deficit. CSF had 11 × 106 white cells/L with normal protein and glucose. Stool examination did not detect parasitic eggs.

MRI showed left anterior temporal and frontal gyrus T2/FLAIR hyperintensity with nodular and linear enhancement. Temporal-lesion biopsy showed necrotizing granulomas containing Schistosoma eggs, some calcified. S. mansoni serology was positive. He received praziquantel 60 mg/kg per day for 1 day and prednisolone 30 mg daily for 1 week followed by a 5 mg weekly taper. He was discharged after 26 days and had complete symptom resolution at 3 months.

Case 2

A previously healthy 26-year-old Filipino man presented in May 2018 after two tonic-clonic seizures and 4 months of intermittent headache. Physical and neurologic examinations were normal. S. mansoni serology was positive and stool examination did not detect parasitic eggs.

MRI showed right parietal and left frontal and parieto-occipital subcortical mass lesions, edema, and mass effect. Frozen section was reported as gliosis, while final histopathology showed Schistosoma eggs surrounded by granulomatous inflammation with perivascular lymphocytes. He received praziquantel 60 mg/kg per day plus prednisolone 50 mg per day for 2 weeks. He was discharged after 14 days and improved, but recurrent neurologic symptoms required repeated corticosteroid courses.

Case 3

A 25-year-old Filipino man presented in September 2010 after two tonic-clonic seizures. He was confused, agitated, and disoriented without focal neurologic deficit. CSF was normal. MRI showed three right-hemisphere T2/FLAIR lesions involving frontal, occipital, and parieto-occipital regions with cortical thickening and post-contrast enhancement. EEG showed right frontal epileptiform and ictal discharges spreading to the left.

Frameless stereotactic biopsy of the right parietal lesions showed Schistosoma eggs surrounded by marked granulomatous inflammation. He received praziquantel 60 mg/kg per day, dexamethasone 12 mg per day, and phenytoin. He was discharged after 14 days. At 4 months he reported complete symptom resolution and repeat MRI showed marked improvement.

Discussion and literature review

The authors place their cases within prior reports of cerebral and spinal neuroschistosomiasis. They describe headache, cognitive impairment, seizures, and focal deficits as reported presentations. They treat MRI as the main imaging test and emphasize clustered nodular post-contrast enhancement as a characteristic but non-specific pattern. They caution that serology may remain positive after infection and may cross-react with other helminths. They describe tissue biopsy as the confirmatory standard used in their cases.

The treatment discussion is narrative and based largely on cited literature. The authors describe praziquantel plus corticosteroids as the usual approach, note reports of deterioration with praziquantel during acute disease, and cite recommendations to use corticosteroids first in acute neuroschistosomiasis. They also cite a 250-case surgical epilepsy report in which 91.8% became seizure-free after resection. None of these secondary claims were generated by the three Qatar cases.

Conclusion and disclosures

The authors recommend a high level of suspicion when seizures and tumor-like lesions occur in people from or returning from endemic areas. They state that nodular MRI enhancement should raise suspicion but is not pathognomonic. They also explain why they were not comfortable relying on parasitology or serology alone when a neoplasm remained possible.

The article reports institutional ethics approval, no financial support, and no author conflict of interest related to publication.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. The article is useful for recognizing a rare tumor mimic and for linking MRI appearance to biopsy pathology. It is weak evidence for treatment decisions.
Relevance: 7/10Useful for infectious disease, neurology, radiology, and diagnostic reasoning. Evidence basis: three biopsy-confirmed cases with detailed imaging and treatment descriptions. Limiting factor: rarity and narrow population. A larger multicenter series with a defined case-finding method would raise the score.
Novelty: 6/10The report adds three unusual cerebral presentations from Qatar and shows tumor-like imaging paired with histopathology. Limiting factor: neuroschistosomiasis and similar imaging patterns were already described in the cited literature. Molecular species confirmation or a distinct diagnostic phenotype would raise the score.
Method strength: 4/10Biopsy gives strong case-level diagnostic confirmation. The article does not report a systematic case-identification method, eligibility criteria, comparator, or prespecified outcomes. Prospective case ascertainment with explicit inclusion criteria would raise the score.
Evidence strength: 4/10The diagnostic observations are credible at the individual case level. Treatment response remains uncontrolled and species attribution is incomplete. Comparative diagnostic testing, molecular confirmation, and standardized follow-up would raise the score.
External validity: 4/10All three patients were young Filipino men treated at one institution. The pattern may apply to migrants and travelers with similar exposure, but the sample does not show how common or specific these findings are. Broader geography and more diverse cases would raise the score.
Implementation value: 6/10The article gives concrete exposure, imaging, pathology, and treatment details that can improve recognition of a rare diagnosis. It does not define a validated diagnostic pathway or treatment protocol. A prospective diagnostic algorithm with performance data would raise the score.

Main strengths

  • All three cases had tissue confirmation rather than diagnosis based only on exposure or serology.
  • The article links clinical presentation, MRI patterns, histopathology, treatment, and follow-up at the case level.
  • The composite figure makes the tumor-mimic pattern easy to recognize.

Main limitations

  • Three patients from one center, with no stated systematic case-finding or selection method.
  • No comparator and no formal diagnostic accuracy or treatment-effect analysis.
  • Follow-up is brief and not uniform across cases.
  • Positive S. mansoni serology in Cases 1 and 2 does not equal molecular species confirmation. The source also discusses S. japonicum as common in the Philippines.
  • The literature review is narrative. It should not be treated as a current guideline or systematic evidence synthesis.

Implementation and practice takeaways

HistoryAsk about residence or travel in endemic areas and freshwater exposure when seizures accompany tumor-like lesions.
ImagingNodular or clustered post-contrast enhancement can support suspicion, but the source does not show that the pattern is specific.
DiagnosisIn these cases, biopsy resolved the tumor-versus-infection uncertainty. Negative stool testing did not exclude cerebral disease.
TreatmentThe three patients received praziquantel plus corticosteroids, with an antiseizure drug in Case 3. These regimens are case descriptions, not comparative evidence.
SpeciesDo not overstate S. mansoni attribution. Serology was positive in two cases, but the source does not report molecular species typing.
