One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Unfractionated heparin is a heterogeneous, rapidly acting parenteral anticoagulant whose clinical response reflects antithrombin-mediated enzyme inhibition, variable protein/cell binding, nonlinear clearance, route-dependent bioavailability, and assay-dependent monitoring. The chapter links these pharmacologic properties to dosing, APTT interpretation, major toxicities, and the emerging advantages and unresolved risks of LMWHs.

The most durable conceptual contribution of the chapter is its integration of molecular size with pharmacology. A high-affinity pentasaccharide enables antithrombin binding; long chains are needed to bridge antithrombin and thrombin, whereas factor Xa inhibition does not require the same ternary complex. Heterogeneous chain length, plasma-protein binding, endothelial/macrophage uptake, and dose-dependent saturable clearance are used to explain variable bioavailability, nonlinear dose-response, and discordance between anti-Xa activity and APTT.

The clinical sections emphasize that APTT response varies substantially by reagent and patient state, so therapeutic interpretation should be calibrated to the local assay rather than treated as a universal ratio. The safety discussion distinguishes bleeding from less common but important complications: osteoporosis with prolonged high-dose exposure, heparin-associated thrombocytopenia/thrombosis, skin reactions, hypoaldosteronism, and hypersensitivity. The final section portrays LMWHs as having less nonspecific binding, better subcutaneous bioavailability, more predictable response, longer half-life, proportionally greater anti-Xa activity, and promising prevention/treatment efficacy, while explicitly noting unresolved questions around pregnancy, osteoporosis, thrombocytopenia, and HIT cross-reactivity.

Best use: foundational and historical understanding of heparin pharmacology, APTT assay dependence, and the transition from unfractionated heparin to LMWHs.
Do not use for: a standalone current dosing protocol, contemporary HIT treatment algorithm, or present-day laboratory therapeutic range without external verification.

Section condensation

Introduction and clinical scope

The chapter presents heparin as the anticoagulant of choice when immediate effect is needed and describes low-dose use for primary prophylaxis and high-dose use for treatment. It states that therapeutic dosing generally requires monitoring and frequent adjustment, and it frames oral anticoagulants as the usual longer-term continuation after a period of overlap.

The authors list established indications with evidence levels, including prevention and treatment of venous thromboembolism, unstable angina, acute myocardial infarction, cardiac bypass surgery, selected vascular surgery, and selected disseminated intravascular thrombosis. The stated purpose is to review mechanism, pharmacokinetics, anticoagulant effects, monitoring, clinical use, and the emerging LMWH class.

Structure and mechanism of action

Heparin is described as a heterogeneous glycosaminoglycan whose major anticoagulant effect depends on a high-affinity pentasaccharide sequence present in roughly one third of molecules. Binding to antithrombin produces a conformational change that markedly accelerates inhibition of thrombin, factor Xa, and factor IXa.

Thrombin inhibition requires heparin to act as a template binding both antithrombin and thrombin, whereas factor Xa inhibition requires only the antithrombin interaction. Chains shorter than 18 saccharides therefore lose the ability to catalyze thrombin inhibition but can retain anti-Xa activity. Molecular-weight heterogeneity is tied to differential clearance and the in-vivo accumulation of lower-molecular-weight species.

Administration, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics

Oral absorption is absent; intravenous and subcutaneous administration are preferred, and intramuscular injection is discouraged because of hematoma risk. Continuous IV infusion is favored over intermittent IV injection when the intravenous route is used. Subcutaneous dosing requires attention to reduced low-dose bioavailability and delayed onset.

The chapter attributes pharmacokinetic variability to binding with plasma proteins, endothelial cells, and macrophages. It describes a rapid saturable clearance pathway plus slower first-order renal clearance and uses dose-dependent half-life data (about 30, 60, and 150 minutes after 25, 100, and 400 U/kg IV boluses) to illustrate nonlinear kinetics. In a cited venous-thrombosis comparison, only 37% of the subcutaneous group versus 71% of the continuous-IV group had therapeutic levels/APTT ratios at 24 hours despite nominally equivalent daily dosing.

Laboratory monitoring and dose-response

APTT is presented as the usual monitoring assay for therapeutic heparin, but the response is variable across patients and across commercial reagents. The chapter links failure to reach a lower therapeutic threshold with thromboembolic events in subgroup analyses and historical studies, while acknowledging that APTT systems differ in sensitivity because of reagent composition, clot detection, and patient procoagulant states.

A historical APTT ratio of roughly 1.5-2.5 is linked to heparin 0.2-0.4 U/mL in the cited calibration. The authors explicitly reject a universal uncalibrated range and recommend local laboratory calibration. For rapid treatment they describe a 5,000 U IV bolus followed by 32,000 U/24 h continuous infusion and an APTT at about six hours; subcutaneous treatment is described with high initial daily dosing to overcome poor bioavailability.

Bleeding and osteoporosis

Bleeding is identified as the major complication, but clinical factors such as recent surgery, trauma, invasive procedures, local lesions, and generalized hemostatic abnormalities are described as at least as important as anticoagulant intensity in predicting bleeding. Table 2 classifies bleeding as common, thrombocytopenia/osteoporosis as uncommon, and several hypersensitivity/metabolic effects as rare.

The osteoporosis section relies on one small randomized trial plus descriptive series, case reports, and a case-control study. The source reports a dose-duration pattern, including 6/10 cases in a high-dose long-duration series versus 0/107 at lower doses, 1/20 cases in a small antenatal heparin arm, and 12/70 radiographic osteopenia cases in a pregnancy cohort versus 0/30 controls. The authors conclude that short courses are unlikely to produce clinically important osteoporosis and that prolonged high-dose exposure is the principal concern.

Thrombocytopenia and other adverse effects

Heparin-associated thrombocytopenia is described as usually appearing 3-15 days after therapy (median about 10 days), with more rapid onset after prior exposure. A pooled randomized comparison is reported as 15.6% thrombocytopenia with bovine-lung heparin versus 5.8% with porcine-gut heparin. Thrombotic complications are emphasized as more important than bleeding, and arterial thrombosis is reported at 0.4% (4/1,080) in one therapeutic porcine-heparin dataset.

The chapter frames HIT as immune-mediated and discusses platelet activation, cross-reactivity of LMWHs, the serotonin-release assay, and historical alternatives such as ancrod and the Organon heparinoid. Other adverse effects include skin necrosis, urticaria, hypersensitivity, and rare hypoaldosteronism with hyperkalemia; the latter is supported primarily by case reports.

Low-molecular-weight heparins

LMWHs are described as depolymerized heparin fragments with mean molecular weights around 4,000-5,000. Shorter chain distributions reduce thrombin inhibition relative to factor Xa inhibition, yielding anti-Xa:anti-IIa ratios of roughly 2:1 to 4:1 versus about 1:1 for standard heparin. Reduced binding to proteins and endothelial cells is used to explain better bioavailability, more predictable dosing, and half-lives approximately twofold to fourfold longer than standard heparin; renal clearance is emphasized.

Animal models are summarized as showing less bleeding for comparable antithrombotic effect. Clinical sections report favorable prevention results in general surgery, orthopedic surgery, stroke, and other high-risk patients, along with treatment studies suggesting similar or better efficacy and a trend toward greater safety versus standard heparin. The authors also explicitly identify unanswered questions about pregnancy, osteoporosis, postoperative timing, thrombocytopenia, HIT cross-reactivity, and whether selected venous-thrombosis patients could be treated at home.

Main strengths

  • Integrates structure, pharmacokinetics, monitoring, dosing, efficacy, and safety rather than treating them as isolated topics.
  • Explains why heparin response is variable: chain-length heterogeneity, protein/cell binding, saturable clearance, route-dependent bioavailability, and assay variability.
  • Preserves useful quantitative examples and distinguishes evidence levels for several clinical claims.
  • Includes detailed discussion of osteoporosis, thrombocytopenia/thrombosis, and LMWH pharmacologic differences.

Main limitations

  • Narrative rather than systematic review methodology; no reproducible search/selection or formal risk-of-bias process is reported.
  • Evidence is heterogeneous and includes subgroup analyses, animal experiments, descriptive series, case reports, and small trials.
  • Some therapeutic targets and management recommendations are explicitly based on historical calibration systems or indirect evidence.
  • The article predates later evidence and practice changes; current applicability was not verified externally in this run.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Preserve conceptually

Heparin's response variability is mechanistically tied to nonspecific binding and nonlinear clearance; monitoring is assay-dependent rather than purely drug-dose dependent.

Useful for protocol provenance

The chapter documents historical reasoning behind loading doses, six-hour APTT checks, local APTT calibration, and high-dose subcutaneous strategies.

Safety architecture

Separate bleeding risk from immune thrombocytopenia/thrombosis, skeletal toxicity with prolonged exposure, and rare endocrine/hypersensitivity effects.

Do not transplant directly

Do not copy the historical APTT target, dosing nomograms, platelet-monitoring threshold, ancrod regimen, or HIT treatment approach into current CDS without external verification.
