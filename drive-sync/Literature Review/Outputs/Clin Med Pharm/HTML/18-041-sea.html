One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper is useful as an early clinical signal for CBD in anxiety and as a compact example of the limits of uncontrolled chart-review evidence. It should not drive treatment or dosing decisions.

The authors reviewed records from adults treated with CBD for anxiety or sleep complaints while continuing usual psychiatric care. Of 103 consecutively treated adults, 72 met the final analysis criteria. Most received 25 mg/day of oral CBD, although some received 50 or 75 mg/day and one patient was titrated to 175 mg/day. Anxiety and sleep were measured monthly with the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale and Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index. The analysis was descriptive. There was no untreated or placebo comparator.

The strongest signal was in anxiety. At month 1, 57 of 72 patients, 79.2%, had improved anxiety and 48 of 72, 66.7%, had improved sleep. At month 2, among the 41 patients still followed, 32, 78.1%, had improved anxiety compared with the prior month and 23, 56.1%, had improved sleep. Mean HAM-A scores fell and then stayed lower, while PSQI scores improved less consistently. The results are hard to attribute to CBD because patients also received psychiatric medications and other care, expectations around cannabis may have affected reporting, and follow-up dropped from 72 patients at month 1 to 27 at month 3.

Reported tolerability was generally favorable, but safety ascertainment relied on spontaneous reports. Two patients stopped CBD within the first week because of fatigue, three reported transient sedation, one reported dry eyes, and one developed sexually inappropriate behavior that resolved when CBD was stopped and returned after rechallenge. The product was supplied by CV Sciences, which the authors stated had no role in collection, interpretation, writing, or submission.

The paper supports a hypothesis, not an efficacy claim. The observed anxiety improvement is compatible with a CBD effect, but it is also compatible with regression to the mean, concurrent care, expectancy effects, selection, and attrition.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames CBD as a nonintoxicating cannabinoid with preliminary neuropsychiatric evidence and states that psychiatric clinical studies were sparse. The stated objective was to determine whether CBD helped sleep and/or anxiety in a clinical population.

The abstract reports a final sample of 72 adults. Anxiety scores improved in 79.2% at the first month and remained lower over the study, while sleep improved in 66.7% at month 1 but fluctuated. The authors conclude that CBD may benefit anxiety-related disorders and call for controlled studies.

Introduction

The introduction reviews cannabis, CBD, historical use, proposed mechanisms, and earlier animal and human studies. Those cited studies are background evidence and were not treated as primary results from this publication.

The authors position the case series as a response to the lack of formal psychiatric trials. They name anxiety, sleep, tolerability, safety, and patient acceptance as targets of the chart review.

Methods

The investigators retrospectively reviewed adult psychiatric outpatients treated with CBD as an adjunct to usual care. Eligible patients had a clinician-diagnosed anxiety or sleep disorder, consented to CBD treatment, and had at least one month of treatment with follow-up. Primary schizophrenia, posttraumatic stress disorder, and agitated depression were exclusion diagnoses. Comorbid illness and concurrent psychiatric medications were otherwise allowed.

Most patients received CBD 25 mg/day in capsules. Morning dosing was used when anxiety predominated, evening dosing when sleep complaints predominated, and a minority received higher doses up to 175 mg/day. HAM-A and PSQI were recorded monthly. Side effects were captured from spontaneous self-report in the chart. The Western Institutional Review Board approved the review. Analysis used descriptive statistics and visual plots only.

Results

The final cohort included 47 patients with primary anxiety concerns and 25 with primary sleep concerns. The anxiety group had a mean age of 34 years, range 18 to 70, and 28 of 47 were men. The sleep group had a mean age of 36.5 years, range 18 to 72, and 16 of 25 were women. Retention fell from 72 patients through month 1 to 41 at month 2 and 27 at month 3.

At month 1, 57 of 72 patients improved in anxiety and 48 of 72 improved in sleep. At month 2, 32 of 41 improved in anxiety and 23 of 41 improved in sleep compared with the preceding month. Anxiety scores showed the more stable pattern. Reported adverse effects were mainly fatigue or sedation, with one notable behavioral event on dechallenge and rechallenge. Four patients declined CBD for religious or ethical concerns related to cannabis.

Discussion and study limitations

The authors interpret the anxiety trajectory as more convincing than the sleep trajectory and note that the doses used were below the 300 to 600 mg/day range reported in some earlier literature. Their own dose rationale was clinical experience plus cost, not a randomized dose-finding comparison.

The paper directly acknowledges the central threats to inference: open-label treatment, no comparison group, concurrent psychiatric medications and counseling, possible expectancy effects, a younger-than-usual clinic sample, and substantial attrition with largely unknown reasons. These limits prevent causal attribution to CBD.

Legality of cannabidiol

The article includes a 2018 discussion of state and federal CBD law. That section is historically informative but time-sensitive. This appraisal does not update or endorse those legal statements.

Conclusion, disclosures, and acknowledgments

The authors call for formal efficacy and dose-finding studies and state that CBD appears more promising for anxiety than sleep in this series. They also state that randomized controlled trials are needed for definitive clinical guidance.

CV Sciences supplied CBD products and was reported to have no role in study conduct, interpretation, writing, or submission. Scott Shannon disclosed compensated principal-investigator work in an MDMA-assisted psychotherapy study and professional books on integrative mental health; the other authors reported no conflicts.

Clinical appraisal

Population
72 adult psychiatric outpatients with primary anxiety or poor-sleep concerns
Intervention
Oral CBD added to usual care, usually 25 mg/day, range in practice up to 175 mg/day
Comparator
None
Outcomes
Monthly HAM-A, PSQI, spontaneous tolerability reports, treatment acceptance

Validity

Internal validity is weak for efficacy. There was no randomization, blinding, placebo control, prespecified inferential model, or control for concomitant therapies. The sample was self-selected into a clinic already using CBD, and the article acknowledges expectancy effects. Attrition was large by month 3. The use of validated scales is a strength for measurement, but spontaneous adverse-event reporting is less complete than systematic safety collection.

Practice translation

No treatment recommendation or dose can be justified from this design. The paper is more useful for generating questions about anxiety response, dose ranges used in routine care, tolerability signals, and trial design. Any current clinical decision also needs newer efficacy, safety, interaction, product-quality, and regulatory evidence that this 2019 article does not provide.

Bottom line

Use now: no, not as efficacy or dosing evidence. Use it as historical, hypothesis-generating observational evidence and as a reminder that apparent symptom improvement in uncontrolled mental-health care can be difficult to separate from concurrent treatment, expectancy, and attrition.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance
7/10

Directly addresses CBD use for anxiety and sleep in routine psychiatric care. Limiting factor: it cannot answer whether CBD caused the observed changes. A comparative trial with current products would raise the score.

Novelty
6/10

At publication, the low-dose outpatient case series added clinical observations in an area with sparse psychiatric data. Limiting factor: novelty comes from setting and observation rather than a new causal test. Dose-response or mechanistic data would raise the score.

Method strength
3/10

Validated symptom scales and a defined sample are positives. The uncontrolled retrospective design, concurrent care, descriptive analysis, and attrition dominate the rating. A randomized blinded comparator and prespecified analysis would raise it sharply.

Evidence strength
3/10

The anxiety signal is consistent within the observed series, but there are no causal estimates, confidence intervals, or controls. The source itself cautions against firm conclusions. Replicated controlled trials with patient-centered outcomes would raise the score.

External validity
4/10

The study reflects real outpatient care, but it comes from one integrative clinic with a younger, selected population and nonstandardized dosing. Multisite recruitment and clearer treatment standardization would raise the score.

Implementation value
3/10

The paper describes dose timing, symptom measures, and observed tolerability, which can inform research planning. It is not sufficient for a clinical protocol. Controlled efficacy, interaction, product-quality, and monitoring evidence would be needed.

Practice and research takeaways

  • The main study signal is anxiety improvement, not durable sleep improvement.
  • Do not interpret the before-and-after score changes as a CBD treatment effect. The design cannot separate CBD from concurrent care, expectancy, regression to the mean, or selection.
  • The dose range is descriptive clinical practice, not dose-finding evidence.
  • The dechallenge and rechallenge behavioral event deserves attention as a case-level safety signal, but the study's spontaneous reporting method cannot estimate adverse-event incidence reliably.
  • A useful follow-up trial would standardize the CBD product and dose, use a randomized blinded comparator, prespecify HAM-A and sleep outcomes, capture adverse events systematically, and minimize loss to follow-up.
