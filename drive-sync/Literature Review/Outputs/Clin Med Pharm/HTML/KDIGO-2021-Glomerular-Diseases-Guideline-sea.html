Executive synthesis

Verdict: foundational for 2021 glomerular-disease practice and unusually broad in implementation detail, but not safe to treat as a fully current 2026 guideline packet because the supplied source explicitly supersedes its AAV and lupus-nephritis chapters.

The guideline is best read as a disease-spanning clinical architecture: establish the pathologic diagnosis when appropriate, characterize severity and progression risk, optimize supportive care, then add disease-specific immunosuppression only when the expected benefit justifies toxicity. Across IgAN, MN, pediatric nephrotic syndrome, adult MCD, FSGS, infection-related GN, MPGN-pattern disease, and anti-GBM disease, the document repeatedly links treatment choice to phenotype, kidney function, proteinuria, immunologic markers, treatment response, relapse pattern, and safety monitoring rather than to diagnosis alone.

The evidence supplement makes the document’s main limitation visible. Many clinically decisive recommendations sit on low or very-low certainty evidence, small trials, surrogate outcomes, or observational series. KDIGO compensates by separating graded Recommendations from ungraded Practice Points and by using algorithms, risk-stratification figures, and explicit monitoring rules. That structure is a strength for implementation, but it means downstream CDS or knowledge-base ingestion must preserve recommendation grade, Practice Point status, source version, and chapter-specific currency.

Version and supersession boundary

Do not operationalize supplied Chapters 9 or 10 as current guidance. The PDF itself marks Chapter 9 (AAV) and Chapter 10 (lupus nephritis) “OUTDATED CHAPTER. PLEASE SEE UPDATE” and directs readers to KDIGO’s 2024 updates. The replacement 2024 chapter text is not contained in either supplied PDF.

The file name KDIGO-2021-Glomerular-Diseases-Guideline_English_2024-Chapter-Updates.pdf can therefore be misleading if interpreted as a consolidated 2024 guideline. It is a 2021 guideline artifact carrying deprecation notices. This ATOM run retains two explicit deprecation atoms, inventories the legacy figures/tables for completeness, but intentionally does not emit the legacy Chapter 9/10 formal statements as active clinical-guidance atoms.

Development methods and evidence architecture

KDIGO used an explicit evidence-review process, systematic reviews, and GRADE. Formal recommendations use Level 1/2 strength and A–D evidence certainty; Practice Points are ungraded and are used where systematic review was absent or insufficient for a graded recommendation. The guideline’s notice dates the searches to October 2018 with supplemental evidence through September 2019 and an update in June 2020.

The data supplement documents search strategies, guideline-development and systematic-review standards, cited Summary-of-Findings tables, and additional evidence profiles. For this run, supplement findings remain secondary-source evidence. No table row is represented as if KDIGO itself enrolled the underlying study participants.

Chapter 1: General principles for the management of glomerular disease

Formal recommendations
0
Practice Points
24

General management is organized around kidney-biopsy interpretation, standardized assessment of kidney function and proteinuria, edema control, blood-pressure/proteinuria management, cardiovascular risk reduction, thrombosis prevention, immunosuppression safety, vaccination/infection prophylaxis, reproductive planning, and coordinated pregnancy care. The chapter repeatedly emphasizes that supportive management and complication prevention are common infrastructure across glomerular diagnoses, while disease-specific chapters determine when immunosuppression is justified.

This chapter is dominated by ungraded Practice Points rather than formal recommendations, reflecting a mixture of physiologic rationale, extrapolation, observational evidence, and consensus. For implementation, the useful unit is not a single rule but a bundle: confirm pathology when needed, quantify proteinuria and kidney function consistently, optimize RAS-based supportive care when appropriate, actively manage edema/thrombotic/infectious risks, and attach explicit monitoring to immunosuppressive therapy.

Chapter 2: Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) / immunoglobulin A vasculitis (IgAV)

Formal recommendations
4
Practice Points
21

IgAN requires kidney biopsy for diagnosis; the Oxford MEST-C score and International IgAN Prediction Tool support risk characterization, but the guideline does not endorse using pathology or the prediction tool to forecast response to a particular therapy. Initial treatment centers on optimized supportive care. ACEi or ARB therapy is recommended when proteinuria exceeds 0.5 g/day, irrespective of hypertension. High risk is framed as persistent proteinuria above roughly 0.75–1 g/day after at least 90 days of optimized supportive care. A 6-month glucocorticoid course is only suggested for patients who remain high risk, with explicit toxicity discussion, especially at eGFR below 50 ml/min/1.73 m².

The chapter draws a sharp line between routine IgAN and variant presentations such as MCD-like disease, AKI, or rapidly progressive GN. In IgAV, glucocorticoids are specifically not recommended solely to prevent nephritis in isolated extrarenal disease. The source’s own evidence tables show why caution is central: signals for kidney benefit coexist with substantial uncertainty and toxicity.

Chapter 3: Membranous nephropathy

Formal recommendations
1
Practice Points
12

Membranous nephropathy management is risk-based. Anti-PLA2R serology is integrated into diagnosis and longitudinal immunologic monitoring, while all patients with proteinuria receive supportive care. Immunosuppression is reserved for patients at risk of progressive kidney injury or with serious nephrotic complications. For patients with at least one progression-risk factor, the guideline recommends rituximab, cyclophosphamide with alternate-month glucocorticoids for 6 months, or CNI-based therapy for at least 6 months, with selection linked to estimated risk.

The chapter also gives algorithms for relapse, resistant disease, transplant recurrence, pediatric MN, and anticoagulation. A major implementation strength is the distinction between immunologic response and proteinuria response: anti-PLA2R trajectories can precede clinical remission, and persistent proteinuria alone is not automatically equivalent to immunologic treatment resistance.

Chapter 4: Nephrotic syndrome in children

Formal recommendations
4
Practice Points
10

In childhood nephrotic syndrome, initial prognosis is driven primarily by glucocorticoid response, so kidney biopsy is usually deferred unless the course is atypical or steroid-resistant. Initial oral glucocorticoid therapy is recommended for either 8 or 12 weeks. Relapse guidance specifies steroid regimens, and children with serious glucocorticoid toxicity or steroid dependence should receive steroid-sparing therapy. Cyclosporine or tacrolimus is recommended as initial second-line therapy for steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome.

Agent selection among cyclophosphamide, levamisole, MMF, rituximab, and CNIs is individualized according to relapse pattern, steroid dependence, toxicity, adherence, resources, and patient/family preferences. The supplement demonstrates that many pediatric comparisons rely on low- or moderate-certainty evidence and surrogate/relapse outcomes rather than long-term kidney failure endpoints.

Chapter 5: Minimal change disease (MCD) in adults

Formal recommendations
2
Practice Points
9

Adult minimal change disease remains a biopsy-defined nephrotic syndrome in which high-dose oral glucocorticoids are recommended for the initial episode. The guideline provides definitions for remission, relapse, frequent relapse, steroid dependence, and steroid resistance and uses these states to direct therapy. For frequently relapsing or steroid-dependent MCD, cyclophosphamide, rituximab, CNIs, or mycophenolic acid analogs are recommended rather than prednisone alone or no treatment.

The evidence base is small relative to the confidence with which clinicians must act. The supplement repeatedly shows very small trials, imprecision, and low or very-low certainty for several MCD comparisons; the chapter therefore depends heavily on accumulated clinical experience and state-based treatment logic.

Chapter 6: Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) in adults

Formal recommendations
2
Practice Points
11

FSGS is treated as a lesion requiring etiologic classification rather than as a single disease. The guideline distinguishes primary FSGS from genetic, secondary, and undetermined-cause forms and stresses evaluation for secondary causes and selective use of genetic testing. Immunosuppression is directed at primary FSGS with nephrotic syndrome; high-dose oral glucocorticoids are the recommended first-line immunosuppressive treatment.

For steroid-resistant primary FSGS, cyclosporine or tacrolimus for at least 6 months is recommended rather than continued glucocorticoid monotherapy or no treatment. The evidence tables are sparse and often low/very-low certainty, making the diagnostic boundary—primary versus secondary/genetic—particularly important because misclassification can expose patients to toxic therapy without a plausible benefit.

Chapter 7: Infection-related glomerulonephritis

Formal recommendations
2
Practice Points
19

Infection-related GN is managed by identifying and treating the underlying infection while providing kidney supportive care. The chapter separates classic bacterial infection-related GN from viral, HIV-associated, schistosomal, and malarial disease. For replicative HBV infection with GN, nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy is recommended according to standard HBV guidance; for HIV with CKD, ART is recommended regardless of CD4 count, especially in biopsy-proven HIV-associated nephropathy, with dosing adjusted to kidney function.

The chapter’s evidence base is heterogeneous because the kidney lesion is secondary to diverse infections and because high-quality kidney-specific RCTs are uncommon. The strongest implementation principle is etiologic: control the infection and avoid reflexively treating an infection-associated immune lesion as if it were a primary autoimmune GN.

Chapter 8: Immunoglobulin- and complement-mediated glomerular diseases with an MPGN pattern of injury

Formal recommendations
0
Practice Points
15

The MPGN chapter reframes membranoproliferative GN as a pattern of injury rather than a final etiologic diagnosis. Evaluation separates immunoglobulin-dominant disease from complement-dominant disease and prompts work-up for infection, autoimmune disease, monoclonal gammopathy, and alternative-complement-pathway abnormalities. The chapter includes C3 glomerulopathy and proliferative GN with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits and emphasizes cause-directed treatment where a driver is identified.

No formal graded recommendations appear in the 2021 summary for this chapter; guidance is carried by Practice Points and algorithms. That is an accurate signal of evidence maturity: the supplement contains small observational series and limited trial evidence, so diagnostic precision and referral/expert interpretation often matter more than a universally applicable immunosuppressive regimen.

Chapter 11: Anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) antibody glomerulonephritis

Formal recommendations
1
Practice Points
8

Anti-GBM disease is treated as a nephrologic emergency. Diagnosis should be pursued without delay in suspected rapidly progressive GN, and treatment can begin while confirmation is pending. The guideline recommends cyclophosphamide plus glucocorticoids and plasma exchange for most patients, except those presenting dialysis-dependent with 100% crescents or more than 50% global glomerulosclerosis in an adequate biopsy and no pulmonary hemorrhage.

Plasma exchange is continued until anti-GBM antibodies are no longer detectable; cyclophosphamide is generally limited to 2–3 months and glucocorticoids to about 6 months. Routine maintenance is unnecessary for isolated anti-GBM disease, while ANCA/anti-GBM double-positive disease follows AAV-style maintenance logic. Transplantation should wait until anti-GBM antibodies have remained undetectable for at least 6 months. The direct randomized evidence supporting plasma exchange is extremely small and very uncertain.

2Practice Point2.4.2—S47IgAN with AKI: • AKI can occur in patients with IgAN in the context of severe visible hematuria, commonly in association with an upper respiratory tract infection. Immediate management of AKI with visible hematuria should focus on supportive care for AKI. • IgAN may also present with AKI either de novo or during its natural history due to an RPGN with extensive crescent formation, commonly in the absence of visible hematuria. 2Practice Point2.4.5—S48IgAN in children: General considerations • For the purposes of this practice point, children are defined as those aged <18 years. It is acknowledged that post-pubertal children in some respects may have a similar course and response to treatment as adults with IgAN, but there are insufficient data currently to recommend that they be managed as adults with IgAN. • Visible hematuria is more frequent in children than in adults, and this may account for earlier diagnosis in children.126 • Children generally have higher eGFR, lower urine protein excretion, and more hematuria than adults at diagnosis.127 Kidney biopsy in children • A kidney biopsy is usually performed at presentation of symptoms (hematuria, proteinuria, normal C3) in order to confirm the diagnosis (and rule out other diagnoses) and assess the degree of inflammation/presence of necrosis. • Inflammation, mesangial, and endocapillary hypercellularity tend to be more prevalent in kidney biopsies of IgAN in children than in those of adults.128–131 Treatment • There is strong evidence suggesting a benefit of RAS blockade in children.132 All children with IgAN and proteinuria >200 mg/d or PCR >200 mg/g (>0.2 g/g [20 mg/mmol]) should receive ACEi or ARB blockade, advice on a low-sodium diet, and optimal lifestyle and blood pressure control (systolic blood pressure [SBP] <90th percentile for age, sex, and height). • It is widely acknowledged that treatment of IgAN with immunosuppression differs between adults and children, and that in children, the use of immunosuppressants is more widespread, particularly the use of glucocorticoids. However, RCTs and specific expert consensus-driven indications are lacking. • Evidence derived mostly from retrospective studies suggests that treatment with glucocorticoids (plus second-line immunosuppression) leads to improved kidney survival.126,133 • In children with proteinuria >1 g/d or PCR >1 g/g (100 mg/mmol) and/or mesangial hypercellularity, most pediatric nephrologists will treat with glucocorticoids in addition to RAS blockade from time of diagnosis. Duration of treatment is not established, but usually 4 weeks of 1–2 mg/kg/d of oral prednisolone (or equivalent) followed by alternate-day tapering over 4-6 months is employed. Regimens including intravenous methylprednisolone are also used.127,128,130,134 • Evidence for the use of non-glucocorticoid immunosuppressants in addition to glucocorticoids is scarce, but this approach may be considered in more severe cases. • As for adults, IgAN with MCD may be found, and it should be treated as steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (SSNS; Chapter 4). • As in adults, children with rapidly progressive IgAN have a poor outcome, and despite limited evidence, this subgroup should be offered treatment with glucocorticoids (usually as methylprednisolone pulses) and cyclophosphamide.128,130,135 Follow-up • Aim for proteinuria ≤200 mg/d (≤400 mg/1.73 m2 /d) or PCR ≤200 mg/g (≤0.2 g/g [≤20 mg/mmol]). • Aim for blood pressure at SBP <90th percentile for age, sex, and height. • Continue to follow patients even after complete remission, as they can relapse even after many years.136

2Practice Point2.6.1—S49Considerations for the prognostication of IgAV: • Retrospective data from a limited number of small registries have identified uncontrolled hypertension and the amount of proteinuria at presentation, and hypertension and mean proteinuria during follow-up, as predictors of a poor kidney outcome in adults with IgAV.142–144 • The Oxford Classification has not been validated for IgAV. • The International IgAN Prediction Tool88 is not designed for prognostication in IgAV.

2Practice Point2.7.1.1—S49Considerations for the treatment of all patients with IgAV-associated nephritis (IgAVN) who do not have an RPGN: • Assess cardiovascular risk and commence appropriate interventions as necessary. • Give lifestyle advice, including information on smoking cessation, weight control, and exercise, as appropriate. • No specific dietary intervention has been shown to alter outcomes in IgAVN. • Treat to nationally agreed-upon blood pressure targets. KDIGO suggests treating to an SBP target of <120 mm Hg measured using standardized office blood pressure measurement (Figure 8). • Treat with maximally tolerated dose of ACEi or ARB if proteinuria >0.5 g/d. • Offer participation in a clinical trial if one is available.
2Practice Point2.7.2.1—S49Considerations for the treatment of patients with IgAVN who are at high risk of progressive CKD despite maximal supportive care: • There is insufficient evidence to support the use of the Oxford Classification MEST-C score in determining whether immunosuppression should be commenced in patients with IgAVN. • The presence of crescents in the kidney biopsy is not in itself an automatic indication for commencement of immunosuppression. • In all patients in whom immunosuppression is being considered, a detailed discussion of the risks and benefits of each drug should be undertaken with the patient with a recognition that adverse treatment effects are more likely in patients with an eGFR <50 ml/min per 1.73 m2. • In those patients who wish to try immunosuppressive therapy, treatment with glucocorticoids is as described above for IgAN.
2Practice Point2.8.1—S49IgAV with RPGN: • The potential risks and benefits of immunosuppression should be evaluated at the individual patient level and discussed with the patient. • Patients agreeing to treatment should be treated in accordance with the guidelines for AAV (Chapter 9). • IgAV with RPGN as well as other IgAVN may be associated with significant extrarenal involvement (pulmonary, gastrointestinal, and skin), which may dictate alternative immunosuppressive strategies. • There are insufficient data to determine the efficacy of plasma exchange in IgAVN with RPGN. However, uncontrolled case series describe the potential role for the addition of plasma exchange to glucocorticoid therapy to accelerate recovery in patients with life- or organ-threatening extrarenal complications of IgAV.151 Clinicians are referred to the guidelines of the American Society for Apheresis regarding recommendations regarding plasma exchange for IgAV.152
2Practice Point2.8.1.1—S50For the purposes of this practice point, children are defined as those aged <18 years. It is acknowledged that post-pubertal children in some respects may have a similar course and response to treatment as adults with IgAN, but there are insufficient data currently to recommend that they be managed as adults with IgAN. Indications for management of IgAVN in children have recently been published as the result of a European consortium initiative.140 Briefly: • There are no data supporting the use of glucocorticoids to prevent nephritis in children with IgAV but mild or absent evidence of kidney involvement.153,154 • Children >10 years of age more often present with non-nephrotic-range proteinuria and impaired kidney function, and they may suffer more chronic histologic lesions with delay in biopsy and delay in treatment longer than 30 days.155 • The majority of children who will develop nephritis will do so within 3 months of presentation. Urinary monitoring is necessary for ≥6 months and optimally 12 months from initial presentation of systemic disease. • Children with IgAVN and persistent proteinuria for >3 months should be treated with an ACEi or ARB. A pediatric nephrologist should be consulted. • A kidney biopsy should be performed in children with nephrotic-range proteinuria, impaired GFR, or persistent moderate (>1 g/d) proteinuria. • Oral prednisone/prednisolone or pulsed intravenous methylprednisolone should be used in children with mild or moderate IgAVN. • Children with IgAVN with nephrotic syndrome and/or rapidly deteriorating kidney function are treated in the same way as those with rapidly progressive IgAN.

Evaluation and appraisal

What the source establishes well

  • A coherent diagnostic-to-treatment framework across a wide spectrum of glomerular diseases, with strong attention to supportive care, treatment toxicity, relapse/resistance, and special situations.
  • Transparent separation of graded recommendations from ungraded Practice Points and explicit GRADE notation on the former.
  • High implementation density: risk algorithms, dosing regimens, monitoring pathways, biopsy/serology strategies, pregnancy considerations, and complication-management figures.
  • A large evidence supplement that exposes certainty, imprecision, risk of bias, and missing outcomes rather than hiding evidence gaps.

Major limitations

  • Evidence currency is primarily through June 2020. Several disease areas have evolved materially since then, and the supplied PDF explicitly acknowledges this for AAV and lupus nephritis.
  • Many treatment questions depend on small RCTs, low/very-low certainty evidence, surrogate endpoints, or observational studies.
  • Practice Points can be clinically useful but should not be flattened into the same evidentiary class as graded recommendations.
  • The data supplement is secondary synthesis; exact primary-study eligibility, protocol, and outcome adjudication require reading the cited publications.

Best use

Use the packet as a structured 2021 foundation for glomerular-disease diagnosis, supportive care, treatment logic, and evidence provenance; use it to seed version-aware CDS/knowledge representations only when Recommendation grade, Practice Point status, chapter, and date are retained.

Do not use for

Do not use the supplied AAV or lupus-nephritis chapters as current guidance. Do not treat supplement effect estimates as primary-study data, and do not assume the supplied “2024 Chapter Updates” filename means the replacement 2024 chapter text is present.
