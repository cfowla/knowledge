Scope warning. This paper evaluates a 1991-1994 era, institution-specific heparin/APTT workflow. It is useful for historical implementation evidence and protocol design concepts; it does not by itself validate the exact dose adjustments or APTT target for present-day use.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: A standardized nurse-applied heparin nomogram in two community hospitals markedly shortened the time to therapeutic APTT and reduced subtherapeutic APTT results, but did not shorten heparin duration or hospital stay and increased the proportion of supratherapeutic APTT results.

Why the paper matters

The study addresses a practical medication-use problem: large physician-to-physician variation in IV heparin dosing and delays between laboratory reporting and dose changes. The protocol moved dose-adjustment authority into an explicit nomogram that nurses could execute after APTT results, reducing dependence on repeated physician contact.

The laboratory effect was large. Mean time to first therapeutic APTT fell from 48.8 to 17.9 hours; 64% versus 36% reached therapeutic APTT by 24 hours (OR 4.0, 95% CI 2.0-8.3); and subtherapeutic APTTs fell from 56% to 28%. However, postnomogram APTTs were also more often above the therapeutic window, and the study was underpowered for rare bleeding or mortality outcomes.

What it did not show

The predefined primary endpoint, length of hospital stay, was unchanged (10.9 vs 11.3 days; P=.60). Heparin duration was also unchanged (6.7 vs 7.1 days; P=.27). APTT tests increased while PT tests decreased, leaving overall coagulation testing essentially unchanged. Thus the paper supports protocol-driven laboratory control and workflow efficiency more strongly than patient-centered outcome or cost benefit.

Important internal discrepancy: the Results prose reports mean time to first therapeutic APTT as 17.8 hours postnomogram, whereas Table 4 and the abstract report 17.9 hours. This appraisal uses 17.9 hours for the table-derived quantitative summary and retains the discrepancy as a source limitation.

Methods

Study population and design

Patients with a primary diagnosis of DVT or PE at either hospital were identified from computerized discharge records. The postnomogram protocol was instituted in April 1993, but April through June was excluded to allow familiarization. Historical controls came from the period before April 1993; the postnomogram group came from after June 1993. The design was retrospective and nonrandomized.

Of 326 records identified, 163 were included. Exclusions included DVT/PE developing during hospitalization for another reason, DVT/PE not being the primary reason for admission, negative subsequent testing, transfer before completion/follow-up, no heparin treatment, and missing information. Groups were broadly similar in age, sex, DVT/PE distribution, baseline APTT/PT, smoking status and listed comorbidities.

Intervention and workflow

All nomogram-treated patients were intended to receive a 5000-IU bolus and a continuous infusion using 20,000 U in 500 mL D5W. Initial infusion rate was selected by physician-assessed bleeding risk: 1680 U/h for low risk and 1280 U/h for high risk. APTT was measured 6 hours after the bolus, then nurses adjusted the infusion according to Table 1. Nomogram use was not mandatory.

Warfarin prescribing remained outside the protocol and was handled by the treating physician. This separation is central to interpretation of the negative length-of-stay result: the intervention improved heparin control but did not standardize transition, discharge readiness or warfarin timing.

Outcomes, reliability and analysis

Hospital length of stay was predefined as the primary outcome. Secondary endpoints included time to therapeutic APTT and frequency of out-of-range APTT results. Complications and survival were reported descriptively, but the authors explicitly stated that the sample size was inadequate for meaningful assessment of these outcomes.

A random sample of 25 charts underwent duplicate review with intraclass correlation coefficients. The study used chi-square tests, unpaired two-tailed t tests, odds ratios with 95% CIs, and Kaplan-Meier/Mantel-Haenszel analysis for time to therapeutic APTT. Because of multiple statistical tests, P<.01 was used as the significance threshold. Sample-size planning targeted roughly 75-80 patients per group to detect a 2-day LOS difference.

Results

Anticoagulation control

Protocol implementation produced a marked improvement in the speed of achieving therapeutic APTT. Mean time to first therapeutic APTT fell from 48.8 to 17.9 hours, and the first dose adjustment occurred earlier (31.3 vs 17.9 hours). At 24 hours, 64% of postnomogram patients versus 36% of controls were therapeutic, corresponding to a reported OR of 4.0 (95% CI 2.0-8.3).

The pattern was not simply a shift from low APTTs into the target range. Therapeutic-window results increased from 29% to 37% and subtherapeutic results fell from 56% to 28%, but high APTT categories also became more frequent. The authors reported OR 3.2 (95% CI 2.7-3.8) for an underanticoagulated result in prenomogram patients and OR 3.0 (95% CI 2.4-3.7) for an overanticoagulated result in postnomogram patients.

Clinical and resource outcomes

Hospital length of stay did not improve (11.3 vs 10.9 days; P=.60), nor did duration of heparin therapy (7.1 vs 6.7 days; P=.27). The intervention therefore failed on the predefined primary endpoint despite clear changes in laboratory control.

APTT testing increased from a mean of 12.7 to 15.6 tests per patient, while PT testing fell from 13.0 to 10.7 tests per patient; the authors concluded overall coagulation testing was equivalent and did not demonstrate laboratory cost savings. No increase in adverse reactions was detected, but the sample was too small to rule out differences in uncommon bleeding or mortality outcomes.

Data validity

Duplicate chart review showed ICCs of 0.75 for hospital stay, 0.99 for number of APTT tests, 0.95 for heparin duration, 0.94 for time to therapeutic APTT and 0.99 for the number of times outside the therapeutic range. This supports reliability of key extracted chart variables, though it does not address confounding inherent in the historical-control design.

Discussion, strengths and limitations

Authors' interpretation

The authors attributed much of the improvement to higher initial infusion rates and faster protocolized responses to APTT results. They also emphasized operational advantages: fewer delays waiting for physician orders, less information transfer and greater nursing efficiency. They regarded the protocol as successful despite the absence of LOS or laboratory-cost savings.

They proposed that LOS was unlikely to change because the nomogram did not include discharge criteria or a structured warfarin-transition strategy. A revised protocol was said to incorporate earlier warfarin initiation and discharge considerations.

Strengths: prespecified primary endpoint; explicit nomogram; two community hospitals; detailed dosing and testing data; reliability assessment; time-to-event analysis; power calculation for LOS; and transparent discussion of the null primary outcome.
Major limitations: retrospective historical controls; possible secular changes or cointerventions; nonmandatory nomogram adherence; surrogate laboratory endpoints; sample too small for uncommon bleeding/death outcomes; and historical assay/dosing context that constrains present-day generalizability.

Claim vs appraisal

Source-supported claim: in these hospitals, introduction of the nomogram was associated with faster and more consistent achievement of the study's therapeutic APTT window. Appraisal: the magnitude and internal consistency of the laboratory signal make a real workflow effect plausible, but the historical-control design prevents strong causal attribution to the nomogram alone.

Source-supported claim: length of stay was unchanged. Appraisal: this negative primary outcome is important and should prevent overstating the paper as evidence that a heparin nomogram improves broad clinical efficiency or patient outcomes.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High-value historical implementation evidence for a heparin deep dive, especially for protocol architecture, nursing autonomy and APTT-response workflow. It is not strong enough to serve as stand-alone contemporary dosing evidence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Standardization effectProtocolized dosing can reduce clinician-to-clinician variation and time lost waiting for individualized dose-change orders.
Nursing workflowThe paper demonstrates an early form of delegated algorithmic medication management: APTT result → table rule → rate change → scheduled repeat test → physician escalation only at predefined extremes.
Measure both tailsReducing subtherapeutic results can simultaneously increase supratherapeutic results; protocol evaluation should track the full distribution, not only time-to-first target.
Do not expect one protocol to fix LOSA heparin nomogram did not standardize warfarin transition or discharge decisions, and LOS did not change. Downstream workflow needs its own explicit design.
Safety boundaryThe source cannot establish safety equivalence because it lacked power for uncommon bleeding/death outcomes.
Modernization requirementAny reuse should preserve the workflow concepts while revalidating drug dosing, laboratory targets, assay interpretation, escalation logic and transition criteria against current local standards.
