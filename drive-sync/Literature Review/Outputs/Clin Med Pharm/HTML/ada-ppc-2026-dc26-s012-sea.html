One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-utility complication-management chapter with directly implementable screening intervals, specialty-referral triggers, medication cautions, neuropathic-pain options, PAD evaluation thresholds, foot-risk surveillance frequencies, and DFU escalation criteria.

The chapter links chronic hyperglycemia and other modifiable risk factors to diabetic retinopathy and neuropathy, then operationalizes prevention through glycemic, blood-pressure, lipid, screening, and specialty-care recommendations. Retinopathy guidance is particularly actionable: type 1 diabetes begins routine dilated examination 5 years after onset; type 2 diabetes begins at diagnosis; normal examinations can permit 1–2-year intervals in selected people; pregnancy with preexisting diabetes requires preconception/first-trimester assessment and potentially trimester/postpartum follow-up. Retinal photography and FDA-authorized AI can expand screening but require referral pathways and do not replace indicated comprehensive eye examinations.

Treatment evidence is strongest for laser and anti-VEGF strategies in sight-threatening retinopathy/DME, while the chapter also highlights emerging uncertainty around GLP-1 receptor agonists and ocular outcomes. Neuropathy is framed as a diagnosis of exclusion with up to half of DPN potentially asymptomatic. The chapter prioritizes routine bedside sensory testing, symptomatic management, four nonopioid drug classes for painful DPN, and avoidance of opioids except rarely. Foot-care guidance combines annual comprehensive evaluation with risk-stratified follow-up, PAD assessment, podiatry/interprofessional referral, education, offloading, infection control, revascularization, and a defined 4-week/50% wound-response threshold for considering advanced therapy.

Diabetic retinopathy

Risk and screening

Diabetic retinopathy is presented as a neurovascular consequence of diabetes whose prevalence rises with duration and chronic hyperglycemia. Nephropathy, hypertension, and dyslipidemia add risk. The chapter emphasizes the durable microvascular benefit of early intensive glycemic management (“metabolic memory”) and advises individualized screening intervals. Rapid A1C reduction can initially worsen retinopathy, which matters when intensifying potent glucose-lowering therapy. GLP-1 RA ocular data remain mixed: trials and observational studies produce signals in both directions, so baseline retinopathy status and follow-up remain important.

Remote retinal photography and FDA-authorized AI are acceptable access-expanding strategies when they are embedded in a timely referral pathway. The chapter names AEYE-DS, EyeArt, and LumineticsCore. Unacceptable image quality or detected abnormality requires in-person follow-up. Pregnancy substantially changes risk in people with preexisting diabetes: pooled early-pregnancy prevalence was 52.3% for any retinopathy and 6.1% for PDR, with progression estimates ranging from 6.3 per 100 pregnancies for NPDR-to-PDR progression to 37.0 per 100 pregnancies for worsening established PDR.

Treatment

High-risk PDR remains an indication for panretinal photocoagulation, with the DRS reporting severe vision loss in 15.9% of untreated versus 6.4% of treated eyes. Intravitreal anti-VEGF can substitute for laser in selected PDR and is first line for most center-involved DME that impairs visual acuity. Anti-VEGF offers favorable visual/anatomic outcomes but imposes follow-up and treatment burden; missed follow-up can worsen outcomes. In NPDR, anti-VEGF reduces neovascularization/DME but has not improved 2-year vision outcomes, limiting routine use for that indication.

Persistent DME can be managed with focal/grid laser or intravitreal corticosteroid when anti-VEGF response is inadequate or anti-VEGF is unsuitable. Blood-pressure management reduces progression, but an SBP target below 120 mmHg did not add retinopathy benefit. Aspirin for cardioprotection is not contraindicated. Fenofibrate and statins have favorable retinopathy associations in selected settings; thiazolidinedione-DME evidence is conflicting. Vision rehabilitation is an explicit component of care because vision loss affects self-management, function, mental health, and independence.

Neuropathy

The chapter treats neuropathy as heterogeneous and emphasizes that diabetic neuropathy is a diagnosis of exclusion. Up to 50% of DPN can be asymptomatic, so annual structured screening matters. The recommended bedside approach spans small-fiber and large-fiber testing and protective-sensation assessment. Alternative causes—including alcohol/toxins, neurotoxic drugs, vitamin B12 deficiency, thyroid or kidney disease, malignancy, infections, inflammatory/inherited neuropathies, and vasculitis—must remain in the differential. Autonomic neuropathy can affect cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and sudomotor systems; CAN is clinically important because it can worsen cardiovascular risk and contribute to heart failure or sudden death.

No therapy reverses established diabetic nerve loss. Early glycemic management strongly prevents or delays DPN/CAN in type 1 diabetes and offers more modest slowing in type 2 diabetes. Weight, blood pressure, and lipids are addressed as modifiable contributors, but evidence for specific obesity/lipid treatments is uneven. For painful DPN, gabapentinoids, SNRIs, TCAs, and sodium-channel blockers are evidence-supported first choices, with combination therapy reasonable after inadequate monotherapy. Opioids, including tramadol/tapentadol, are discouraged except rarely because long-term benefit is unproven and harms are substantial. Capsaicin 8% patch is FDA approved; lidocaine patches have limited evidence.

Gastroparesis requires exclusion of medication effects and structural causes; 4-hour solid-meal gastric-emptying scintigraphy is the diagnostic gold standard. Management starts with dietary and medication review. Metoclopramide is the only FDA-approved drug, but evidence is weak and serious neurologic adverse effects limit prolonged use. Orthostatic hypotension management targets symptoms rather than normalization of blood pressure and starts with nonpharmacologic measures; midodrine and droxidopa are FDA-approved pharmacologic options.

Foot care

Foot care is built around early identification of ulcer/amputation risk. Annual comprehensive examination is the minimum, with every-visit inspection for sensory loss or previous ulcer/amputation. LOPS, PAD, deformity, callus, prior ulcer/amputation, smoking, retinopathy, nephropathy, and access-related social factors all matter. Monofilament testing should be paired with another neurologic test; it should not serve as the sole DPN diagnostic tool. For PAD, ankle-brachial indices can be misleading because of noncompressible vessels, while toe pressure is more reliable; toe systolic pressure below 30 mmHg signals severe ischemic concern and poor healing potential.

Education is necessary but not sufficient: evidence suggests knowledge improves more reliably than behavior, so repeated reinforcement is required. Moderate/high-risk feet warrant specialty surveillance. A warm, swollen, erythematous foot without an open ulcer should trigger urgent Charcot evaluation, non-weight-bearing, imaging, and specialty referral. Foot infection requires prompt culture/imaging and drainage when indicated; necrotizing infection and wet gangrene are surgical emergencies. The chapter's five standard DFU pillars are offloading, debridement, revascularization, infection management, and wound-appropriate dressings.

Advanced wound therapy is considered when a wound fails to shrink by at least 50% after 4 weeks of appropriate care once deformity and osteomyelitis have been addressed. Evidence quality is heterogeneous: trials are hard to blind, often exclude CKD/dialysis, and vary in wound characteristics. Hyperbaric oxygen has inconsistent trial support and is not advocated broadly for nonischemic/noninfected nonhealing DFU. Topical oxygen has stronger contemporary RCT/systematic-review support for 12-week healing. The chapter advises favoring modalities supported by robust randomized evidence or systematic reviews.

Recommendations 12.1–12.32

  1. 12.1 Grade A Implement strategies that help people with diabetes reach glycemic goals to reduce the risk or slow progression of diabetic retinopathy.
  2. 12.2 Grade A Implement strategies that help people with diabetes reach blood pressure and lipid goals to reduce the risk or slow progression of diabetic retinopathy.
  3. 12.3 Grade B Adults with type 1 diabetes should have an initial dilated comprehensive eye examination by an ophthalmologist or optometrist 5 years after diabetes onset.
  4. 12.4 Grade B People with type 2 diabetes should have an initial dilated comprehensive eye examination by an ophthalmologist or optometrist at diabetes diagnosis.
  5. 12.5 Grade B After one or more annual eye examinations show no retinopathy and glycemic indicators are within goal, screening every 1–2 years may be considered; any retinopathy warrants at least annual dilated retinal examination, with more frequent ophthalmologist examinations for progressing or sight-threatening disease.
  6. 12.6 Grade B Retinal photography with remote reading or FDA-approved artificial intelligence algorithms is an appropriate diabetic retinopathy screening strategy when the program provides a pathway for timely comprehensive eye-examination referral when indicated.
  7. 12.7 Grade B Counsel people of childbearing potential with preexisting type 1 or type 2 diabetes who are planning pregnancy or are pregnant about the risk of development or progression of diabetic retinopathy.
  8. 12.8 Grade B People with preexisting type 1 or type 2 diabetes should receive an eye examination before pregnancy and in the first trimester and may need monitoring every trimester and for 1 year postpartum according to retinopathy severity.
  9. 12.9 Grade A Promptly refer any diabetic macular edema, moderate or worse nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy, or any proliferative diabetic retinopathy to an ophthalmologist experienced in diabetic retinopathy management.
  10. 12.10 Grade A Panretinal laser photocoagulation is indicated to reduce vision-loss risk in high-risk proliferative diabetic retinopathy and in some cases of severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy.
  11. 12.11 Grade A Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections are a reasonable alternative to traditional panretinal laser photocoagulation for some people with proliferative diabetic retinopathy and reduce vision-loss risk.
  12. 12.12 Grade A Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections are first-line treatment for most eyes with center-involved diabetic macular edema that impairs visual acuity.
  13. 12.13 Grade A Macular focal/grid photocoagulation and intravitreal corticosteroids are reasonable for persistent diabetic macular edema after anti-VEGF therapy or when the eye is not a candidate for anti-VEGF first-line therapy.
  14. 12.14 Grade A Diabetic retinopathy is not a contraindication to aspirin used for cardioprotection because aspirin does not increase retinal hemorrhage risk.
  15. 12.15 Grade E People with diabetes-related vision loss should be counseled about vision rehabilitation and provided or referred for a comprehensive visual-impairment evaluation by a practitioner experienced in vision rehabilitation.
  16. 12.16 Grade E People with diabetes-related vision loss should receive eye-care educational materials and resources in addition to diabetes self-management education.
  17. 12.17 Grade B Assess all people with diabetes for diabetic peripheral neuropathy starting at type 2 diabetes diagnosis or 5 years after type 1 diabetes diagnosis and at least annually thereafter.
  18. 12.18 Grade B Assess distal symmetric polyneuropathy using history plus small-fiber testing with temperature or pinprick and large-fiber testing with vibration using a 128-Hz tuning fork; perform annual 10-g monofilament testing to identify feet at risk for ulceration and amputation.
  19. 12.19 Grade E Assess symptoms and signs of autonomic neuropathy starting at type 2 diabetes diagnosis or 5 years after type 1 diabetes diagnosis, at least annually and when other microvascular complications are present, especially kidney disease and diabetic peripheral neuropathy.
  20. 12.20 Grade A/C/B Optimize glucose management to prevent or delay neuropathy in type 1 diabetes and slow neuropathy progression in type 2 diabetes; optimize weight, blood pressure, and lipid management to reduce risk or slow progression of diabetic neuropathy.
  21. 12.21 Grade B/E Assess and treat painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy and symptoms of autonomic neuropathy to improve quality of life.
  22. 12.22 Grade A/A/B Use gabapentinoids, SNRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, or sodium channel blockers as initial pharmacologic treatments for diabetic neuropathic pain; combination therapy can provide additional relief, while opioids including tramadol and tapentadol should generally not be used except in rare circumstances.
  23. 12.23 Grade A Perform a comprehensive foot evaluation at least annually to identify risk factors for foot ulcers and amputations.
  24. 12.24 Grade B The foot examination should include skin inspection, assessment of deformity, neurologic assessment with 10-g monofilament or Ipswich touch plus at least one additional sensory assessment, and vascular assessment including leg and foot pulses.
  25. 12.25 Grade A People with sensory loss or prior ulceration or amputation should have their feet inspected at every visit.
  26. 12.26 Grade B Obtain history of prior ulceration, amputation, Charcot foot, angioplasty or vascular surgery, cigarette smoking, retinopathy, and renal disease and assess current neuropathy and vascular symptoms.
  27. 12.27 Grade B Initial PAD screening should assess lower-extremity pulses, capillary refill, dependent rubor, elevation pallor, and venous filling; people with suggestive symptoms or decreased/absent pedal pulses should be referred for ankle-brachial index with toe pressures and further vascular assessment.
  28. 12.28 Grade B Use an interprofessional approach facilitated by a podiatrist with appropriate team members for foot ulcers and high-risk feet, including dialysis, Charcot foot, prior ulcer or amputation, and PAD.
  29. 12.29 Grade B/A Refer smokers with prior lower-extremity complications, loss of protective sensation, structural abnormalities, or PAD to foot-care specialists for preventive care and lifelong surveillance, and provide smoking-cessation information and counseling referral.
  30. 12.30 Grade B Provide preventive foot self-care education to all people with diabetes, including those with loss of protective sensation, with instruction on daily foot surveillance by palpation or visual inspection using an unbreakable mirror.
  31. 12.31 Grade B Recommend specialized therapeutic footwear for people at high ulceration risk, including those with loss of protective sensation, deformity, ulcers, callus formation, poor peripheral circulation, or previous amputation.
  32. 12.32 Grade A For chronic diabetic foot ulcers that fail to heal with optimal standard care alone, consider adjunctive randomized-trial-proven advanced therapies such as negative-pressure wound therapy, selected skin substitutes, or topical oxygen therapy.

Evaluation & appraisal

Relevance 10/10

Directly actionable complication screening, treatment, medication-safety, neuropathic-pain, PAD, and foot-ulcer guidance for diabetes care.

Evidence basis: The chapter covers common and high-consequence microvascular/limb complications across outpatient and inpatient-facing practice.

Principal limit: It is section-specific and does not cover the complete Standards methodology.

Would change: A locally tailored implementation plan tied to institutional workflows would raise implementation specificity.

Novelty 6/10

The chapter mainly updates and integrates established practice while adding newer evidence on GLP-1 ocular/limb outcomes, AI retinal screening, modern anti-VEGF regimens, and advanced wound therapy.

Evidence basis: Current 2026 synthesis and recent citations are the main added value.

Principal limit: Many foundational recommendations rest on mature evidence and longstanding practice.

Would change: Major new comparative trials changing screening or treatment pathways would increase novelty.

Method strength 7/10

Recommendations draw on RCTs, systematic reviews, guidelines, cohort evidence, and expert opinion, with source-specific grades preserved.

Evidence basis: Multiple landmark and contemporary randomized data sets support major retinopathy and neuropathy/foot-care interventions.

Principal limit: The development method and formal evidence-grading definitions are in a separate ADA methodology section not included here.

Would change: Direct access to the full methodology and evidence tables would strengthen appraisal of recommendation development.

Evidence strength 8/10

Several core recommendations are supported by grade A/B evidence and landmark trials, while important areas explicitly remain observational or expert-opinion driven.

Evidence basis: Laser/anti-VEGF, glycemic control, neuropathic pain classes, and selected advanced wound therapies have direct trial/review support.

Principal limit: GLP-1 ocular effects, some PAD/neuropathy interventions, education, footwear, hyperbaric oxygen, and some advanced wound modalities remain uncertain or heterogeneous.

Would change: More high-quality, inclusive RCTs for the weaker domains would raise the score.

External validity 8/10

The chapter addresses broad diabetes populations and common clinical settings and includes person-centered escalation based on risk.

Evidence basis: Recommendations span type 1/type 2 diabetes, pregnancy, visual impairment, neuropathy, PAD, DFU, and high-risk foot states.

Principal limit: Some underlying wound-care and neuropathy trials exclude CKD/dialysis populations or use narrow trial populations.

Would change: More pragmatic evidence in high-comorbidity and access-limited populations would improve generalizability.

Implementation value 10/10

The chapter provides clear screening intervals, referral triggers, medication cautions, pain-treatment classes, PAD workup thresholds, foot-risk frequencies, and wound-care escalation gates.

Evidence basis: Recommendations 12.1–12.32 and Table 12.1 can be translated directly into clinical checklists and CDS rules.

Principal limit: Local referral availability, formulary, podiatry/vascular access, and specialty pathways still determine operational feasibility.

Would change: Mapping the recommendations to local EHR data and referral pathways would make the guidance directly executable.

Strengths

  • Actionable recommendation set with explicit evidence grades and escalation thresholds.
  • Combines mature landmark evidence with newer 2024–2025 literature.
  • Preserves uncertainty around GLP-1 ocular effects, neuropathy interventions, footwear, hyperbaric oxygen, and advanced wound products rather than presenting all domains as equally certain.
  • Operationally useful Table 12.1 risk stratification.

Limitations

  • The source is a secondary guideline chapter, not a primary study; underlying study results should not be treated as if generated by the ADA chapter.
  • The full Standards development method and grading definitions are external to this exact source.
  • Some wound-care RCTs exclude CKD/dialysis populations and cannot be blinded; real-world applicability varies.
  • Several medication safety/effectiveness areas are based on observational evidence or conflicting data and require clinical context.
Do not use for: replacing ophthalmology, neurology, vascular, podiatry, wound, or infectious-disease assessment when referral criteria are met; treating the chapter's secondary summaries as primary-study data; assuming recommendation grade definitions without consulting the ADA methodology.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Retinopathy screening CDS

Therapy-intensification safety
Check retinopathy status when rapid A1C reduction is likely; surface follow-up needs rather than prohibiting effective therapy.
Painful DPN order set

Foot risk registry
