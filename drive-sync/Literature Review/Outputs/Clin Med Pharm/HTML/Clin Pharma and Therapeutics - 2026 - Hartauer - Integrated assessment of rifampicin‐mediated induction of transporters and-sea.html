One-page synthesis / Abstract

Core thesis: A long-term primary-human-hepatocyte liver tissue chip can sustain repeated rifampicin exposure and jointly measure transcript, protein, CYP function, bile-acid handling, and intracellular perpetrator exposure. The integrated readouts reveal that induction signals are not interchangeable across biological layers.

The study treated three-donor liver tissue chips with 10 µM rifampicin (or DMSO vehicle) for 7 days and quantified transporter and drug-metabolizing-enzyme mRNA/protein, five CYP activities in Donors 2–3, extracellular bile acids, and intracellular rifampicin. The strongest protein responses were CYP-related: CYP2C19 increased up to 32.9-fold and CYP3A4 up to 8.7-fold, while transporters were generally more modest (e.g., OATP2B1 1.9–2.7-fold and P-gp 1.5–2.6-fold; OAT2 fell to 0.4–0.8-fold). Intracellular total rifampicin reached 203–249 µM despite 10 µM nominal media concentration, with estimated unbound intracellular concentrations of 1.94–2.39 µM.

The practical message is methodological rather than clinical: mRNA alone was sometimes discordant with protein or CYP activity, donor-to-donor effects were substantial, and intracellular drug exposure diverged markedly from nominal media concentration. The platform is therefore useful as an integrated mechanistic NAM test bed, but the single inducer/concentration, lack of direct transporter function, absence of IVIVE/PBPK, and small donor base prevent direct clinical DDI-risk prediction.

Introduction / Study rationale

Rifampicin is a canonical CYP inducer, but the clinical and mechanistic significance of hepatic transporter induction remains less certain. Conventional hepatocyte models lose differentiated function over time, often constrain induction experiments to short durations, and frequently rely on mRNA as a surrogate for protein or functional changes.

The authors position a long-term liver tissue chip as a way to observe repeated exposure while maintaining hepatocyte function and to place transcript changes beside protein abundance, CYP activity, bile-acid homeostasis, and intracellular rifampicin—readouts that can reveal when a single surrogate is misleading.

Study Highlights

Known

Rifampicin strongly induces CYPs; the magnitude and clinical meaning of hepatic transporter induction are less established, and short-lived models/mRNA surrogates create uncertainty.

Question

Can a long-term human liver tissue chip characterize rifampicin effects across matched transporter mRNA/protein, CYP mRNA/protein/activity, bile acids, and intracellular drug exposure?

Adds

Cross-layer matched measurements across three PHH donors plus intracellular rifampicin and bile-acid endpoints.

Potential impact

Provides a foundation for future qualification of an integrated liver NAM, rather than immediate clinical use.

Methods

Culture and exposure

Primary human hepatocytes from three donors were seeded at 215,000 cells/chamber on collagen I/fibronectin, overlaid with Matrigel, and maintained with recirculating media at 2 mL/h. On culture day 3, medium was replaced with 0.1% DMSO or 10 µM rifampicin; rifampicin was spiked daily for 7 days and cultures terminated on day 10. Morphology and albumin were used as longitudinal function/viability indicators.

Molecular and functional readouts

mRNA was quantified by RT-qPCR (ΔΔCt, beta-actin reference); transporter and enzyme proteins were measured by targeted absolute proteomics. CYP activity used a five-probe cocktail in Donors 2 and 3—midazolam/CYP3A4, diclofenac/CYP2C9, R-omeprazole/CYP2C19, tacrine/CYP1A2, and dextromethorphan/CYP2D6—with LC-MS/MS metabolite analysis. Donor 1 was excluded from this functional cocktail because CYP2D6 protein was undetectable and a CYP2D6 polymorphism was noted.

Bile acids, intracellular rifampicin, statistics

Daily media were profiled for GCA, GCDCA, GCDCA-3G, GCDCA-S, TCA, and TCDCA. Intracellular rifampicin was measured after the 7-day treatment period; unbound concentration used a reported intracellular unbound fraction of 0.0096. Multiple two-sided unpaired t-tests with Holm-Sidak correction were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.6.1.

Results

Culture integrity and albumin

DMSO controls maintained stable albumin output over 10 days. Rifampicin-treated Donor 1 and Donor 3 cultures showed approximately 47% and 30% lower albumin production, respectively, by day 10 versus controls, although reported albumin production remained within 5–17 µg/day/106 hepatocytes and morphology remained stable.

Transporters and drug-metabolizing enzymes

Transporter protein changes were comparatively modest: OATP2B1 rose 2.5-, 2.7-, and 1.9-fold across Donors 1–3; P-gp rose 2.6-, 1.6-, and 1.5-fold; OAT2 declined to 0.4-, 0.7-, and 0.8-fold. In contrast, CYP2C19 was the most strongly induced CYP protein (up to 32.9-fold), CYP3A4 reached up to 8.7-fold, and UGT1A4 increased 3.6–5.3-fold.

Matched transporter transcript/protein data showed pronounced discordance, especially in Donor 3 (e.g., OST-β mRNA 20.3-fold vs protein 1.2-fold). Matched CYP data also showed differing magnitudes across molecular and functional layers: for Donor 2 CYP2C9, mRNA/protein/activity changes were 1.9/2.0/4.2-fold, whereas Donor 3 values were 8.7/3.3/10.0-fold. This is the study's clearest demonstration that transcript alone does not reliably represent downstream response.

Bile-acid endpoints and intracellular rifampicin

TCA and TCDCA were significantly higher with rifampicin on days 8–10 in all donors. Donor 1 also showed sustained increases in GCDCA-3G and GCDCA-S; this pattern was not reproduced in Donors 2–3. Total extracellular bile-acid pools increased over time in both treatment groups, with GCA and GCDCA comprising most of the measured pool. BACS mRNA increased 3.1-fold (Donor 2) and 9.3-fold (Donor 3).

At study end, total intracellular rifampicin was 249 µM (Donor 1), 203 ± 28 µM (Donor 2), and 221 ± 18 µM (Donor 3); estimated unbound intracellular concentrations were 2.39, 1.94 ± 0.27, and 2.12 ± 0.18 µM, respectively. This substantial total intracellular accumulation relative to 10 µM nominal media exposure supports the authors' caution against assuming intracellular and extracellular unbound exposures are equivalent.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the study as evidence that a long-term human liver tissue chip can capture multiple regulatory layers during repeated inducer exposure. The transporter response was smaller than the CYP response, and donor-specific mRNA–protein–activity relationships reinforce the value of measuring protein/function rather than treating transcript as a universal induction surrogate.

The paper explicitly stops short of clinical qualification. Only one rifampicin concentration and one prototypical inducer were studied; transporter activity was not directly measured; and no IVIVE or PBPK model tested whether the in vitro signals predict patient DDI magnitude. The proposed next phase is multi-concentration/multi-inducer testing with transporter functional assays, CYP activity, and static/PBPK translation.

Interpretation boundary: The source supports mechanistic/NAM characterization. It does not establish a clinically actionable rifampicin transporter-induction magnitude or validate the platform for patient-specific DDI prediction.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for mechanistic DDI/NAM model development; do not use this study alone for clinical DDI decisions.
Relevance
9/10

Rationale: Highly relevant to transporter/CYP induction, DDI translational methods, and NAM qualification.

Evidence basis: Matched transporter, enzyme, bile-acid, and intracellular-exposure measurements in a human liver MPS.

Principal limiting factor: The work is pre-qualification and not a clinical outcomes study.

Would change with: Demonstrated clinical prediction or regulatory qualification would raise practical relevance further.

Novelty
8/10

Rationale: The integrated cross-layer readout within a long-duration human liver chip adds information beyond single-surrogate induction assays.

Evidence basis: Matched mRNA/protein/activity plus intracellular rifampicin and bile-acid measurements across the same platform.

Principal limiting factor: Several component assays and rifampicin induction concepts are established individually.

Would change with: Replication across more inducers/platforms and prospective prediction would strengthen novelty.

Method strength
7/10

Rationale: The design is internally coherent with matched controls and orthogonal assays, but the experimental breadth exceeds the biological sample depth.

Evidence basis: Three PHH donors; daily exposure; targeted proteomics; RT-qPCR; functional CYP cocktail; LC-MS/MS; corrected multiple testing.

Principal limiting factor: Single concentration/inducer, small donor set, technical replicates, and incomplete CYP activity in Donor 1.

Would change with: Pre-specified multi-dose/multi-inducer experiments with more donors and external replication.

Evidence strength
6/10

Rationale: The data directly support mechanistic cross-layer differences within this model, but not clinical prediction.

Evidence basis: Large and consistent CYP signals, transporter patterns, matched mRNA/protein/activity, Table 1 exposure data.

Principal limiting factor: No direct transporter function, IVIVE/PBPK, or clinical validation; supplement unavailable in this task.

Would change with: Functional transporter endpoints plus external/predictive validation and quantitative IVIVE.

External validity
4/10

Rationale: Generalization is limited to this liver-chip implementation and the tested donor/inducer conditions.

Evidence basis: Three primary-human-hepatocyte donors provide some biological heterogeneity.

Principal limiting factor: One platform, three donors, one drug, one concentration, no independent site/platform replication.

Would change with: Multisite/platform replication with broader donor and inducer diversity and clinical benchmark compounds.

Implementation value
7/10

Rationale: The paper provides a concrete integrated assay concept and interpretable readouts useful for designing induction studies.

Evidence basis: Detailed culture/exposure scheme, assay layers, probe cocktail, exposure measurement, and proposed qualification path.

Principal limiting factor: Supplementary material is unavailable here, and qualification/prediction workflows are unfinished.

Would change with: Open full SOPs/data, validated decision thresholds, and a demonstrated IVIVE/PBPK workflow.

Strengths

  • Orthogonal measurements expose mRNA–protein–activity discordance rather than assuming surrogate equivalence.
  • Intracellular rifampicin measurement addresses a major hidden assumption in induction assay interpretation.
  • Three PHH donors reveal donor-dependent response structure.
  • Main-text visual evidence is quantitative and internally cross-referential.

Limitations

  • Single rifampicin concentration: no Emax/IndC50 characterization.
  • Single inducer: limited generalizability to other agonists/inducers.
  • No direct transporter functional induction assay.
  • No IVIVE/PBPK or clinical predictive validation.
  • Small donor set and technical-replicate structure constrain external validity.
  • Supplementary material referenced by the paper was not supplied for Task 13 and could not be independently reconciled.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use as a design reference for integrated induction experiments: pair transcript with protein/function and measure intracellular perpetrator exposure when feasible.

Do not use now

Do not convert the reported transporter-protein fold changes into a clinical dose adjustment or assume the chip has been qualified for clinical DDI prediction.

High-value signal

Donor- and layer-specific discordance—especially CYP2C9 activity vs transcript and transporter mRNA vs protein—argues against relying on a single induction biomarker.

Next evidence needed

Multi-concentration and multi-inducer panels, transporter functional assays, larger donor sets, external replication, and static/PBPK translation against clinical DDI benchmarks.

Clinical translation: No immediate patient-care change is supported. This is platform-characterization evidence, not clinical efficacy/safety or validated DDI-management evidence.
