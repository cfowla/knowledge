One-page synthesis

Bottom line verdict: Skim deeply. The article is useful for mapping candidate genes and recent pharmacogenomic signals, but the clinical evidence remains heterogeneous, largely observational or PK-focused, and insufficient for routine practice change.

Core thesis. The review argues that polymorphisms in rivaroxaban metabolism and transport genes—especially CYP3A4, CYP3A5, ABCB1, and ABCG2—may explain part of the inter-individual variability in exposure, efficacy, and bleeding risk. The authors also highlight emerging bleeding-associated loci such as SUSD3 and APOB, and suggest that pharmacogenomic data plus drug–drug interaction information could eventually support safer dose individualization.

Main value. The article assembles clinically relevant gene–variant signals for rivaroxaban, identifies where evidence is conflicting, and places PGx into a pharmacist-relevant context: renal function, age, polypharmacy, CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors or inducers, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, and adherence remain central modifiers of DOAC safety.

Main limitation. The source does not establish a validated dosing algorithm. Many included studies are small, population-specific, surrogate-focused, or inconsistent. The authors’ own chi-square comparison of significant versus non-significant variant annotations was non-significant (P = 0.157), which weakens any strong claim that the listed SNPs are clinically reliable predictors.

Best use
Background for anticoagulation PGx notes, research triage, case discussion when exposure or bleeding is unexpected, and decision-support concept generation.
Do not use for
Genotype-only dose reduction, routine screening justification, or declaring rivaroxaban failure/bleeding causality without renal function, interacting drugs, indication, dose, food intake, and adherence.
Most actionable clinical idea
PGx signals are most plausible in high-risk contexts: older adults, renal impairment, polypharmacy, strong CYP3A4/P-gp modulation, antiplatelet exposure, and unexplained bleeding or thrombosis.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames thromboembolism and atrial fibrillation-related stroke as high-burden conditions and positions rivaroxaban as a direct oral factor Xa inhibitor with generally predictable pharmacokinetics but meaningful inter-individual variability. The authors state that some patients have inadequate response or recurrent thrombosis while others experience bleeding risk.

The abstract’s main claim is that variants in CYP3A4, CYP3A5, ABCB1, and ABCG2 can affect rivaroxaban PK and response, while SUSD3 and APOB are emerging bleeding-risk loci. It concludes that PGx analysis combined with interaction data could guide future dose adjustment, but the evidentiary strength is not quantified in the abstract.

Introduction

The introduction contrasts warfarin’s monitoring burden and interaction profile with DOAC advantages, then narrows to rivaroxaban. It notes fixed dosing, lower monitoring requirements, and non-inferiority versus warfarin for treatment and prevention contexts, while listing important non-use populations such as mechanical valves, antiphospholipid syndrome, severe renal or hepatic dysfunction, pregnancy, and breastfeeding.

The clinically relevant gap is variability despite fixed dosing. The authors note bleeding and inadequate response, the hazard of missed doses due to rivaroxaban’s short half-life relative to warfarin, once-daily administration, food-dependent absorption for relevant doses, moderate renal clearance, and lower bleeding risk with apixaban compared with rivaroxaban in some contexts.

Some Pharmacological Features of Rivaroxaban

This section summarizes mechanism and disposition: rivaroxaban selectively inhibits factor Xa, decreases thrombin generation, reduces fibrin clot formation, has approximately 80%–100% oral bioavailability, reaches peak concentration within a few hours, and is about 95% protein bound. Approximately two-thirds undergoes metabolic degradation and one-third is renally excreted unchanged.

The pharmacogenomic rationale centers on hepatic metabolism through CYP3A4/5 and CYP2J2, plus transporter effects. The section also situates rivaroxaban among other DOACs whose exposure can be influenced by genes such as CES1, ABCB1, CYP3A4, sulfotransferases, and P-glycoprotein.

Aim and research methodology

The stated aim is to clarify and analyze the impact of genetic polymorphisms on rivaroxaban pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and toxicity. The search used ClinPGx, SNPedia, and PubMed for material from 2009 through 2025 and early 2026, including ClinPGx drug-label annotations, clinical annotations, and 65 variant annotations.

Inclusion criteria focused on ClinPGx articles about rivaroxaban-related SNPs with general minor allele frequency above 0.1 in SNPedia and PubMed articles relevant to rivaroxaban pharmacogenomics. The authors excluded meta-analyses and reviews from the analysis, excluded similar-outcome articles unless needed, and generally excluded SNPs with MAF below 1% unless supportive. The methods lack a formal systematic-review protocol, PRISMA flow, duplicate screening description, risk-of-bias tool, or pooled effect approach.

Results of data analysis

The authors downloaded ClinPGx variant annotations for the top four rivaroxaban-related genes—ABCG2, ABCB1, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5—and classified SNPs as significantly or non-significantly associated with pharmacokinetics or toxicity. Some SNPs appeared as significant in one study and non-significant in another, especially in ABCB1.

The chi-square comparison across the four genes found no statistically significant difference between the frequency of significant and non-significant SNP results (P = 0.157). The authors interpret this as pointing to insufficiently strong conclusions, especially for ABCB1, and a need for further studies to confirm or reject clinically meaningful effects.

Individual genetic polymorphisms

CYP3A4 variants such as rs2246709, rs3735451, and rs2242480 are presented as associated with altered concentrations, prothrombin time, and minor bleeding in selected AF data. CYP3A5 rs776746 is framed as a functional variant where reduced enzyme activity can raise rivaroxaban exposure, although studies disagree and renal function sometimes dominates concentration variability.

CYP2J2 is treated as a less established contributor; CYP2J2 inhibition by imatinib may increase exposure, but CYP2J2*7 rs890293 did not influence exposure in one NVAF catheter ablation cohort, with limited power because the TT variant was not detected. ABCG2 and ABCB1 transporters receive the most extensive discussion, with recurrent but inconsistent signals involving exposure, bleeding, thromboembolism, and clearance.

Emerging genetic predictors for bleeding

The review identifies APOB rs13306198 as associated with higher bleeding risk in 468 patients on DOAC therapy. It also discusses CYP2C19 only as a clinically indirect modifier when rivaroxaban is combined with clopidogrel, where CYP2C19*17 carriers may have lower platelet reactivity and higher hemorrhage risk without altered rivaroxaban concentration.

Other exploratory loci include NCMAP, PRF1, PRKAG2, POU2F3, GOT2, and MMP13. The review appropriately labels these as less robust and targets for future studies rather than established clinical predictors.

Clinical implications, future perspectives, and conclusion

The clinical implications section argues that genetic diversity may contribute to rivaroxaban efficacy and safety, especially through CYP3A4/5, ABCB1, and ABCG2. It also emphasizes ethnic allele-frequency differences, older age, renal impairment, antiplatelet or NSAID exposure, and CYP3A4/P-gp drug interactions as clinically relevant contexts.

The future-perspectives and conclusion sections call for larger, less heterogeneous studies, genome-wide approaches, prospective validation, and cost-effectiveness work. The authors ultimately recommend integrating PGx and drug interaction data, while acknowledging that drug–drug interactions may be more important than PGx analysis in practice.

Gene-level appraisal

CYP3A4
Most biologically plausible metabolic signal. Variants rs2246709, rs3735451, and rs2242480 are associated with higher rivaroxaban concentration, PT changes, or minor bleeding in selected studies. Limitation: evidence is not sufficient to define a dosing action, especially without interaction context.
CYP3A5
rs776746 has plausible functional relevance, with reduced activity potentially raising exposure. Limitation: studies conflict, and renal function may dominate concentration variability in some cohorts.
CYP2J2
Mechanistically relevant but clinically underdeveloped. CYP2J2*7 rs890293 was not associated with exposure in one cohort; drug inhibition by imatinib is a more plausible near-term concern than genotype alone.
ABCG2
rs2231142 / 421C>A is linked to higher exposure or altered distribution in healthy-volunteer studies, particularly relevant to East Asian allele-frequency considerations. Limitation: clinical bleeding and trough-concentration findings are inconsistent.
ABCB1
Most heavily discussed transporter gene, with rs1045642, rs1128503, rs2032582, and rs4148738 tied to PK, bleeding, or thromboembolism in different studies. Limitation: direction and clinical meaning vary across cohorts.
Emerging loci
SUSD3, APOB, PRF1, PRKAG2, NCMAP, GOT2, and MMP13 are research leads. Limitation: not ready for clinical decision rules.

Implementation and practice takeaways

For acute-care pharmacy
Do not adjust rivaroxaban dose solely by genotype. Prioritize indication-appropriate dose, renal/hepatic function, food requirements, adherence, interacting drugs, antiplatelets/NSAIDs, age, and bleeding history.
For unexplained bleeding
PGx can be a secondary explanatory layer after assessing dose, timing, renal function, hepatic function, interacting inhibitors, antithrombotic overlap, and procedural factors.
For unexplained thrombosis
Assess adherence, missed doses, absorption with food, renal/hepatic changes, interacting inducers, indication, and contraindicated populations before attributing failure to genotype.
For research notes
Track CYP3A4/5, ABCB1, and ABCG2 as primary candidate-gene buckets. Keep SUSD3, APOB, and other exploratory loci as emerging rather than actionable.
For clinical decision support
A safer CDS rule would flag high-risk combinations and patient factors first, then optionally display PGx evidence as low-certainty contextual information.
What would change practice
A prospective trial showing that genotype-informed selection, dose adjustment, or monitoring reduces major bleeding or thromboembolism without unacceptable under-anticoagulation.
