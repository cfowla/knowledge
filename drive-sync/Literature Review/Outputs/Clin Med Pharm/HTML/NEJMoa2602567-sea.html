One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. A large, well-controlled phase 3 trial showing a clinically meaningful steroid-sparing efficacy signal for secukinumab in recently relapsed polymyalgia rheumatica. The evidence is strong enough to matter, but one trial with one-year follow-up is not sufficient by itself to define routine practice.
Primary efficacySustained remission at week 52: 41.2% with 300 mg, 40.6% with 150 mg, vs 20.4% with placebo; P<0.001 for each dose vs placebo.
Steroid exposureAdjusted annual cumulative glucocorticoid dose: 1603.7 mg (300 mg), 1683.2 mg (150 mg), vs 2093.0 mg placebo; differences −489.4 mg and −409.9 mg.
Escape / rescue50.8%, 51.6%, and 75.6% received escape or rescue treatment; hazard ratio 0.5 for each secukinumab dose vs placebo.
SafetySerious AEs were similar across arms (13.5%, 15.9%, 14.2%), but infections, fungal infections, and hypersensitivity were numerically more frequent with secukinumab.

Core interpretation. Adding secukinumab to a protocolized 24-week prednisone taper approximately doubled the proportion achieving sustained remission and reduced cumulative glucocorticoid exposure versus the taper alone. Efficacy signals were directionally consistent for complete sustained remission, delay to escape/rescue therapy, fatigue, physical function, and glucocorticoid-toxicity measures. The two secukinumab doses had similar point estimates, but the trial was not designed to prove dose equivalence or noninferiority between 150 mg and 300 mg.

Main caution. Follow-up for efficacy is limited to one year. The selected population had recently relapsed PMR and excluded giant-cell arteritis and several important competing conditions. Safety event counts are too small for uncommon harms; one cryptococcal-meningitis death in the 150-mg group was considered potentially treatment related by the investigator in the setting of substantial baseline risk factors.

Methods

Trial design and oversight

The REPLENISH trial was phase 3, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, and conducted at 148 sites in 28 countries. Patients were randomized 1:1:1. The sponsor, Novartis, and steering committee participated in trial design; investigators collected data; the sponsor performed site monitoring, data collation, and analysis. The study followed Good Clinical Practice and local ethical requirements, with written informed consent.

This sponsor role does not invalidate the design, but it increases the importance of the prespecified analysis framework, transparency of amendments, and reproducibility of results.

Population and randomization

Eligibility required age ≥50 years, EULAR/ACR PMR classification, bilateral shoulder pain, elevated CRP (≥10 mg/L) or ESR (≥30 mm/hr), recent relapse while tapering glucocorticoids, and substantial prior glucocorticoid treatment. Randomization was stratified by region; non-Japan participants were additionally stratified by baseline glucocorticoid dose.

474 patients were screened; 381 were randomized (127 per arm). Two randomized patients, one in each secukinumab arm, did not receive treatment because they did not meet eligibility criteria. The safety population therefore included 126, 126, and 127 patients.

Intervention and glucocorticoid taper

Secukinumab was given weekly through week 4 and every 4 weeks thereafter through week 52 at either 300 mg or 150 mg. Placebo matched the schedule. All groups received a protocolized prednisone taper starting at 10 or 15 mg/day, targeted for completion over 24 weeks.

The appendix allowed limited pre-week-12 taper flexibility: up to two dosing intervals could be prolonged to four weeks or one single-step dose increase could be used, but these options were mutually exclusive. Escape glucocorticoid therapy could be used for inadequate response/relapse while continuing assigned study treatment; alternative rescue therapy required discontinuation of study drug/placebo.

Outcomes and estimands

The primary outcome required remission by week 12 sustained through week 52, with no PMR recurrence and no new GCA diagnosis requiring escape/rescue. Complete sustained remission additionally required inflammatory-marker criteria. Secondary outcomes included annual cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, time to first escape/rescue, FACIT-Fatigue, and HAQ-DI. GTI-AIS and GTI-CWS were exploratory.

For the primary estimand, treatment discontinuation or prohibited medication before week 52 was handled with a composite nonresponse strategy. Logistic regression adjusted for baseline prednisone dose, region, and sex. Multiplicity was controlled with a sequential strategy. A tipping-point sensitivity analysis and a supplementary treatment-policy estimand were prespecified/supportive.

Results

Disposition and baseline

Of 379 treated participants, 289 (76.3%) completed the treatment period; discontinuation was more frequent with placebo. Mean age was approximately 70 years, 70.3% were women, and 70.0% were White. Baseline features were broadly balanced across groups.

Three deaths occurred: cryptococcal meningitis and cardiac failure in the SEC-150 group, and completed suicide in placebo. The investigator considered cryptococcal meningitis potentially related to secukinumab; the patient also had long-term glucocorticoid exposure and a preexisting meningioma. The other two deaths were considered unrelated. The appendix provides additional case-level context. No inflammatory bowel disease or hepatitis B reactivation events were reported; other events of interest were uncommon.

Safety interpretation: Similar serious-AE percentages should not be read as proof of equivalent safety. With roughly 126 treated patients per active arm, the trial has limited ability to characterize uncommon or delayed harms. The numerical increase in fungal infections is clinically relevant in an older, glucocorticoid-exposed population.

Companion-material findings that affect interpretation

Supplementary Appendix

  • The taper allowed limited early adjustment, reducing the risk that the nominal “24-week taper” is misread as completely inflexible.
  • Missing-data rules classified many important intercurrent events as nonresponse; a treatment-policy supplementary estimand still supported the primary efficacy signal.
  • The tipping-point sensitivity analysis did not identify a tipping point within the evaluated assumptions.
  • Tables S5 and S6 supply the patient-reported outcome and glucocorticoid-toxicity estimates used above; S7–S10 expand the safety profile.
  • The fatal-event summary adds context to the cryptococcal-meningitis death and underlying immunosuppression risk.

Protocol and statistical analysis plan

  • The final protocol was Amended Protocol Version 03. Amendment 3 (September 12, 2024) states that its changes did not affect the study population or primary/secondary objectives.
  • Amendment 2 (June 5, 2024), when 264 participants had been randomized, included clarifications and estimand/statistical updates; the protocol states these did not affect the population or objectives.
  • The final SAP was Amendment 4, released October 1, 2025, before database lock. It documents that analysis changes were made before database lock.
  • On August 4, 2025, before database lock, the analysis of annual cumulative glucocorticoid dose, FACIT-Fatigue, and HAQ-DI was updated to ANCOVA with Huber–White sandwich standard errors after health-authority feedback; some intercurrent-event/missing-data handling was also revised.
  • No formal interim analysis was planned; the primary analysis was to occur after all participants completed week 52 or discontinued.
Appraisal implication. Post-enrollment analytical amendments for secondary outcomes deserve disclosure, but the documented timing before database lock/unblinding and the stable primary objective lessen concern that they were outcome-driven. The primary endpoint remains the most secure efficacy claim.

Discussion, source claims, and limitations

Authors’ interpretation

The authors conclude that secukinumab plus a 24-week glucocorticoid taper produced superior sustained remission and a significant glucocorticoid-sparing effect compared with taper alone, supporting IL-17A inhibition as an effective treatment option for recently relapsed PMR.

They also interpret the fatigue, physical-function, and GTI findings as reinforcing clinical relevance and state that the overall safety findings align with the known secukinumab safety profile.

Limitations reported by the source

The article explicitly notes the absence of efficacy data beyond one year, greater discontinuation in placebo (which might underdetect adverse events from conventional treatment), and uncertainty about whether continued glucocorticoids for 52 weeks would have increased placebo remission.

The source also compares the low placebo remission with prior PMR/GCA trials to argue against a simple taper-duration explanation, but that comparison is contextual rather than randomized within REPLENISH.

Additional appraisal limitations

The trial answers efficacy versus a standardized taper, not comparative effectiveness versus another biologic steroid-sparing agent. Similar estimates in the two secukinumab groups do not establish that 150 mg and 300 mg are clinically interchangeable. Sponsor involvement in design, monitoring, analysis, and medical writing creates a conflict-of-interest context that should be considered alongside the strong trial design.

External validity is best for older adults with recently relapsed, inflammatory-marker-positive PMR in specialty-care settings who resemble the eligibility population; conclusions should be more cautious in newly diagnosed disease, concomitant GCA, inflammatory-marker-negative presentations, or patients at especially high infection risk.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses steroid-sparing treatment of relapsed PMR with patient-relevant remission, function, fatigue, and safety outcomes.

Evidence basis: Large phase 3 PMR trial with 52-week follow-up.

Principal limit: Applies to a selected relapsed-PMR population rather than all PMR.

What would change the score: Current guideline/regulatory integration and replication would increase practice relevance.

Novelty
8/10

Tests IL-17A inhibition in a large phase 3 PMR program and adds a new mechanism to the steroid-sparing evidence base.

Evidence basis: Two secukinumab doses were tested against placebo with a standardized taper.

Principal limit: Novel mechanism does not itself establish superiority over other steroid-sparing options.

What would change the score: Head-to-head comparative data would raise the score.

Method strength
8/10

Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multinational design with multiplicity control, prespecified estimands, and sensitivity analyses is strong.

Evidence basis: 381 randomized across 148 sites in 28 countries; primary outcome analyzed with adjusted logistic regression; tipping-point and supplementary estimands support robustness.

Principal limit: Sponsor performed monitoring/collation/analysis; some secondary-endpoint SAP methods were amended after enrollment but before database lock.

What would change the score: Independent replication and fully pre-enrollment finalization of all secondary analysis methods would raise confidence.

Evidence strength
8/10

Both secukinumab doses approximately doubled sustained-remission proportions and reduced glucocorticoid exposure, with consistent escape/rescue and PRO signals.

Evidence basis: Sustained remission 41.2%/40.6% vs 20.4%; cumulative glucocorticoid dose reduced by about 409–489 mg; hazard ratio for escape/rescue 0.5 for both doses.

Principal limit: One-year efficacy horizon and limited power for uncommon safety outcomes constrain certainty.

What would change the score: Longer follow-up and larger pooled safety experience in PMR would raise the score.

External validity
7/10

Broad multinational enrollment improves transportability to specialty-care patients with relapsed PMR.

Evidence basis: 148 sites, 28 countries; mean age about 70 years; 70.3% women.

Principal limit: Eligibility excluded GCA and several competing inflammatory/infectious conditions and required recent relapse plus inflammatory-marker criteria.

What would change the score: Pragmatic trials in broader PMR populations and routine-care settings would raise the score.

Implementation value
7/10

The regimen, taper, remission definitions, and rescue rules are detailed enough to inform evidence review and protocol design.

Evidence basis: 300-mg and 150-mg dosing schedules, 24-week taper, rescue/escape criteria, and safety events are described in the article and companions.

Principal limit: The trial alone does not establish current approval status, formulary position, or guideline recommendation; those were not externally verified in this source-grounded run.

What would change the score: Current regulatory/guideline verification, cost data, and comparative-effectiveness evidence would raise the score.

Bottom line: The efficacy signal is persuasive for the studied population and is supported by multiple endpoints and sensitivity analyses. For practice, the strongest defensible use of this paper is as high-quality evidence that secukinumab can be glucocorticoid-sparing in relapsed PMR—not as a standalone directive to initiate therapy without current guideline, regulatory, safety, and patient-specific context.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the internal trial interpretation because the primary article, supplement, and protocol/SAP were available and reconciled. Confidence is deliberately lower for current implementation status because this run did not substitute external regulatory/guideline sources for the requested Drive publication set.
