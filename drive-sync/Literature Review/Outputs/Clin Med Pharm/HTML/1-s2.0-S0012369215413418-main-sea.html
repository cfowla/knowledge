One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Cairns and colleagues frame coronary thrombosis as a platelet- and thrombin-driven complication of plaque disruption and argue that antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy can reduce thrombotic complications across acute MI and unstable angina. The article then translates a heterogeneous evidence base into 1992 recommendations for aspirin, heparin, and—in selected post-MI situations—warfarin.

For acute MI, the review draws on older randomized anticoagulation trials, mural-thrombus studies, thrombolytic-era trials, and long-term oral-anticoagulant trials. Early studies suggest fewer stroke, pulmonary embolism, mural thrombus, and systemic embolic events with anticoagulation; mortality effects are less consistent. In anterior MI, mural thrombus is emphasized as a high-risk phenotype: the article reports mural thrombosis rates up to roughly 40% in anterior infarction and cites randomized/subrandomized heparin studies showing substantial reductions in echocardiographic thrombus formation. For thrombolytic-era adjunctive heparin, however, benefit is much less secure: large studies show little or no mortality advantage while bleeding increases, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that commonly used heparin regimens had not been tested in well-designed trials.

For antiplatelet therapy, the evidence is stronger. ISIS-2 is used as the pivotal acute-MI evidence: aspirin 160 mg daily for one month reduced five-week vascular mortality by 21% and, when combined with streptokinase, produced an approximately 40% relative reduction; nonfatal reinfarction and stroke were also reduced. A 25-trial antiplatelet overview (about 29,000 patients) is reported as reducing vascular mortality by 15%, nonfatal MI by 32%, nonfatal stroke by 27%, and overall vascular events by 25%. In unstable angina, four randomized aspirin trials show consistent reductions in cardiac death/nonfatal MI, while heparin trials suggest benefit but are smaller and methodologically less secure. Primary-prevention aspirin lowers MI but increases bleeding and does not lower all-cause or cardiovascular mortality in the evidence summarized here.

The panel's final recommendations are historically aggressive by present-day context: therapeutic heparin is recommended for embolic-risk AMI and anterior Q-wave MI; at least low-dose heparin is recommended for all AMI; heparin is considered with thrombolysis; aspirin 160–325 mg is recommended promptly in AMI and unstable angina; and full-dose heparin is added for severe hospitalized unstable angina. These recommendations are useful as a map of historical decision logic, not as a modern order-set specification.

Section condensation

Introduction and pathophysiology

The article centers plaque disruption, platelet activation, and thrombin generation as the pathophysiologic bridge from stable atherosclerosis to unstable angina, acute MI, and sudden coronary death. It cites early angiographic/pathologic observations showing occlusive thrombosis in more than 80% of transmural infarctions and thrombosis/plaque injury or both in most sudden-death specimens, using these observations to motivate antiplatelet and anticoagulant strategies.

The authors distinguish unstable angina as intermittent/partial compromise of coronary flow, Q-wave MI as generally associated with complete occlusion, and non-Q-wave MI as an intermediate phenotype. This mechanistic framework is plausible and coherent, but it is not itself comparative treatment evidence.

Myocardial infarction — anticoagulation

Older randomized studies suggest anticoagulation reduces thromboembolic complications after AMI. The review cites a pooled randomized dataset of 3,854 patients in which case fatality fell from 19.6% to 15.4% and thromboembolism from 21.3% to 11.1%, while hemorrhage increased from 2.75% to 8.4%. The review then focuses on left-ventricular mural thrombosis, particularly after anterior MI: Turpie's study (n=221) reports mural thrombus at day 10 in 11% with higher-dose subcutaneous heparin versus 32% with lower-dose heparin; a SCATI anterior-MI subgroup reports 17.7% versus 36.5%. Echocardiography is presented as useful but imperfect for thrombus detection.

Evidence becomes less consistent in the thrombolytic era. GISSI-2 and a large international comparison show no significant mortality benefit from adjunctive heparin but more bleeding; TAMI-3 shows identical 90-minute patency (79%) with and without heparin. SCATI reports signals toward lower mortality/recurrent ischemia but more bleeding. Crucially, the authors state that common heparin regimens used with thrombolytics lacked testing in well-designed clinical trials. Long-term oral anticoagulation trials (notably Sixty Plus and WARIS) show reductions in mortality/reinfarction, but these findings arose before contemporary secondary prevention.

Myocardial infarction — antiplatelet therapy

Acute aspirin evidence is dominated by ISIS-2. The review reports a 21% relative reduction in five-week vascular mortality with aspirin 160 mg/day for one month, a 40% relative reduction when aspirin is combined with streptokinase, and reductions in nonfatal reinfarction and nonfatal stroke. Minor bleeding increased modestly, without a significant increase in major bleeding or hemorrhagic stroke in the source's summary.

For longer-term therapy, individual mortality trials are mixed, but the Antiplatelet Trialists' overview of 25 randomized trials and about 29,000 patients is presented as persuasive: vascular mortality −15%, nonfatal MI −32%, nonfatal stroke −27%, and total vascular events −25%. The panel therefore favors aspirin over warfarin for routine long-term post-MI therapy unless embolic risk warrants anticoagulation.

Unstable angina

Aspirin has the clearest evidence in this section. Lewis, Cairns, Theroux, and RISC each show substantial reductions in cardiac death/nonfatal MI; for example, Lewis reports 5.0% vs 10.1% at three months and Theroux 3.3% vs 12.0% at six days. The consistency across trials supports the panel's grade A aspirin recommendation for unstable angina in the source's evidence framework.

Heparin is supported by smaller trials. Theroux reports fatal/nonfatal MI of 1.2% with heparin versus 7.5% with placebo and less refractory angina, but aspirin-plus-heparin did not demonstrate a statistically significant advantage over heparin alone and the study was underpowered for active-treatment comparisons. The review also flags limitations in RISC heparin dosing/timing and post-randomization exclusions. Thus the heparin signal is clinically plausible but methodologically less secure than the aspirin evidence.

Primary prevention and stable coronary artery disease

The Physicians' Health Study (22,071 male physicians) is summarized as showing fewer first MIs with aspirin 325 mg every other day (2.55 vs 4.37 per 1,000/year) and fewer important vascular events, but more bleeding and transfusion. The review reports no reduction in cardiovascular or all-cause mortality and notes concern for stroke/hemorrhagic stroke. The Nurses' Health Study is observational and suggests an association between aspirin use and lower first-MI risk, particularly among women at least 50 years old, but confounding remains possible.

For stable angina and established CAD, the source extrapolates from secondary-prevention and subgroup evidence to recommend indefinite aspirin. For primary prevention it recommends against routine aspirin below age 50 and consideration above age 50, with different evidence grades by sex. Those age/sex recommendations are historically informative but should not be carried forward as current practice rules.

Summary and recommendations

The final pages convert the review into an explicit 1992 treatment framework. For high-embolic-risk AMI without thrombolysis, therapeutic heparin is recommended at approximately 75 U/kg IV bolus followed by about 1,250 U/h IV (or 17,500 U SC every 12 h), targeting an APTT 1.5–2 times control, then warfarin INR 2–3 for 1–3 months; long-term warfarin is recommended for atrial fibrillation. Similar full-dose heparin is recommended for anterior Q-wave MI, while at least low-dose heparin 7,500 U SC every 12 h for at least seven days/until ambulatory is recommended for all AMI. With thrombolysis, heparin for about 48 hours receives a lower grade.

Aspirin 160–325 mg is recommended promptly and indefinitely in AMI and unstable angina. For more severe hospitalized unstable angina, the panel recommends adding full therapeutic heparin for 3–4 days or until pain resolves. Ticlopidine is suggested for aspirin allergy; warfarin is reserved for selected aspirin/ticlopidine contraindications. These are source-derived historical recommendations and are not independently verified against current practice standards in this artifact.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High value for reconstructing the historical rationale, evidence chain, dose/monitoring conventions, and uncertainty around heparin in coronary disease; unsuitable as a stand-alone contemporary practice source.
Relevance — 9/10Highly aligned with a heparin deep dive and directly addresses AMI/unstable-angina anticoagulation. Basis: multiple heparin trials, dosing approaches, thromboembolic endpoints, bleeding, and recommendations. Limit: historical coronary-care context. Would rise: not meaningfully; relevance is already high.
Novelty — 3/10Low present-day novelty because the article is from 1992, although it is historically informative. Basis: synthesizes then-current trials and recommendations rather than introducing a new prospective study. Limit: later evidence necessarily absent. Would rise: if the task were specifically historical guideline evolution.
Method strength — 4/10The evidence base includes randomized trials, but the article itself is a narrative/consensus synthesis without a transparent systematic search/selection method. Basis: heterogeneous designs, some underpowered/open/retrospective evidence, and explicit author caveats. Limit: no reproducible review methodology. Would rise: prespecified search, selection, risk-of-bias assessment, and quantitative synthesis.
Evidence strength — 5/10Moderate overall: strong aspirin evidence and some randomized anticoagulation evidence coexist with surrogate endpoints, older regimens, and inconsistent heparin effects in the thrombolytic era. Basis: large ISIS-2/meta-analytic antiplatelet data, older anticoagulation RCTs, thrombolysis-era neutral results. Limit: mixed eras and evidence types. Would rise: contemporary randomized patient-centered outcomes using current background therapy.
External validity — 3/10Low for current coronary-care practice because the source predates modern PCI, contemporary dual-antiplatelet strategies, and many current secondary-prevention standards. Basis: publication era and studied regimens. Limit: baseline risks and treatment pathways have changed. Would rise: replication in modern ACS populations.
Implementation value — 4/10Useful for historical rule provenance and identifying primary trials to retrieve, but not for direct CDS/order-set implementation. Basis: explicit dose/APTT/INR recommendations and evidence grades. Limit: 1992 practice framework. Would rise: modern guideline reconciliation and validation against current care pathways.

Implementation / research takeaways

High-value historical signalHeparin's clearest historical rationale is prevention of mural thrombosis/systemic embolism in high-risk anterior AMI and treatment of severe unstable angina—not a uniform mortality effect across all AMI settings.
Important uncertaintyThe authors themselves state that commonly used heparin regimens with thrombolytics lacked well-designed trial validation. This is a key anti-overclaiming point for any historical CDS provenance chain.
Safety tradeoffAcross several datasets, anticoagulation decreases thromboembolic outcomes while increasing bleeding; the strength of benefit varies sharply by phenotype and treatment era.
Source-following strategyUse this review to identify and retrieve the underlying primary trials (e.g., Turpie, SCATI, GISSI-2, WARIS, Theroux, ISIS-2) before creating primary-study atoms or contemporary rules.
Schema implicationThe current LiteratureAtom schema lacks a dedicated recommendation kind, so historical panel recommendations require tagged other atoms rather than pretending they are trial results.
Practice boundaryKeep the 1992 dose/APTT/INR recommendations as historical evidence provenance only until reconciled with current guidelines and institutional policy.
