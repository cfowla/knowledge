One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In this long-running institutional series, recurrent thromboembolism remained concentrated during treatment and the first several months after discharge. The authors argued that anticoagulants were preferable to operative management, favored a more intensive oral-anticoagulation target for patients presenting with PE, and favored several months of continued anticoagulation after discharge.

The primary dataset comprised 2,027 episodes in 1,744 patients treated from 1945 through 1964. During hospital treatment, recurrent pulmonary embolic complications occurred in 31/639 courses (4.9%) initially presenting with PE and 44/1,388 (3.2%) initially presenting with DVT; fatal PE occurred in 1.3% and 0.14%, respectively. Major bleeding occurred in 2.4% of treatment episodes and minor bleeding in 5.5%. The paper emphasizes that some early “complications” may have begun before treatment because PE could be clinically silent until infarction symptoms appeared.

An embedded prospective allocation by odd/even hospital registration number compared Quick prothrombin activity targets of 10-29% versus 30-49%. Among patients presenting with PE, thromboembolic complications were 3.0% versus 7.4% (chi-square 3.55; 0.05<p<0.10), a borderline result rather than conventional statistical significance. No meaningful difference appeared among patients presenting with DVT (3.3% versus 3.6%). After discharge, events clustered early: 45% of complications within 12 weeks occurred in the first 2 weeks and another 25% in the next 2 weeks. A nonrandom comparison showed 12-week complication rates of 12.5% after incomplete inpatient therapy, 7.2% after complete inpatient therapy, and 4.6% when outpatient treatment was added; the overall three-group test was significant, but complete inpatient therapy versus added outpatient therapy alone was only borderline (chi-square 3.4; 0.05<p<0.10).

Central interpretive constraint: The strongest contemporary-use limitation is not simply age. The observational treatment-duration analysis had differential follow-up and nonrandom treatment selection, and the randomized “intensity” experiment concerns Quick prothrombin activity rather than a heparin assay. The paper is therefore poor direct evidence for modern heparin dosing or monitoring targets.

Section-by-section condensation

Background and study purpose

The University of Michigan developed a long-standing interdepartmental anticoagulation service after World War II and retained standardized records for diagnosis, therapy, hemorrhage, thromboembolic complications, and autopsy findings. The report focuses on morbidity and mortality from thromboembolic complications during and after anticoagulant treatment of lower-extremity DVT and PE over 1945-1964.

The paper is both a large descriptive treatment experience and a vehicle for two clinical hypotheses: whether greater oral-anticoagulation intensity reduces recurrence and whether longer treatment reduces early post-discharge events.

Methods of Analysis

The investigators excluded superficial thrombosis of the arms or legs, neoplasm-related venous obstruction, and upper-extremity DVT. Recommended therapy was initial heparinization until Quick prothrombin activity reached therapeutic levels plus oral anticoagulants for at least 7 days; mean in-hospital anticoagulant treatment was 16±11 days. PE diagnosed before treatment was analyzed separately from DVT because the authors considered those patients at higher embolic risk.

Any compatible new thromboembolic sign or symptom after the first anticoagulant dose was counted as a complication. Five terminal patients with lethal PE at presentation were excluded from mortality analyses. Follow-up was incomplete and differential: 1,735 episodes had some follow-up, 1,371 had at least 12 weeks, and 12-week follow-up was more complete among courses with complications (88%) than among courses without them (63%). The authors explicitly recognized this as a source of bias and used assumptions to estimate post-treatment event frequency.

Results — in-hospital recurrence and anticoagulation intensity

During hospital treatment, recurrent PE was more hazardous among patients who entered with PE than among those who entered with DVT, especially for fatal recurrence (1.3% versus 0.14%). The authors found no detectable effect of initial heparin use on complication frequency, but most heparin-treated episodes had short exposure: 906/1,289 received heparin for 48 hours or less, limiting interpretability.

The prospective intensity comparison used odd/even registration-number allocation to Quick targets of 10-29% or 30-49%. In presenting PE, complications were lower at the more intensive target (3.0% versus 7.4%), but the reported p-value range crossed 0.05. In presenting DVT, complication rates were essentially identical. The authors nevertheless preferred Quick activity below 30%, especially for PE.

Results — bleeding and post-discharge recurrence

Major bleeding requiring transfusion or treatment termination occurred in 2.4% of episodes, with about half associated with heparin. Minor bleeding occurred in 5.5%, with about one quarter attributed to heparin. Within 12 weeks after hospital treatment, PE recurrence was 5.5% after an initial PE diagnosis and 1.8% after initial DVT; DVT recurrence was 2.6% and 4.6%, respectively.

Events were concentrated early after treatment: 45% of the 12-week complications occurred in the first 2 weeks and another 25% in the next 2 weeks. Eleven embolic deaths occurred after treatment, with 9 within 12 weeks. The authors had therefore extended oral anticoagulation after discharge when feasible.

Results — treatment duration and late events

Table 5 compared incomplete inpatient treatment, complete inpatient treatment, and complete inpatient plus outpatient treatment. The 12-week complication rates were 12.5%, 7.2%, and 4.6%, respectively. The overall three-group chi-square was 8.0 (p<0.025), whereas the direct complete-inpatient versus added-outpatient comparison was chi-square 3.4 with 0.05<p<0.10. Treatment continuation was not randomized; patients were selected by feasibility of safe outpatient management.

After 12 weeks, recurrent events continued but were less tightly linked to the original treatment period. The authors noted that many late events followed later hospitalization or immobilization and that few patients remained on outpatient anticoagulation beyond 3 months, limiting attribution of late outcomes to treatment duration.

Discussion and Summary

The authors emphasize major ascertainment problems: clinically silent PE, missed PE at routine autopsy, and delayed symptoms of pulmonary infarction could both undercount and overcount treatment-emergent events. They considered fatal PE a more reliable efficacy measure than nonfatal recurrence. They compared their observed fatal recurrence rates favorably with historical untreated series and argued that anticoagulation was the treatment of choice.

The final recommendations were to favor more intensive Quick prothrombin activity targets below 30% for PE, continue anticoagulation for several months or more to reduce early recurrence, and reserve operative management for contraindication to anticoagulation or recurrent thromboembolism despite adequate therapy.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High historical relevance for the evolution of VTE anticoagulation, especially recurrence timing, safety, and the transition toward longer treatment. Direct evidentiary value for heparin therapeutic-range selection is limited because the intensity experiment used Quick prothrombin activity.

Clinical question / PICO

Population: patients with lower-extremity DVT or PE treated at a single academic center. Intervention/exposures: initial heparin plus oral anticoagulation; different Quick prothrombin activity targets; differing treatment duration. Comparators: higher versus lower Quick activity target, incomplete versus complete inpatient therapy, and addition of outpatient anticoagulation. Outcomes: recurrent PE/DVT, fatal PE, timing, and bleeding.

Relevance 9/10

Rationale: The paper is central to the historical development of anticoagulation for VTE and contains explicit heparin exposure and bleeding data.

Evidence basis: Large VTE series, recurrence timing, heparin use, oral-anticoagulation intensity, and treatment-duration analyses.

Principal limit: The randomized intensity target is not a heparin assay or heparin therapeutic range.

Would change with: A study directly linking heparin dose/assay targets to VTE recurrence and bleeding.

Novelty 8/10

Rationale: For its era, the combination of a large longitudinal treatment registry and a prospective intensity comparison was distinctive.

Evidence basis: The authors describe a multi-decade institutional database and an 8.5-year randomized intensity experiment.

Principal limit: Historical novelty does not equal present-day clinical validity.

Would change with: Contemporaneous multicenter replication or stronger randomized methodology.

Method strength 4/10

Rationale: Descriptive event counts are useful, but causal comparisons are limited by follow-up bias, diagnostic misclassification, nonrandom duration selection, and sparse statistical reporting.

Evidence basis: The paper explicitly documents differential follow-up; Table 5 is observational; Table 3 uses odd/even registration-number allocation.

Principal limit: No allocation concealment/blinding details, no modern adjudication, and no confidence intervals.

Would change with: Prospective blinded adjudication, complete follow-up, concealed randomization, and prespecified analysis.

Evidence strength 4/10

Rationale: The source supports historical associations and event frequencies more strongly than treatment-effect claims.

Evidence basis: Large denominators and internally tabulated counts, but key causal claims rely on borderline or nonrandom comparisons.

Principal limit: Most comparisons lack effect sizes with uncertainty; the PE intensity result did not cross a conventional 0.05 threshold.

Would change with: Well-powered randomized estimates with confidence intervals and consistent replication.

External validity 3/10

Rationale: The cohort is single-center and spans 1945-1964 diagnostic and treatment practices.

Evidence basis: All patients came from one institutional anticoagulation service; management and ascertainment were historically specific.

Principal limit: Substantial changes in VTE diagnosis, anticoagulant selection, dosing, monitoring, and care settings limit transportability.

Would change with: Validation in contemporary diverse centers using current diagnostic and treatment pathways.

Implementation value 4/10

Rationale: The paper is useful for historical rationale and hypothesis generation, not for operational dosing rules.

Evidence basis: It highlights early recurrence, bleeding burden, treatment duration, and the distinction between PE and DVT risk.

Principal limit: It does not provide a weight-based heparin regimen, aPTT/anti-Xa target, assay calibration, or contemporary duration framework.

Would change with: A directly applicable protocol with validated heparin monitoring targets and modern clinical outcomes.

Strengths

  • Large longitudinal clinical experience with explicit denominators and fatal-event reporting.
  • Separates patients presenting with PE from those presenting with DVT.
  • Reports bleeding as well as thromboembolic recurrence.
  • Explicitly acknowledges follow-up and diagnostic ascertainment limitations.
  • Contains a prospectively assigned intensity comparison rather than only retrospective observation.

Limitations

  • Single-center historical cohort and episode-level analyses; repeated episodes may not be independent.
  • Differential follow-up can inflate or distort post-treatment recurrence estimates.
  • Clinical PE ascertainment was difficult, and delayed symptoms could misclassify pre-treatment emboli as treatment-emergent complications.
  • Table 5 treatment-duration groups were not randomized and were selected partly by feasibility of safe outpatient management.
  • The randomized intensity result in PE was borderline by conventional significance thresholds and the DVT result was null.
  • Quick prothrombin activity is not a direct measure of heparin intensity.

Practice translation

Do not use this paper alone to set current heparin doses, aPTT/anti-Xa targets, or treatment duration. The paper supports historical concepts—anticoagulation reduces recurrence risk, early post-treatment recurrence matters, and bleeding is a major counterweight—but it does not supply a contemporary heparin dosing or monitoring contract.

Implementation and research takeaways

For the heparin deep dive
Classify this as historical context rather than direct therapeutic-range evidence. The key randomized target is Quick prothrombin activity.
For evidence graphs
Keep distinct nodes for in-hospital recurrence, early post-discharge recurrence, bleeding, anticoagulation intensity, and treatment duration. Do not merge Table 3 and Table 5 into one causal claim.
For clinical interpretation
The strongest descriptive signal is recurrence timing and the higher fatal recurrent-PE risk in patients presenting with PE.
For appraisal
Preserve the authors’ own recognition of follow-up bias and diagnostic misclassification; these directly constrain treatment-effect inference.
