One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Rifampicin produced a large, consistent reduction in minocycline exposure in this small within-patient NTM cohort. The AUC0-24h GMR was 49.0% (90% CI 44.1-54.4), with Cmax 67.2% and Ctrough 30.9%; all CIs were below the prespecified 80%-125% no-interaction range.

The design is useful for a PK drug-drug interaction question because each participant serves as their own control, limiting between-person PK variability. Dense 24-hour minocycline sampling at steady state and non-compartmental analysis provide direct exposure estimates. The central signal is internally coherent: AUC fell in all nine participants completing both periods, apparent clearance increased from 3.4 to 6.9 L/h, and half-life shortened from 13.3 to 7.8 hours.

The study is not sufficient to tell clinicians how to compensate for the interaction. Only nine participants contributed to the paired interaction analysis; the fixed-order design permits time-dependent confounding; the rifampicin AUC limited-sampling equation was not validated in NTM; and no clinical efficacy endpoint established what minocycline exposure is required in MAC pulmonary disease. The authors' exploratory PK/PD ratios rely on preclinical targets and an assumed 80% protein binding value.

Methods

Clinical question / PICO

P: Adults with NTM disease eligible for rifampicin-based treatment. I/exposure: Minocycline 200 mg once daily at steady state during weight-based rifampicin therapy. C: The same participants receiving minocycline before rifampicin. O: Minocycline AUC0-24h, Cmax, Ctrough and other PK parameters; adverse events as a secondary safety description.

Study sequence

Two 5-day minocycline courses were given in fixed order. Course I was completed before rifampicin; course II started 1 month (±1 week) after rifampicin initiation. On day 5 of each course, minocycline was dosed after an overnight fast and sampled at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 24 hours. Rifampicin samples at 2, 4 and 6 hours were obtained during course II.

Analysis

Non-compartmental analysis estimated AUC0-24h, Cmax, Tmax, Ctrough, Vd/F, Cl/F and T1/2. Rifampicin AUC0-24h was estimated using a limited-sampling equation derived in tuberculosis. A linear mixed-effects analysis calculated within-study GMRs (with versus without rifampicin), with 80%-125% as the prespecified no-interaction bioequivalence range. Spearman correlations explored whether rifampicin exposure explained the magnitude of the minocycline interaction.

Results

Population

Twelve participants enrolled; one withdrew before completing course I and two withdrew after course I, leaving 11 for the no-rifampicin PK description and nine for the paired DDI analysis. Among course-I completers, median age was 61 years (IQR 52-67), 4/11 were female, median weight was 62.8 kg, 10/11 had pulmonary NTM disease, and all were Caucasian.

AUC0-24h decreased in all nine paired participants; Cmax decreased in eight of nine. Rifampicin geometric mean AUC0-24h was 53.8 mg·h/L (95% CI 33.9-85.4) and Cmax 12.3 mg/L (8.4-18.1). Rifampicin AUC was not significantly correlated with the minocycline AUC, Cmax, or Ctrough ratios.

Safety

Sixty-nine adverse events occurred in 12 participants, all CTCAE grade I or II, with no serious adverse events. Nine events (13%) were considered probably related to minocycline, and one participant discontinued minocycline because of an adverse event. The short exposure periods and small cohort preclude conclusions about long-term tolerability.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the roughly 51% AUC reduction as a pharmacokinetic interaction likely driven by rifampicin induction of drug-metabolizing enzymes and/or transporters. That interpretation is mechanistically plausible within the source's framing, and the accompanying increase in apparent clearance and shortened half-life support an induction-type effect. However, this study does not identify the responsible pathway.

Clinical translation remains uncertain. The authors calculate exploratory free AUC0-24h/MIC ratios of 1.5 without rifampicin and 0.7 with rifampicin using an MIC50 of 8 mg/L and 80% protein binding, but explicitly note that the cited PK/PD targets came from preclinical minocycline monotherapy and have not been clinically validated. Their suggestion to evaluate 200 mg twice daily is a future-study proposal, not a validated dosing recommendation.

  • Small cohort: 11 first-period and 9 paired PK participants.
  • Fixed-order design permits disease/treatment changes between periods to confound PK.
  • Single center; all course-I completers were Caucasian; cohort largely older adults with advanced pulmonary disease.
  • Rifampicin AUC limited-sampling model was not validated in NTM disease.
  • Short courses cannot establish chronic safety/tolerability.
  • No clinical efficacy endpoint connects the PK reduction to microbiologic or patient-centered outcomes.

Bottom line

Use now: treat rifampicin as a clinically important potential reducer of minocycline exposure when interpreting therapy, PK data, or designing studies. Do not use now: automatically double minocycline dosing or infer treatment failure from the PK data alone. Confidence in appraisal: high for the PK interaction signal; moderate-to-low for clinical translation.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What changes conceptually

Rifampicin should not be assumed PK-neutral with minocycline; exposure may fall by roughly half.

What remains unknown

The exposure needed for MAC efficacy, the best dose adjustment, and chronic safety of higher dosing with rifampicin.

What to monitor in future studies

Steady-state AUC, Cmax/Ctrough, MIC-linked PK/PD, tolerability, and microbiologic outcomes under candidate dose-adjustment strategies.

Safety boundary

200 mg twice daily appears only as a proposed dose to evaluate; the article does not establish it as a recommended regimen.
