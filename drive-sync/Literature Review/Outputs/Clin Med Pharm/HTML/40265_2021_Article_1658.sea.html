One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Cluster headache pharmacotherapy is divided into rapid abortive treatment, short transitional therapy while prevention becomes effective, and longer-term prevention. Acute evidence is strongest for high-flow oxygen and triptans; transitional evidence favors corticosteroids or occipital steroid injection; preventive evidence is strongest for verapamil and lithium, while many alternatives rest on small open studies or negative/limited randomized trials.

For acute attacks, the review emphasizes 100% oxygen and rapidly absorbed triptans because attacks last only 15-180 minutes. Subcutaneous sumatriptan has the most persuasive rapid-response data summarized in the article: one cited 39-patient trial reported 74% response at 15 minutes versus 26% with placebo, and a Cochrane pooled analysis reported an NNT of 3.3 for pain freedom at 15 minutes. Intranasal sumatriptan and zolmitriptan are slower but supported by placebo-controlled trials. Oxygen is presented as effective, typically at at least 12 L/min using a non-rebreather mask.

For transitional therapy, prednisone and greater-occipital-nerve corticosteroid injections are used to suppress attacks while preventive therapy is titrated. A cited multicenter prednisone trial reported 7.1 versus 9.5 attacks in the first week; an occipital steroid trial reported 85% attack freedom versus 0% with placebo. For prevention, verapamil is the preferred option in the review and requires ECG surveillance during titration; lithium is second-line with serum, renal, and thyroid monitoring. Galcanezumab showed benefit in episodic but not chronic cluster headache in cited trials. Evidence for topiramate, gabapentin, melatonin, ketamine, and onabotulinumtoxinA is substantially weaker and often open-label.

Section condensation

1. Introduction

The review defines episodic and chronic cluster headache and frames treatment as three linked tasks: aborting the individual attack, bridging until preventive therapy becomes effective, and maintaining prevention during the active period. The authors explicitly note that preventive evidence is sparse and that many recommendations rely on open observational studies.

2. Treatment of cluster attacks

Oral medication is discouraged because individual attacks are short. Subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg is presented as the most effective acute drug. In a cited 39-patient randomized trial, 15-minute response was 74% with sumatriptan versus 26% with placebo; pain freedom at 10 minutes was 36% versus 3%. A cited Cochrane analysis reported pain freedom at 15 minutes in 48% versus 17%, NNT 3.3 (95% CI 2.4-5.0).

Intranasal sumatriptan 20 mg produced 30-minute response rates of 57% versus 26% and pain-free rates of 47% versus 18% in a cited 118-patient trial. Intranasal zolmitriptan trials showed dose-responsive improvement, with a cited meta-analysis reporting 30-minute relief in 63% with 10 mg, 48% with 5 mg, and 30% with placebo. Dihydroergotamine evidence is older and less rigorous; the C213 microneedle program had no public results at the time of the review.

3. Oxygen treatment

The review recommends 100% oxygen at at least 12 L/min, sometimes up to 15 L/min, generally for about 20 minutes through a non-rebreather mask. A cited Cochrane review included 145 patients in quantitative analysis and reported a 75% responder rate at 15 minutes. The authors also describe a clinical pattern in which oxygen may appear to truncate rather than terminate attacks in some patients, leading to recurrent attacks within an hour.

4. Bridging or transitional therapy

Corticosteroids are used short term because preventive drugs require slow titration. The largest cited prednisone study randomized patients with a new episodic bout to prednisone 100 mg for 5 days followed by taper or placebo while both groups received escalating verapamil; among 109 analyzed patients, mean first-week attacks were 7.1 versus 9.5. Adverse events occurred in 71% of both groups.

Occipital steroid injection evidence is also summarized. In one cited placebo-controlled study, 11 of 13 corticosteroid-treated patients became attack-free in the first week versus 0 of 10 on placebo. A separate cortivazol trial reported 20/21 versus 12/22 patients with two or fewer daily attacks after treatment, OR 14.5 (95% CI 1.8-116.9; p=0.012), with a very wide interval indicating substantial imprecision.

5. Prevention of cluster headache

Verapamil. The review calls verapamil the medication of choice. A cited 30-patient randomized trial found significant reduction in attack frequency and abortive-medication need. Open studies reported high response rates, including 94% complete relief in episodic and 55% in chronic cluster headache in one cohort; a pair-wise meta-analysis of open studies reported 87% complete response or greater than 50% attack reduction. Clinical use is described as starting 40-80 mg three times daily, sometimes titrating to 960 mg/day, with baseline ECG and repeat ECG after 160-mg increments.

Lithium and alternatives. A cited meta-analysis of three open lithium trials (103 patients) reported 77% complete response or greater than 50% reduction; the review recommends serum, renal, and thyroid monitoring with lithium concentrations 0.4-0.8 mEq/L. Topiramate, gabapentin, and melatonin are supported mainly by small or open studies. Sodium valproate failed to separate from placebo in a 96-patient randomized trial. Galcanezumab reduced attack frequency by 52% versus 27% with placebo in a 106-patient episodic trial but did not significantly outperform placebo in a 237-patient chronic trial.

6. Future therapy

Ketamine, pasireotide, onabotulinumtoxinA, serotonergic hallucinogens, and sodium oxybate are described as investigational or weakly supported. In an open 17-patient ketamine-magnesium study, daily attacks decreased from 4.3 to 1.3, mean difference -3.1 (95% CI -4.5 to -1.6; p<0.001), but the design lacked placebo control and mild sedation occurred in 41.2%. A pasireotide phase II trial was stopped after 28 patients for lack of efficacy. An open onabotulinumtoxinA trial reported a greater than 50% reduction in cumulative headache minutes in 59% of 17 participants.

7. Conclusions and declarations

The authors conclude that approved acute options include oxygen and subcutaneous/intranasal triptans, while preventive therapy remains more difficult because few placebo-controlled trials exist and established agents have important adverse effects. They highlight spontaneous ending of episodic bouts and high placebo response as major trial-design problems. The article reports Projekt DEAL open-access funding; Diener reports honoraria from several pharmaceutical companies, and May reports no conflicts of interest.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a compact pharmacotherapy orientation and historical evidence map. Do not use it as the sole current practice standard because it is a narrative review with no reproducible evidence-synthesis methods and its clinical currency is anchored to 2021.
Relevance: 9/10Directly addresses acute, transitional, preventive, monitoring, and safety decisions. Limit: narrow disease state. Would rise: guideline-level implementation detail.
Novelty: 5/10Primarily consolidates existing therapies and trials rather than introducing a new method or intervention. Would rise: new comparative synthesis or formal meta-analysis.
Method strength: 4/10Clinical claims cite controlled trials and reviews, but the article itself does not describe a systematic search, study-selection protocol, or risk-of-bias framework. Would rise: preregistered systematic methods.
Evidence strength: 6/10Acute triptan/oxygen and some bridging claims rest on randomized evidence, but preventive claims are heterogeneous and often open-label. Would rise: larger replicated randomized trials with patient-centered outcomes.
External validity: 6/10Evidence spans episodic and chronic cluster headache, but many cited studies are small and treatment access/cost varies. Would rise: contemporary multicenter pragmatic studies.
Implementation value: 8/10Provides practical dosing, monitoring, adverse-effect, and contraindication summaries. Limit: not a current guideline and some table entries require contemporary verification.

Main strengths

  • Clear organization by acute, bridging, preventive, and investigational therapy.
  • Preserves useful treatment magnitudes and pragmatic monitoring details.
  • Explicitly acknowledges weak preventive evidence and trial-design problems.

Main limitations

  • Narrative review methodology is not reproducible; literature search and selection procedures are not reported.
  • No formal study-level risk-of-bias or certainty assessment.
  • Many preventive alternatives are supported by open studies, case series, or small trials.
  • Evidence from cited primary studies is secondarily reported; primary publications remain necessary for high-stakes quantitative reuse.
  • Practice currency was not externally verified in this packet-only production workflow.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Acute attackThe source favors oxygen and rapid triptan delivery over oral agents because attacks are short.
TransitionShort corticosteroid courses or occipital steroid injections can bridge the delay while preventive therapy is titrated.
PreventionVerapamil has the strongest practical role in this review; ECG monitoring during dose escalation is a load-bearing safety requirement.
LithiumRequires serum concentration, kidney, and thyroid monitoring; tolerability and interaction burden are important.
AlternativesTopiramate, gabapentin, melatonin, and investigational therapies have materially weaker evidence and should not inherit the certainty of the better-supported acute therapies.
