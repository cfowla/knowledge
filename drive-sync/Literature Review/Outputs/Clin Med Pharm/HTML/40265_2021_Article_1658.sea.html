One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Acute cluster attacks are best treated with high-flow 100% oxygen or rapidly delivered triptans; corticosteroids or greater occipital nerve steroid blockade can bridge the delay to preventive therapy; verapamil has the strongest preventive evidence, lithium is the principal alternative, and most other preventives rest on smaller or uncontrolled evidence.

The review is clinically efficient because it links evidence magnitude to concrete dosing, adverse effects, contraindications, and monitoring. Its strongest sections are acute sumatriptan, oxygen, corticosteroid transition therapy, and the comparative discussion of preventive evidence. The preventive evidence base is explicitly weaker: many data come from open series, small cohorts, or case reports, and the authors themselves emphasize the need for placebo-controlled trials.

The main appraisal limitation is the review method: it does not report a reproducible search strategy, selection process, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or evidence-certainty framework. Section condensation

Introduction

The review defines cluster headache as a trigeminal autonomic headache with strictly unilateral severe orbital/retro-orbital/frontotemporal pain and ipsilateral cranial autonomic symptoms. Attacks last 15 minutes to 3 hours and can occur from once every 2 days to eight times daily. Episodic disease is described as about 90% of cases, with chronic disease about 10%.

The authors frame management as three linked tasks: abort the individual attack, provide rapid transitional therapy while a preventive is being titrated, and maintain preventive treatment. They explicitly acknowledge that preventive evidence is sparse and often based on open observational studies rather than placebo-controlled trials.

Treatment of cluster attacks

Because attacks are short, the review discourages oral acute medication. The main acute options are subcutaneous sumatriptan, intranasal sumatriptan or zolmitriptan, and high-flow oxygen. Subcutaneous sumatriptan has the fastest and strongest trial evidence in the review: 6 mg produced 15-minute relief in 74% versus 26% with placebo in one trial, and pooled pain-free rates at 15 minutes were 48% versus 17%, NNT 3.3 (95% CI 2.4–5.0).

Intranasal sumatriptan 20 mg and zolmitriptan 5–10 mg are slower but useful alternatives. The review reports 30-minute pain-free rates of 47% versus 18% for intranasal sumatriptan and placebo, and zolmitriptan meta-analytic relief rates of 63% (10 mg), 48% (5 mg), and 30% (placebo). Ergot derivatives are presented as less well supported, and a microneedle zolmitriptan system had no public results at the time of publication.

Oxygen treatment

The review recommends 100% oxygen at at least 12 L/min, sometimes up to 15 L/min, for about 20 minutes through a non-rebreather mask; nasal cannulae are described as generally insufficient. A 2015 Cochrane synthesis cited by the review included 145 patients in three quantitative trials and reported a 75% responder rate after 15 minutes.

The authors add an experience-based caution that some patients may experience recurrence within an hour after oxygen and may perceive more frequent short attacks. That observation is explicitly clinical experience rather than controlled evidence and should not be weighted like the randomized oxygen data.

Bridging or transitional therapy

The review uses corticosteroids as the main bridge while long-term prophylaxis is titrated, delivered orally, intravenously, or as greater occipital nerve injections. In the largest prednisone trial, 109 analyzed patients had 7.1 attacks in week 1 with prednisone versus 9.5 with placebo while verapamil was titrated; adverse events occurred in 71% of both groups.

Greater occipital nerve steroid trials are summarized as strongly positive but small. One study reported 11/13 (85%) attack-free in week 1 versus 0/10 with saline. Another 43-patient trial reported 20/21 versus 12/22 achieving a mean of two or fewer daily attacks after cortivazol injections (OR 14.5, 95% CI 1.8–116.9), with the very wide interval signaling substantial imprecision.

Prevention of cluster headache

Verapamil is presented as first choice and lithium as second choice. A 30-patient placebo-controlled verapamil trial showed significant reductions in attack frequency and abortive-treatment use; open series and meta-analysis reported high response rates, but the review also stresses ECG monitoring because of conduction risk. Lithium evidence is mainly open-label; a meta-analysis of 103 patients reported 77% complete or >50% response, while a placebo-controlled episodic trial was negative.

Topiramate, gabapentin, and melatonin are presented as possible alternatives with weaker evidence. Sodium valproate failed to separate from placebo in a 96-patient trial. Galcanezumab is the key newer preventive: the review reports a 52% versus 27% reduction in attack frequency over 3 weeks in episodic cluster headache (n=106), but no significant benefit in chronic cluster headache (n=237).

Future therapy

Ketamine, pasireotide, onabotulinumtoxinA, indoleamine hallucinogens, and sodium oxybate are described as investigational. The strongest numerical signal in this section is an uncontrolled 17-patient ketamine-magnesium series in which daily attacks fell from 4.3 to 1.3, mean difference −3.1 (95% CI −4.5 to −1.6), with 13 responders and mild sedation in 41.2%.

The section appropriately flags the lack of placebo control for ketamine and its addiction potential. Pasireotide was stopped after 28 patients for lack of efficacy. OnabotulinumtoxinA data were open-label and inconsistent. Survey and case-report evidence for hallucinogens and sodium oxybate is hypothesis-generating only.

Conclusions

The authors conclude that acute therapy is concentrated in oxygen and triptans, while preventive therapy remains more problematic because of sparse placebo-controlled evidence and tolerability limits. They cite Danish real-world data showing low use and variable response to subcutaneous sumatriptan and only 20% reported response to verapamil prophylaxis.

The paper ends with a methodological point that matters for future trials: episodic bouts may end spontaneously and placebo response can be substantial, making uncontrolled improvement difficult to interpret. The authors therefore call for better fast-acting acute treatments and effective, well-tolerated preventive options supported by randomized trials.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best use: a compact map of pharmacologic options and the evidence landscape as of 2021. Do not use as the sole source for current dosing, regulatory status, or comprehensive management.
Relevance — 8/10

Highly relevant to cluster-headache pharmacotherapy and practical dosing/monitoring.

Evidence basis: Broad coverage of acute, bridge, and preventive options with treatment tables.

Limiting factor: The article is disease-specific and dated.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with explicit alignment to current guidelines and newer therapies.

Novelty — 5/10

Useful synthesis rather than a novel experiment or systematic evidence synthesis.

Evidence basis: Integrates controlled and open-label evidence available through 2021.

Limiting factor: Most treatment principles predate the review.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with new comparative evidence or a formal evidence synthesis.

Method strength — 4/10

Narrative review methods are not described in enough detail for reproducibility.

Evidence basis: The article cites controlled trials, meta-analyses, open studies, and case series.

Limiting factor: No search strategy, selection criteria, risk-of-bias process, or formal certainty framework is reported.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with prespecified systematic-review methods and duplicate appraisal.

Evidence strength — 6/10

Core acute treatments are backed by randomized evidence, while much preventive evidence is small or uncontrolled.

Evidence basis: The review preserves trial sizes, response rates, placebo comparisons, and some confidence intervals.

Limiting factor: Evidence quality is heterogeneous and review-level grading is informal.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with larger controlled prevention trials and formal GRADE-style synthesis.

External validity — 6/10

The treatments address both episodic and chronic cluster headache but many cited studies are small and highly selected.

Evidence basis: The review distinguishes episodic versus chronic responses and includes real-world data.

Limiting factor: Small samples and open series limit generalizability, especially for refractory disease.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with multicenter pragmatic studies and broader patient populations.

Implementation value — 8/10

The review provides concrete doses, contraindications, monitoring, and bridge/preventive sequencing.

Evidence basis: Three treatment tables and narrative dosing instructions are directly reusable.

Limiting factor: At least one dose in Table 3 is outdated/inconsistent with later guidance, and current-practice verification is needed.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with corrected dosing and explicit current guideline concordance.

Main strengths

  • Clear separation of acute, transitional, preventive, and investigational therapies.
  • Preserves clinically useful doses, monitoring, contraindications, sample sizes, response rates, p values, and confidence intervals.
  • Distinguishes episodic from chronic cluster headache where treatment response differs.
  • Explicitly acknowledges the weakness of much preventive evidence.

Main limitations

  • No reported systematic search strategy, eligibility criteria, study-selection flow, risk-of-bias process, or formal certainty grading.
  • Evidence is heterogeneous: randomized trials are mixed with open series, surveys, and case reports.
  • Some recommendations rely partly on author clinical experience.
  • Published in 2021/2022; current-practice details require later guideline verification.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the source’s structure and reported evidence; moderate for current-practice translation because the source predates later guidance.

Currency verification — external, not source-derived

Later guideline concordance: The 2023 European Academy of Neurology guideline broadly confirms the review’s core acute and preventive hierarchy: 100% oxygen at at least 12 L/min over 15 minutes and 6 mg subcutaneous sumatriptan for attacks; verapamil for prevention; corticosteroids for transitional treatment; and lithium, topiramate, and galcanezumab (episodic only) as alternatives.
Dosing discrepancy: The 2023 EAN guideline specifies galcanezumab 300 mg subcutaneously monthly for episodic cluster headache, whereas this review’s Table 3 lists 120 mg monthly. Treat the review table as outdated/inconsistent for that dose.

A 2025 clinical-practice review focused on transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation also reflects expansion of nonpharmacologic options. This does not contradict the source because neurostimulation was explicitly outside the review’s scope, but it reinforces that the 2021 article is not comprehensive for present-day overall cluster-headache management.

External sources: European Academy of Neurology guideline, Eur J Neurol. 2023;30:2955–2979, DOI 10.1111/ene.15956; 2025 Pain Practice clinical-practice guideline on non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (PubMed PMID 41133770).

Implementation / practice takeaways

Acute
Prioritize rapid-delivery treatments: oxygen and subcutaneous sumatriptan have the strongest evidence in the review; intranasal triptans are useful slower alternatives.
Bridge
Use short-course corticosteroids or greater occipital nerve steroid blockade to cover the latency of preventive therapy; avoid chronic corticosteroid exposure.
Preventive
Verapamil is the evidence anchor, with ECG monitoring as doses rise. Lithium is a second-line option requiring level, renal, and thyroid monitoring.
Weaker evidence
Topiramate, gabapentin, melatonin, and other agents are supported mainly by smaller or uncontrolled studies; sodium valproate did not separate from placebo in the cited randomized trial.
CGRP
Galcanezumab has positive episodic but negative chronic cluster-headache evidence in the review. Verify current dose and labeling before use.
Research
Interpret uncontrolled improvements cautiously because episodic bouts can end spontaneously and placebo response is substantial.
