2. One-page synthesis and clinical bottom line

Verdict: Read first; practice-changing caution against routine bicarbonate for this population. In adults in the ICU receiving vasopressors with metabolic acidosis defined by pH <7.30, base excess ≤ −4 mmol/L, and non-hypercapnic physiology, a 5-hour protocolized sodium bicarbonate infusion corrected acid-base variables but did not reduce the primary patient-centered composite of death, renal-replacement therapy, or persistent renal dysfunction by 30 days.

What it changes

Do not treat pH <7.30 plus vasopressor use as an automatic indication for IV sodium bicarbonate to improve renal outcomes, mortality, or vasopressor-free days.

What it preserves

Bicarbonate may still be used as a short-term physiologic intervention for selected severe acidemia, hyperkalemia/alkalinization contexts, or established nontrial indications. This trial does not address toxicology, DKA, major bicarbonate losses, or urgent RRT indications.

Key risk

A normalizing pH may create a false sense that the kidney or shock state improved. In this trial, pH correction did not translate into fewer major adverse kidney events or improved survival.

3. PICO and clinical question

Population

Adults ≥18 years in ICU receiving continuous vasopressor infusion for hypotension and meeting all metabolic acidosis criteria within 2 hours before randomization: pH <7.30, base excess ≤ −4 mmol/L, and PaCO2 ≤45 mm Hg if not intubated or ≤50 mm Hg if intubated.

Intervention

IV sodium bicarbonate diluted in 5% dextrose to 600 mmol/L, started within 1 hour, infused for up to 5 hours using pH/base-excess stopping rules. Median total dose: 300 mmol.

Comparator

Placebo 5% dextrose, matching protocol and adjustment algorithm, with clinicians and patients blinded. Open-label bicarbonate discouraged but allowed at clinician discretion after protocolized infusion.

Outcome

Primary: major adverse kidney event within 30 days, defined as death from any cause, receipt of renal-replacement therapy, or persistent renal dysfunction. Secondary outcomes included mortality, RRT use/dependence, acidemia recurrence, AKI, and organ-support-free days.

Clinical question: In vasopressor-dependent critically ill adults with metabolic acidosis that is not primarily hypercapnic, does early short-course IV sodium bicarbonate improve kidney-centered and mortality outcomes compared with placebo?

5. Population, exclusions, and external validity

Included population

Enrolled patients were high-risk but not uniformly profoundly acidemic: median pH was 7.26, median base excess about −9 mmol/L, median lactate 2.5 mmol/L, median APACHE II score 21, median SOFA score 7, and approximately 46% had acute kidney injury at baseline. Approximately two thirds had sepsis and about 40% had septic shock. About 80% were mechanically ventilated.

Key exclusions that matter for bedside translation

Renal exclusions

eGFR <30 mL/min due to chronic kidney disease; current or planned renal-replacement therapy.

Acidosis-etiology exclusions

Diabetic ketoacidosis, major bicarbonate losses, known or suspected poisoning, and patients already receiving sodium bicarbonate at screening.

Safety exclusions

High cerebral edema risk/acute brain injury or intracranial hypertension risk, pulmonary edema with PaO2:FiO2 <100, hypocalcemia, severe dysnatremia, hypokalemia, pregnancy/breastfeeding, imminent death, and life expectancy <30 days.

Do not extrapolate safety too broadly. Patients with several classic bicarbonate-risk features were excluded. Low adverse-event frequency in SODa-BIC should not be interpreted as proof of safety in severe hypokalemia, severe dysnatremia, pulmonary edema/ARDS-level oxygenation impairment, intracranial pathology, or advanced CKD.

Monitoring checklist for any local use

  • Before order verification: confirm indication, pH, base excess/bicarbonate, PaCO2, lactate, potassium, sodium, ionized or corrected calcium when relevant, volume status, oxygenation, AKI/RRT status, suspected toxin/DKA/bicarbonate-loss context, and goals of care.
  • During therapy: repeat ABG/VBG per ICU protocol; check sodium, potassium, bicarbonate/CO2, calcium, glucose if D5W carrier used, fluid balance, ventilatory adequacy/CO2 clearance, and vasopressor trajectory.
  • Stop/reassess triggers: pH above target, base excess corrected, rising sodium/osmolality, worsening hypokalemia, alkalemia, volume overload, ionized hypocalcemia concerns, inadequate ventilation for CO2 generation, or decision to initiate RRT.

Contraindications and interaction considerations

Contraindications/cautions from trial exclusions and pharmacology: severe dysnatremia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, pulmonary edema or severe oxygenation impairment, acute brain injury/cerebral edema risk, current/planned RRT, advanced CKD outside trial criteria, and unclear cause of acidosis where definitive therapy should not be delayed.

Drug interaction frame: sodium bicarbonate has no CYP-mediated interaction issue, but it materially changes sodium load, extracellular pH, and urinary alkalinization. Use caution with concurrent sodium loads such as hypertonic saline, large-volume sodium chloride, citrate-containing blood products/CRRT anticoagulation, and other alkalinizing agents. It may worsen hypokalemia; it can alter weak-acid/weak-base renal elimination in toxicology contexts, but those contexts were outside this trial.

Renal/hepatic considerations: no hepatic dose adjustment is relevant. Renal impairment is not a dose-adjustment issue so much as an indication/risk/context issue. SODa-BIC excluded chronic eGFR <30 mL/min and current/planned RRT, so it cannot validate dosing or safety in those groups.

7. Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The trial tested whether sodium bicarbonate improves kidney-centered outcomes in critically ill adults with metabolic acidosis receiving vasopressors. Adults with pH <7.30, base excess ≤ −4 mmol/L, and limited PaCO2 were randomized to sodium bicarbonate or 5% dextrose placebo; infusion was titrated for up to 5 hours to pH ≥7.30 and base excess ≥0 mmol/L.

Among 498 analyzed patients, MAKE30 occurred in 40.2% with bicarbonate vs 39.4% with placebo (adjusted difference +1.2 percentage points; 95% CI −7.1 to +9.4; P=0.78). RRT use was numerically lower with bicarbonate (16.8% vs 20.9%) but not definitive; day-30 in-hospital mortality was similar (25.4% vs 24.0%). Adverse effects were uncommon but occurred only in the bicarbonate group.

Background

The paper frames metabolic acidosis as common in ICU shock states and associated with impaired cardiac contractility, reduced catecholamine responsiveness, AKI, RRT, and death. Prior bicarbonate evidence suggested possible reduction in RRT but came from open-label trials where treatment assignment could influence the decision to start dialysis.

SODa-BIC addressed a broader and less severe acidosis population than prior severe-acidemia trials by enrolling vasopressor-dependent patients at pH <7.30 rather than pH ≤7.20. The clinical hypothesis was kidney-centered: early pH correction could reduce RRT, persistent renal dysfunction, or death.

Methods

This was a pragmatic, adaptive, double-blind randomized trial with stratification by center, pH (<7.25 vs ≥7.25), and creatinine (<1.7 vs ≥1.7 mg/dL). The intervention was sodium bicarbonate in D5W at 600 mmol/L; placebo was D5W alone. Both were started within 1 hour and adjusted by an identical algorithm based on pH and base excess at 1 and 3 hours, then stopped at 5 hours.

The primary endpoint was MAKE30: death, RRT, or persistent renal dysfunction by 30 days, censored at hospital discharge or day 30. Secondary endpoints included mortality, RRT, renal dysfunction, RRT dependence, acidosis recurrence, AKI, and organ-support-free days. The trial planned 500 patients to detect a 14 percentage-point absolute primary-outcome difference, larger than the observed between-group difference.

Results

The modified intention-to-treat population included 244 bicarbonate and 254 placebo patients. Baseline risk was high but balanced: median age 66 years, APACHE II 21, SOFA 7, baseline pH 7.26, base excess around −9 mmol/L, lactate 2.5 mmol/L, mechanical ventilation about 80%, and baseline AKI about 46%.

The intervention achieved acid-base separation: bicarbonate increased pH, bicarbonate, base excess, and sodium values and reduced recurrence of metabolic acidosis. It did not improve the primary outcome, mortality, AKI occurrence, organ-support-free days, or ICU/hospital-free days. Prespecified subgroup analyses did not identify a definitive group with lower MAKE30.

Discussion and limitations

The authors conclude that sodium bicarbonate rapidly corrected acidemia but did not lower major adverse kidney events. They emphasize that RRT findings must be interpreted cautiously because severe acidemia can trigger RRT; pH correction may defer RRT without proving kidney recovery.

Key limitations include some open-label bicarbonate use, lack of regulation of all sodium/buffer sources, exclusion of patients for whom bicarbonate was unsafe, potential unblinding by acid-base changes, absence of physiologic data at RRT initiation, no blood-glucose collection, limited power for smaller effects, and inter-ICU variation in RRT thresholds. These limitations mainly temper subgroup and RRT interpretations rather than reversing the negative primary result.

9. Outcome interpretation and bedside meaning

Primary endpoint

Neutral. The point estimate slightly favored placebo for MAKE30, and the confidence interval crossed clinically plausible benefit and harm. The trial was powered for a 14 percentage-point effect, so it does not exclude smaller effects.

Mortality

No signal of benefit. Day-30 and day-90 in-hospital mortality were similar, and the survival curves overlapped.

Renal replacement therapy

Numerically lower with bicarbonate but not definitive. Because pH correction can influence the decision to initiate RRT, this endpoint is partly clinician-behavior sensitive.

Acid-base correction

Clear and expected. The trial supports bicarbonate as an effective alkalinizing agent over hours, but not as a proven outcome-improving intervention in this population.

10. Safety signals and medication-safety interpretation

Adverse effects were uncommon: 4 patients (1.6%) in the bicarbonate group had an adverse effect compared with none in placebo; this was driven largely by hypokalemia requiring correction. The trial also showed a sodium increase with bicarbonate, which is expected from a 300 mmol median sodium exposure.

Safety interpretation: Low observed adverse-event frequency is reassuring only for patients resembling the trial population under a short, monitored infusion. It is not evidence that open-ended bicarbonate drips are benign in severe dysnatremia, hypokalemia, pulmonary edema, severe oxygenation impairment, acute brain injury, advanced CKD, or current/planned RRT.

Pharmacist-specific verification issues

  • Check the “why.” Is the order intended for trial-like metabolic acidosis, severe acidemia with AKI, hyperkalemia adjunctive therapy, toxicology, DKA, bicarbonate loss, or a nonspecific “low CO2” response?
  • Check ventilation. Bicarbonate can generate CO2; inadequate ventilation or hypercapnia shifts the risk-benefit balance. SODa-BIC required PaCO2 ≤45 mm Hg when not intubated or ≤50 mm Hg when intubated.
  • Check sodium and volume. A 300 mmol exposure is a meaningful sodium load; the median infusion volume was 500 mL.
  • Check potassium. Hypokalemia was the principal adverse-effect driver and was an exclusion if present at high-risk levels.
  • Check stop criteria. Orders should include reassessment times and objective goals, not indefinite continuation.

11. Implementation constraints and what should/should not change

What should change in practice

  • ICU bicarbonate use for vasopressor-dependent metabolic acidosis should be framed as physiologic correction, not as a proven renal-protective or mortality-reducing intervention.
  • Pharmacist interventions can target orders lacking a clear indication, pH/base-excess threshold, electrolyte review, volume/sodium plan, or stop criteria.
  • Order sets should favor short reassessment loops: repeat gas, potassium/sodium review, and escalation to source control/perfusion/RRT evaluation rather than serial bicarbonate escalation.
  • Educational messaging should separate “pH improves” from “patient-centered outcomes improve.”

What should not change solely because of this trial

  • Do not use SODa-BIC to deny bicarbonate for toxicologic sodium-channel blockade, urine alkalinization strategies, major bicarbonate losses, or severe hyperkalemia adjunctive management; these were not the studied questions.
  • Do not delay RRT when conventional indications are met: refractory acidosis, hyperkalemia, volume overload, uremic complications, or other nephrology/ICU indications.
  • Do not claim the severe acidemia plus advanced AKI subgroup is settled. The AKI stage 2/3 subgroup estimate favored bicarbonate but remained imprecise.

Guideline alignment

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign adult guideline page lists sodium bicarbonate as a conditional recommendation with very low certainty for adults with septic shock, severe metabolic acidemia (pH ≤7.2), and AKI stage 2 or 3. SODa-BIC enrolled a broader pH <7.30 vasopressor population and did not show improved MAKE30, including no definitive benefit in prespecified severe pH or AKI subgroups. Therefore, the trial supports narrowing—not broadening—routine bicarbonate use while preserving local-policy review for the pH ≤7.2 plus AKI stage 2/3 scenario.

12. Critical appraisal and ratings

Relevance 9/10

Rationale: Directly relevant to acute-care/ICU pharmacy verification of bicarbonate drips in shock-associated acidosis.

Evidence basis: ICU, vasopressors, acid-base dosing protocol, renal outcomes, safety signals.

Limiting factor: Excludes several high-stakes bicarbonate contexts.

Would raise/lower: Higher if it included more severe acidemia and local US compounding/order-set details; lower if used for non-ICU or toxicology cases.

Novelty 8/10

Rationale: Adds blinded placebo-controlled evidence in a broader vasopressor-dependent acidosis population than prior severe-acidemia trials.

Evidence basis: International double-blind design; pH <7.30 threshold; protocolized short infusion.

Limiting factor: It does not fully resolve the severe AKI subgroup question from prior trials.

Would raise/lower: Higher with definitive severe-acidemia subgroup power; lower if prior blinded trials had already addressed this exact population.

Method strength 8.5/10

Rationale: Strong randomization, blinding, placebo control, multicenter execution, and complete primary-outcome ascertainment.

Evidence basis: 498-patient modified intention-to-treat analysis; central randomization; indistinguishable solutions; prespecified analyses.

Limiting factor: Open-label bicarbonate and postintervention RRT decisions could dilute or bias secondary endpoints.

Would raise/lower: Higher with standardized RRT initiation criteria and physiologic data at RRT start; lower with worse crossover or missing outcomes.

Evidence strength 8/10

Rationale: Strong evidence against a moderate-to-large MAKE30 benefit in the included population; weaker for small effects and subgroups.

Evidence basis: Primary endpoint 40.2% vs 39.4%, adjusted difference +1.2 pp, 95% CI −7.1 to +9.4.

Limiting factor: Trial powered for a 14 pp effect; secondary outcomes not adjusted for multiplicity.

Would raise/lower: Higher with meta-analysis of blinded trials; lower if substantial center-level RRT practice variation drove results.

External validity 7/10

Rationale: Seven-country, 55-ICU enrollment supports generalizability to many ICUs, but exclusions narrow bedside application.

Evidence basis: Broad international ICU setting; high proportion mechanically ventilated, septic, and AKI patients.

Limiting factor: Excluded advanced CKD, planned/current RRT, DKA, poisoning, bicarbonate loss, high cerebral edema risk, and several electrolyte/oxygenation hazards.

Would raise/lower: Higher with data in ED-to-ICU transitions and advanced CKD/RRT-borderline patients; lower in non-ICU, mixed respiratory acidosis, or maintenance-fluid contexts.

Implementation value 8/10

Rationale: Provides a concrete infusion algorithm, targets, and stop criteria useful for medication-use review.

Evidence basis: 600 mmol/L, 100 mL/h start, 1- and 3-hour gas checks, reduce/stop thresholds, 5-hour maximum.

Limiting factor: Local compounding, osmolarity, line administration, and order-set workflows may differ.

Would raise/lower: Higher with protocol appendix operational details and compatibility data; lower if local systems cannot support rapid reassessment.

Final appraisal: High internal validity for the main negative result; moderate-to-high confidence that routine short-course bicarbonate should not be expected to improve MAKE30 or survival in the studied population. Confidence is lower for narrow severe-acidemia/advanced-AKI subgroups and for nontrial indications.
