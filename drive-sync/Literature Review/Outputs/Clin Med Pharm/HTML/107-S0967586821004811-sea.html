One-page synthesis

The paper aggregates 61 studies and reports a stroke-mimic rate of roughly one quarter of evaluated patients. In the main Results section, the rate is 24.9% (15,595/62,664); the abstract instead reports 24.8% (15,044/60,703), and the source does not reconcile the discrepancy. The most frequent pooled mimic categories are peripheral vestibular dysfunction (23.2%), toxic/metabolic causes (13.2%), seizure (13.0%), functional disorder (9.7%), and migraine (7.76%). Compared with patients classified as ischemic stroke, mimics are younger, more often female, have lower NIHSS and blood pressure, and show less conventional vascular-risk burden. Headache, vomiting, loss of consciousness, ability to walk, and an examination with no stroke signs are more frequent among mimics, but none of these features is presented as sufficiently specific to exclude true stroke.

Observed thrombolysis was common in mimics: 26.3% (635/2,407) versus 61.7% (10,232/16,586) in ischemic stroke. Intracranial hemorrhage (0.7% vs 9.4%), death (1.9% vs 11.3%), and excellent outcome by mRS 0-1 (68.9% vs 41.8%) favored the mimic group. The authors interpret the low complication rate as supportive of thrombolysis safety in mimics and emphasize experienced clinical assessment, especially for vertigo, headache, seizure, and functional presentations. The strongest practical value is descriptive: it maps common mimic phenotypes and diagnostic traps. The main methodological weakness is that heterogeneous studies are pooled without a conventional meta-analytic framework, reported risk-of-bias assessment, heterogeneity analysis, or effect-size uncertainty.

Bottom line: useful as a clinical evidence map and high-yield review of stroke-mimic patterns; too methodologically loose to serve as a quantitative prevalence standard, validated diagnostic rule, or stand-alone basis for thrombolysis decisions.

Section condensation

Introduction

The paper frames ischemic stroke as a time-critical diagnosis in which speed can increase the chance that non-stroke presentations enter stroke/thrombolysis pathways. It emphasizes that noncontrast CT is typically used early, while multimodal CT/MR imaging may help in uncertain cases. The stated gap is the lack of a single review integrating mimic etiology, clinical presentation, risk factors, treatment exposure, and outcomes.

The objective is explicitly descriptive: summarize prevalence, type, risk factors, presenting symptoms, and outcome of stroke mimics in ischemic stroke/thrombolysis settings.

Methods

The authors searched PubMed, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library, restricted to English-language publications through January 2020. Search terms were combinations of stroke, ischemic stroke, mimic, thrombolysis, rt-PA, alteplase, imaging, outcome, and mortality. Abstracts were reviewed, then full texts and reference lists of relevant articles were examined.

The paper does not report a reproducible Boolean search strategy, explicit eligibility criteria, screening/selection flow, dual review, protocol registration, risk-of-bias assessment, or evidence-certainty framework. These absences are appraisal findings inferred from the reported methods, not author-stated limitations.

Statistical analysis

Participants from contributing studies were divided into ischemic stroke and stroke-mimic groups. Mimic types, clinical findings, risk factors, thrombolysis rates, and outcomes were compared. Means ± SD were evaluated with Student t tests and categorical data with chi-square tests in SPSS 22.0.

The report does not explain how study-level heterogeneity, weighting, clustering, duplicate populations, or between-study variance were handled. It also does not provide pooled confidence intervals for most outcomes.

Results: evidence base and mimic prevalence

The paper reports 61 studies and 62,664 participants. It labels 5 prospective multicenter, 1 observational prospective, 17 prospective single-center, 9 retrospective multicenter, and 30 retrospective single-center studies; these categories total 62, which is not reconciled. The main Results stroke-mimic rate is 24.9% (15,595/62,664), whereas the abstract gives 24.8% (15,044/60,703).

Results: mimic types

Peripheral vestibular dysfunction is the largest category at 23.2% (1,109/4,769), followed by toxic/metabolic causes 13.2% (1,053/7,985), seizure 13.0% (1,257/9,629), functional disorder 9.7% (373/3,822), migraine 7.76% (697/8,983), collapse/presyncope 5.95%, sepsis 5.3%, mononeuropathy 5.0%, space-occupying lesion 4.3%, acute confusion 1.9%, dementia 1.2%, and spinal lesion 0.7%. Another 9.69% is labeled undefined etiology.

Denominators differ substantially by category, indicating that these percentages are derived from different subsets of the included literature rather than one uniform cohort.

Results: clinical findings and imaging

Mimics have lower mean NIHSS (4.99 vs 8.06), younger age (60.9 vs 68.4 years), more female patients (68% vs 56%), and lower average blood pressure. Loss of consciousness, vomiting, headache, ability to walk, and no stroke signs on examination are more frequent in mimics. Wake-up symptoms, confusion, and GCS <15 are not significantly different in the pooled comparisons shown.

CT is reported as diagnostic in 57.8% of mimics versus 80.2% of ischemic strokes. MRI is performed in 50.0% of mimics versus 41.5% of ischemic strokes. A cited CT-perfusion study reports higher odds of confirming clinical stroke with CTP than with NCCT+CTA or NCCT alone; this is a secondary report of a cited study, not a pooled primary result generated by this review.

Results: risk factors

Hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, known atrial fibrillation, and peripheral vascular disease are more frequent in the ischemic stroke group. Previous stroke, seizure history, and malignancy do not differ significantly. Cognitive impairment and migraine history are more frequent in mimics; the paper also reports pre-existing psychological disturbance as more common in ischemic stroke than mimic patients.

Results: thrombolysis and outcomes

Twenty studies contribute thrombolysis rates: 61.7% (10,232/16,586) in ischemic stroke versus 26.3% (635/2,407) in mimics. Three studies report thrombectomy: 12.2% (140/1,150) in ischemic stroke and 0/607 in mimics. Intracranial hemorrhage is 9.4% (786/8,403) versus 0.7% (4/524), death 11.3% (955/8,472) versus 1.9% (15/789), and mRS 0-1 41.8% (3,806/9,095) versus 68.9% (646/937), all favoring mimics in the reported comparisons.

These are unadjusted descriptive differences between fundamentally different diagnostic groups. They are useful for observed-risk context but do not, by themselves, estimate a causal treatment effect of thrombolysis.

Discussion

The discussion emphasizes posterior-circulation/vestibular presentations, toxic-metabolic disorders, seizures, functional neurological disorder, migraine, tumors, syncope/sepsis, neuropathies, delirium-like presentations, and stroke recrudescence. HINTS is highlighted for acute vestibular syndromes and Hoover sign for functional weakness; MRI DWI and multimodal imaging are presented as useful but imperfect. The authors propose a Figure 4 triage workflow integrating clinical probability, time from onset, CTA/CTP, MRI DWI, HINTS, and selective blood/toxicology testing.

The paper’s own explicit limitation is that it did not fulfill conventional meta-analysis criteria. The discussion nonetheless sometimes uses pooled descriptive differences to make stronger clinical interpretations, including a thrombolysis-safety conclusion. That interpretation should be read as hypothesis-supportive rather than causal because the mimic and stroke groups differ strongly at baseline and treatment assignment was not randomized.

Conclusion and disclosures

The authors conclude that peripheral vertigo, toxic/metabolic causes, seizure, migraine, and functional disorders are leading mimic etiologies; mimics tend to be younger, female, and less severely symptomatic with fewer vascular risk factors. They state that no single specific identification method exists apart from HINTS or Hoover sign and that MRI DWI/perfusion may help but needs further investigation.

Data are stated to be available on request. Funding is NKFI (OTKA)-135316. The authors declare no known competing financial interests or personal relationships affecting the work.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best use: etiologic map, diagnostic-pitfall review, and source discovery. Do not use for: stand-alone stroke exclusion, exact standardized prevalence, or current treatment-pathway decisions.

Strengths

  • Large reported aggregate sample and unusually broad coverage of mimic etiologies, clinical features, risk factors, imaging, thrombolysis, bleeding, mortality, and disability.
  • Many pooled findings retain numerators and denominators, making the descriptive data more inspectable than direction-only narrative claims.
  • Main figures and tables are clinically interpretable and Figure 4 captures a useful mental model for uncertainty management.
  • The authors disclose the major methodological limitation that the work is not a conventional meta-analysis.

Major limitations

  • Review methods are insufficiently reproducible for a high-confidence systematic review: no explicit eligibility criteria, selection flow, dual screening, quality assessment, protocol, or certainty grading are reported.
  • Pooling spans heterogeneous prospective/retrospective and single-/multicenter studies with outcome-specific denominators; no formal heterogeneity or weighting strategy is described.
  • Most comparisons emphasize p-values rather than effect estimates with uncertainty intervals, making clinical magnitude harder to judge.
  • Internal inconsistencies occur in the overall mimic numerator/denominator, the count of study designs, the “one out of three” thrombolysis summary, and the Table 1 “median” label paired with mean ± SD values.
  • Low hemorrhage/mortality among mimics is compatible with low observed risk but cannot establish causal safety of thrombolysis because diagnostic groups differ markedly at baseline and treatment was not randomized.
  • Supplementary Table 1 was not among the retrieved files, preventing full reconciliation of study-level rates and design categories.

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses acute stroke differential diagnosis, stroke-mimic phenotypes, imaging, thrombolysis exposure, and clinically important outcomes.

Evidence basis: 61-study review with etiologic, clinical, risk-factor, imaging, and outcome summaries.

Principal limiting factor: Relevance is high for acute-stroke assessment, but the source is not a current guideline or validated diagnostic rule.

Would raise/lower: Higher if linked to a validated contemporary pathway; lower for questions outside acute stroke triage.

Novelty

Rationale: The article assembles several dimensions of stroke mimics into one review and proposes an integrated emergency workflow.

Evidence basis: The authors present cross-study summaries of mimic types, clinical features, risk factors, revascularization, outcomes, and a management figure.

Principal limiting factor: Most underlying evidence predates January 2020 and some topics were already covered by prior reviews and meta-analyses cited by the authors.

Would raise/lower: Higher with a prospectively specified systematic-review protocol or new validated diagnostic model; lower if used only as a repetition of prior narrative reviews.

4/10

Method strength

Rationale: The source uses a broad literature search and large aggregate sample but does not report methods expected for a reproducible systematic review or conventional meta-analysis.

Evidence basis: Search of PubMed, MEDLINE, and Cochrane Library; English-only; simple pooled counts/means; t tests/chi-square; authors explicitly state it did not fulfill conventional meta-analysis criteria.

Principal limiting factor: No reported PRISMA-style flow, explicit eligibility criteria, duplicate screening, risk-of-bias assessment, heterogeneity model, publication-bias assessment, or certainty grading.

Would raise/lower: Higher with a registered protocol, reproducible search strings, study-level quality assessment, prespecified pooling, and heterogeneity/sensitivity analyses; lower if pooled denominators cannot be reconstructed.

5/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Large denominators support descriptive signal detection, but the pooled comparisons are observational, heterogeneous, and mostly unadjusted.

Evidence basis: 62,664 reported participants across selected studies; many outcomes include raw numerators/denominators and p-values.

Principal limiting factor: Variable outcome-specific denominators, mixed study designs, no confidence intervals for most pooled comparisons, no formal weighting/heterogeneity assessment, and internal numerical inconsistencies.

Would raise/lower: Higher with study-level effect estimates, confidence intervals, random-effects/meta-regression, bias assessment, and sensitivity analyses; lower if study overlap or duplicate populations are present.

6/10

External validity

Rationale: The review spans multiple study designs and settings, supporting broad descriptive applicability, but applicability cannot be quantified confidently.

Evidence basis: Prospective and retrospective, single- and multicenter studies are included, with telestroke, emergency, and stroke-unit populations among cited studies.

Principal limiting factor: Study selection and setting mix are not systematically characterized; pediatric/adult and pathway-specific cohorts may differ materially; no heterogeneity assessment.

Would raise/lower: Higher with stratified estimates by setting, age, geography, and diagnostic pathway; lower when applying pooled rates to a single local population.

5/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper is useful for pattern recognition and for identifying diagnostic failure modes, but its Figure 4 pathway is author-proposed rather than prospectively validated.

Evidence basis: Figure 4 integrates stroke probability, onset time, CTA/CTP, MRI DWI, HINTS, blood tests, and toxicology; the Results quantify common mimic patterns.

Principal limiting factor: The search cutoff is January 2020, the algorithm is not validated in this report, and the source is not a guideline.

Would raise/lower: Higher after validation against current guidelines and local workflow data; lower if used as a stand-alone treatment decision rule.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use nowUse the paper as a differential-diagnosis map: peripheral vestibular disorders, toxic/metabolic causes, seizure, functional disorder, and migraine account for much of the mimic burden in the authors’ synthesis.
Do not overinterpret demographicsYounger age, female sex, lower NIHSS, lower blood pressure, headache, vomiting, or absent focal signs shift probability in pooled data but do not exclude ischemic stroke.
Imaging insightMRI DWI and multimodal imaging can help in uncertainty, but the paper itself notes that imaging can be imperfect and calls for further investigation.
Vertigo requires special careThe review devotes substantial attention to vestibular mimics and posterior-circulation stroke, using HINTS as a focused bedside tool rather than a universal stroke screen.
Thrombolysis safety claimTreat the low observed hemorrhage rate in mimics as descriptive reassurance, not as a causal safety estimate from randomized evidence.
Best next evidenceThe most valuable follow-up is to read the primary studies behind thrombolysis safety, HINTS/vestibular diagnosis, functional stroke mimics, and imaging performance; all 112 citations were placed into a separate task queue.
