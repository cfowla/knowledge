Scope warning. This is a historical single-center audit using kaolin cephalin clotting time (KCT). It is informative for monitoring-process and protocol-design history, but it should not be used as a modern heparin dosing target or contemporary practice standard.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In 45 consecutive patients receiving heparin, therapeutic KCT control was achieved inconsistently; clinicians often failed to act on out-of-range results, sometimes adjusted in the wrong direction, and most remaining adjustments still failed to reach the target. The authors argued for closer attention to monitoring results and individualized titration.

The study prospectively analysed 180 heparin-treatment days. KCT was within the defined therapeutic range on 45 days, above it on 51, and below it on 84. Patients received heparin for a median of four days, yet therapeutic KCT was achieved for a median of only one day per patient, and 13 patients never entered the therapeutic range. Day-by-day control did not show a trend toward improvement over treatment duration.

Process failures were prominent: 26% of actionable KCT measurements were ignored, 12% of responses changed the infusion rate in the wrong direction, and 85% of the remaining correctly directed changes still failed to achieve therapeutic KCT. Pump use did not significantly outperform nurse-operated burettes (27% vs 19% of treatment days in range; p>0.05). In a 28-patient dose-response subset, the mean infusion rate associated with KCT 60 seconds was 1404 U/h (SD 432; range 690-2500), illustrating substantial between-patient dose requirements.

Section condensation

Opening / rationale

The authors framed the study as an audit of heparin treatment following prior evidence of poor anticoagulation control with warfarin. They used KCT as the local/national monitoring method and defined the therapeutic range as 1.5-2.5 times control, noting the clinical concern that higher values might increase bleeding risk whereas lower values could indicate inadequate anticoagulation.

Patients, methods, and results

Data were collected prospectively from 45 consecutive patients (23 men, 22 women; mean age 55 years, range 24-80) across 180 heparin days. Thirty patients received heparin via electric infusion pump and 15 via a nurse-operated paediatric burette. The investigators evaluated clinicians' infusion-rate responses to KCT results outside the therapeutic range. In 28 patients they also constructed dose-response curves to estimate the infusion rate required for KCT 60 seconds, equal to 1.5 times control in their laboratory.

KCT was in range on 45 days, above range on 51, and below range on 84. Median treatment duration was four days and median time in therapeutic range was one day; 13 patients never reached the range. Clinicians ignored 26% of measurements requiring a rate change, adjusted in the wrong direction 12% of the time, and 85% of remaining correctly directed changes failed to achieve the range. Pump versus burette control was 27% versus 19% of days (p>0.05). The 28-patient dose-response subset required a mean 1404 U/h (SD 432; range 690-2500) for KCT 60 seconds; men and women had means 1513 and 1278 U/h respectively (p>0.05), and no significant relation with age or weight was reported.

Comment

The authors interpreted the audit as evidence of poor local heparin control. They emphasized two clinically relevant challenges drawn partly from prior literature: large interpatient variability in heparin kinetics/response and a nonlinear relationship between infusion-rate changes and KCT response. They also noted that the expected advantage of pumps over burettes was not demonstrated, suggesting that device precision alone did not explain poor control.

Their practical recommendations were to pay closer attention to out-of-range KCT results, use smaller rate increments as KCT approaches the therapeutic range, and titrate infusion rates individually. These recommendations are source-authored conclusions from a historical monitoring system rather than validated modern dosing rules.

Clinical appraisal

Population45 consecutive heparin-treated patients at one hospital; 23 men, 22 women; mean age 55 years.
Intervention / exposureHeparin infusion monitored with KCT; administration by pump or nurse-operated burette.
ComparatorDescriptive pump-versus-burette comparison; not randomized.
OutcomesKCT therapeutic-range control, clinician response to actionable KCT, dose-response infusion rate.

Strengths

  • Prospective collection in consecutive patients.
  • Reports process-level failure modes, not only final therapeutic-range attainment.
  • Provides full day-by-day KCT distribution and a 28-patient dose-response estimate.
  • Separates infusion-device comparison from clinician response, supporting a medication-use-system interpretation.

Limitations

  • Small, single-center observational audit; no randomized protocol intervention.
  • No clinical efficacy or bleeding outcome comparison.
  • Heparin indications, initial doses, bolus strategies, sampling intervals, and exact actionable-measurement denominators are not fully reported.
  • KCT target and workflow are historical and not directly portable to modern aPTT or anti-Xa practice.
  • The 28-patient dose-response subset lacks reported sex-specific denominators, limiting subgroup interpretation.
Do not use for practice: Do not use the reported KCT target, 60-second goal, or mean 1404 U/h infusion rate as a contemporary dosing recommendation. The value of this paper is primarily historical and systems-oriented.

Implementation & practice takeaways

Protocol designA monitoring protocol should specify what to do for both subtherapeutic and supratherapeutic results; simply obtaining the lab value is insufficient.
Decision supportGuardrails should prevent wrong-direction dose changes and scale the size of adjustment near target rather than relying on ad hoc intuition.
AuditabilityTrack ignored actionable results, wrong-direction adjustments, time-to-next measurement, and failure to reach target after a change.
Individual variabilityA fixed infusion rate is unlikely to fit all patients; the source demonstrates wide infusion-rate requirements in its historical assay system.
Modern translationUse the paper to motivate contemporary protocol evaluation, not to set modern therapeutic ranges or dosing increments.
