Longitudinal observational analysis of two open prospective community-recruited cohorts (VIDUS and ACCESS).

Setting

Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada; December 1, 2014–March 30, 2020.

Scope warning

Association and trend study. It does not establish that prescribing-policy changes caused the observed availability pattern or that non-prescribed BUP-NX use is clinically safe.

One-page synthesis

Core finding: immediate availability of non-prescribed BUP-NX rose from 16% in 2014 to 63% in 2020 (p<0.001), while non-prescribed use remained rare: only 17 of 1,617 participants ever reported use.

The study used repeated semiannual observations from two established Vancouver cohorts of people who use drugs to examine whether non-prescribed buprenorphine-naloxone became more available as provincial prescribing restrictions relaxed. The main outcome was immediate availability, defined as the ability to acquire non-prescribed BUP-NX within less than 10 minutes. Delayed availability and self-reported non-prescribed use in the previous six months were also measured.

Availability rose markedly across the study period. In adjusted GEE analysis, each later calendar year was associated with higher odds of immediate availability (aOR 1.19, 95% CI 1.15–1.23). Immediate availability was also independently associated with residence in the Downtown Eastside, daily non-prescribed opioid use, prescribed BUP-NX treatment, recent overdose, naloxone-kit possession, recent incarceration, and drug dealing. Because only 17 participants reported non-prescribed use, the investigators did not model correlates of use. The authors interpret the divergence between rising availability and persistently rare reported use as evidence that relaxed prescribing restrictions were not a major driver of non-prescribed use in this population.

Interpretation boundary: the policy implication is suggestive, not causal. Prescribing access, illicit-drug-market conditions, clinic density, OAT alternatives, and participant characteristics changed over time and were not experimentally controlled.

Section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame BUP-NX as effective first-line OUD therapy whose prescribing has historically been restricted because of diversion and misuse concerns. British Columbia removed the exemption requirement for BUP-NX prescribing in 2016 and endorsed it as first-line OUD treatment in 2017 while also moving toward less restrictive take-home dosing.

The unresolved question was whether easier prescribing would substantially increase diverted availability and non-prescribed use. The study therefore followed availability and use longitudinally in Vancouver and examined factors associated with availability.

Methods

VIDUS includes HIV-negative adults with recent injection-drug use at enrollment; ACCESS includes HIV-positive adults with recent use of unregulated substances other than cannabis. Both are open prospective community cohorts with interviewer-administered questionnaires at baseline and every six months. The present analysis included participants with a history of injection drug use and at least one visit from December 2014 through March 2020.

Immediate availability meant acquisition within <10 minutes; delayed availability meant acquisition taking longer than 10 minutes; no availability meant the participant “could not score.” Non-prescribed use was self-reported use in the prior six months. Trends were tested with the Cochran-Armitage test. Because non-prescribed use was rare, subsequent regression focused on availability. GEE logistic models evaluated repeated observations, with bivariable p<0.10 used for multivariable entry.

Results

The 1,617 participants had median baseline age 47 years (IQR 37–54); 966 (60%) were male. Median follow-up was 7 visits (IQR 4–10). Immediate availability increased from 16% in 2014 to 63% in 2020, delayed availability from 18% to 50%, and inability to obtain BUP-NX fell from 71% to 27% (all trend p<0.001). Only 17 participants generated 17 total reports of non-prescribed BUP-NX use.

Calendar year predicted immediate availability in bivariable analysis (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.20–1.27) and after adjustment (aOR 1.19, 95% CI 1.15–1.23). Other independent adjusted correlates included Downtown Eastside residence (1.46, 1.30–1.64), daily non-prescribed opioid use (1.29, 1.14–1.46), prescribed BUP-NX OAT (1.78, 1.35–2.35), recent overdose (1.20, 1.03–1.39), naloxone-kit possession (1.39, 1.24–1.55), recent incarceration (1.43, 1.11–1.84), and drug dealing (1.37, 1.18–1.58).

Discussion and conclusions

The authors interpret increasing non-prescribed availability alongside very infrequent use as evidence that expanding prescribed access did not translate into a commensurate increase in non-prescribed consumption in this Vancouver population. They note that prescribed BUP-NX availability and low-barrier OAT access may partly explain the low rate of non-prescribed use.

They explicitly limit causal interpretation: the analysis is observational, relies on self-report, has uncertain generalizability beyond these cohorts and this treatment environment, and cannot identify the drivers of temporal trends. Their conclusion is therefore best read as evidence against a simple “more prescribing necessarily produces more misuse” model, not as proof of a policy causal effect.

Strengths

  • Longitudinal repeated measures across a meaningful policy period.
  • Established community cohorts and semiannual data collection.
  • Clear operational definition of immediate availability.
  • GEE appropriately addresses repeated observations.
  • Rare non-prescribed use was not over-modeled after the event count proved inadequate.

Limitations

  • Observational design prevents causal attribution to prescribing-policy changes.
  • Self-reported sensitive outcomes may be misclassified or underreported.
  • Vancouver’s fentanyl market, OAT options, and low-barrier clinic density constrain generalizability.
  • The study cannot determine why availability increased or why use remained rare.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use

Use the study as empirical evidence that greater availability of non-prescribed BUP-NX can coexist with very low reported non-prescribed use in a high-access treatment environment.

Do not overclaim

Do not infer that removing prescribing restrictions caused the observed pattern, that diversion is harmless, or that the findings generalize to low-access settings.

Policy relevance

The findings weaken a simple assumption that easing BUP-NX prescribing necessarily produces proportional growth in non-prescribed use.

Research need

Policy-linked causal analyses and replication across treatment systems should measure prescribed access, diversion, treatment retention, overdose, and patient-reported use together.
