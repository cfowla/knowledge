One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The task force frames pulmonary embolism as a common, underdiagnosed and often preventable cause of morbidity and death. It integrates contemporary diagnostic approaches with acute anticoagulation, thrombolysis, long-term anticoagulation, and a strong prevention agenda, especially for surgical and immobilized patients.

Heparin-specific signal

The report treats heparin as foundational acute PE therapy. It cites a 35-patient randomized trial in which recurrent PE occurred in 0/16 heparin-treated patients versus 10/19 placebo-treated patients, with five fatal recurrences in the placebo group. For treatment, it recommends an initial intravenous bolus followed by continuous infusion and an activated partial thromboplastin time target of 1.5-2.5 times control. It also recommends overlapping heparin with oral anticoagulation for 4-5 days.

For prophylaxis, it summarizes a 4121-patient trial using UFH 5000 units subcutaneously 2 hours preoperatively and every 8 hours for 7 days, and a review of 78 randomized trials (15,598 patients) reporting 40% lower nonfatal PE and 64% lower fatal PE with perioperative subcutaneous heparin. It also presents early LMWH evidence, including a 295-patient abdominal-surgery study with DVT rates of 2.5% versus 7.5% for LMWH versus UFH and a reported meta-analytic 20% lower VT rate with LMWH.

Diagnosis

Clinical suspicion anchors the diagnostic pathway. The task force gives ventilation-perfusion scanning a central role, describes a normal scan as effectively excluding significant PE, and reports about 90% diagnostic accuracy for high-probability scans in affected patients. D-dimer by ELISA is described as highly sensitive (about 95%) but nonspecific. Echocardiography and leg-vein testing are positioned as adjuncts in selected settings, and pulmonary angiography remains the reference invasive test.

Prevention

The prevention section is unusually implementation-oriented for its era. It recommends risk-based prophylaxis for virtually all hospitalized adults, especially major surgical patients, and supports extending prophylaxis after discharge in moderate- or high-risk patients. Mechanical measures, UFH, LMWH, warfarin, dextran, vena-cava filters, and combined approaches are compared, with separate recommendations for orthopedic surgery, gynecologic cancer surgery, general surgery, and immobilized medical patients.

Main limitation for reuse: the report is a narrative task-force synthesis from 1992. Many quantitative statements are secondary summaries of cited studies, and the report does not describe a systematic evidence-search process, risk-of-bias assessment, or formal grading of recommendation strength.

Section condensation

Task-force purpose and process

The WHO and ISFC convened the task force to assess the state of the art of VT and PE. Members prepared position papers and oral presentations, contributed sections in their areas of expertise, revised the report during the meeting and afterward, and obtained final steering-committee approval. The abstract emphasizes major knowledge gaps: better epidemiology in hospitalized medical and high-risk outpatient populations, global incidence/survival/long-term-outcome data, better prophylaxis education, and research on changing physician behavior.

Epidemiology and autopsy studies

The report describes VT/PE as common in North America and Europe and emphasizes persistent underdiagnosis at autopsy, particularly in older patients and those with cardiopulmonary disease. It also notes sparse and methodologically inconsistent population data, acquired and inherited risk states, and continuing postoperative risk after discharge. A Geneva study is cited as increasing the measured postoperative PE rate by 30% when events through 30 days after discharge were included.

The task force calls for standardized case definitions, age-corrected epidemiology, region/race/gender-specific studies, and further study of calf-vein and upper-extremity thrombosis as antecedents of PE.

Diagnosis

Clinical suspicion, symptoms, signs of VT, and predisposing conditions are the starting point. Chest radiography and ECG are supportive rather than definitive; arterial blood gases are not recommended as a screening test. D-dimer ELISA is described as highly sensitive (~95%) and potentially useful to exclude thrombosis, but positive tests require more specific confirmation. V/Q scanning is recommended as the primary screening test when available; normal scans are described as effectively excluding significant PE, whereas high-probability scans are reported to identify PE accurately in about 90% of affected patients.

Echocardiography is presented as a selected-patient tool without definitive sensitivity/specificity data. Leg-vein imaging can establish proximal DVT and thereby influence treatment but cannot exclude PE when negative. In hypotensive patients, angiography, V/Q scanning, and echocardiography are selected according to local capability. Properly performed pulmonary angiography is reported to carry a death rate no higher than 2-5 per 1000.

Treatment

The report cites a small randomized heparin-vs-placebo trial as the principal direct support for anticoagulation, with 0 recurrent PEs among 16 heparin-treated patients versus 10 among 19 placebo patients. It recommends IV heparin as a bolus followed by continuous infusion, with aPTT maintained at 1.5-2.5 times control, while acknowledging bleeding risk and heparin-associated thrombocytopenia. Thrombolysis is discussed for selected patients, including some with high clinical suspicion and compelling imaging when angiography is not obtained.

For long-term therapy, oral anticoagulation is usual; heparin overlap for 4-5 days is recommended, with a target INR of 2.0-3.0 and a typical treatment duration of 3-6 months, longer when major risk factors persist. IVC filters are offered when anticoagulation cannot be tolerated. The task force explicitly identifies LMWH, hirudin, and embolectomy as areas needing further study.

Prevention

The task force recommends a broad risk-based prophylaxis strategy and emphasizes that risk persists after discharge. Mechanical options include graduated compression stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression; vena-cava interruption is reserved for anticoagulation contraindication/failure or selected extreme-risk circumstances. Combined mechanical and pharmacologic prophylaxis is favored for many moderate/high-risk settings.

For UFH prophylaxis, the report summarizes the International Multicentre Trial (4121 patients) and a 78-trial review (15,598 patients), reporting large reductions in nonfatal and fatal PE with a modest absolute increase in excessive bleeding. LMWH is described as potentially offering less bleeding, once-daily dosing, and greater efficacy, supported by early trials and meta-analysis. Special recommendations are provided for orthopedic surgery, gynecologic cancer surgery, and immobilized medical patients. The closing prevention section calls prophylaxis underused and advocates institutional risk-assessment systems and extended prophylaxis in high-risk patients.
