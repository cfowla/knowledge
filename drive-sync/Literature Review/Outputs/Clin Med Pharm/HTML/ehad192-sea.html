The guideline focuses on cardiovascular disease in diabetes and states that its recommendations use evidence published through the end of January 2023. It covers diagnosis, cardiovascular risk assessment, lifestyle, glycaemic targets, glucose-lowering therapy, blood pressure, lipids, antithrombotic therapy, coronary artery disease, heart failure, arrhythmias, chronic kidney disease, peripheral and aortic disease, type 1 diabetes, and person-centred care.

Executive synthesis

Clinical center of gravity. The guideline moves cardiovascular and kidney protection closer to the front of diabetes care. In type 2 diabetes with established ASCVD, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists with proven cardiovascular benefit are recommended independent of baseline or target HbA1c. The document also integrates heart-failure and CKD treatment into the same cardiovascular-risk framework rather than treating glucose lowering as the sole therapeutic axis.

Risk assessment is tiered. Established ASCVD and severe target-organ damage define high-risk groups without the need for SCORE2-Diabetes. For eligible adults with type 2 diabetes without those conditions, SCORE2-Diabetes estimates 10-year cardiovascular risk. The supplement makes the model's boundaries explicit: sex-specific point tables cover ages 40 to 69 years and use country risk regions, while documented ASCVD, severe target-organ damage, CKD, pregnancy, and specified rare or genetic lipid and blood-pressure disorders fall outside the algorithm.

The risk-factor program is conventional in structure but aggressive in execution: smoking cessation, exercise, weight management, Mediterranean or plant-based dietary patterns, individualized glycaemic targets, intensive LDL-C reduction by risk category, and early combination antihypertensive therapy. The guideline repeatedly distinguishes therapies that improve cardiovascular outcomes from agents that only have cardiovascular safety data.

Heart failure and kidney disease are major cross-cutting domains. SGLT2 inhibitors are recommended across HFrEF and in type 2 diabetes with LVEF above 40%. In CKD, the treatment pathway combines statin-based therapy, blood-pressure control, ACE-I or ARB when indicated, SGLT2 inhibition, and finerenone in eligible type 2 diabetes. Class I recommendations dominate the guideline. The 144 extracted rows include 50 Class I, Level A recommendations, 20 Class I, Level B recommendations, and 25 Class I, Level C recommendations. The remaining rows are Class IIa, IIb, or III. This distribution matters for implementation: many high-priority actions rest on randomized or meta-analytic evidence, while a substantial set of diagnostic, sequencing, monitoring, and special-population recommendations remains Level C.

SCORE2-Diabetes. The supplement provides country risk-region tables and separate point tables for men and women aged 40-69 years. Use is bounded by the exclusions stated in the source; it is not a substitute for established ASCVD, severe target-organ damage, or CKD classification.

Therapeutic priorities by domain

Glucose-lowering therapy

In established ASCVD, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists with demonstrated cardiovascular benefit receive Class I, Level A recommendations. In patients without ASCVD or severe target-organ damage, the document uses SCORE2-Diabetes and distinguishes metformin and outcome-proven SGLT2 or GLP-1 therapies by calculated cardiovascular risk.

Blood pressure and lipids

Office blood pressure of at least 140/90 mmHg triggers drug treatment. The general systolic target is 130 mmHg and below 130 mmHg if tolerated, but not below 120 mmHg. Lipid targets tighten with cardiovascular risk: LDL-C below 100 mg/dL at moderate risk, below 70 mg/dL plus at least 50% reduction at high risk, and below 55 mg/dL plus at least 50% reduction at very high risk. Statins are first line, followed by ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibition when needed.

Antithrombotic therapy

The guideline separates primary prevention from established coronary disease, PCI, and long-term oral anticoagulation. It recommends standard secondary-prevention antiplatelet therapy after MI or revascularization and structured combinations after ACS or PCI. It also stresses gastrointestinal protection when antithrombotic drugs are combined.

Coronary artery disease

Revascularization indications are broadly aligned with non-diabetes practice, but the document emphasizes complete revascularization where appropriate and the evidence favoring CABG over PCI for many patients with diabetes and multivessel disease. Routine immediate non-culprit revascularization in cardiogenic shock is not recommended.

Heart failure

For HFrEF with diabetes, SGLT2 inhibition, sacubitril/valsartan or ACE-I, beta-blockers, and MRAs are Class I therapies. The guideline also recommends rapid initiation and up-titration of evidence-based therapy after heart-failure hospitalization. Empagliflozin or dapagliflozin is Class I, Level A for type 2 diabetes with LVEF above 40%. Pioglitazone and saxagliptin are specifically discouraged in patients at risk of heart failure because of hospitalization signals.

Chronic kidney disease

Routine eGFR and UACR screening is part of cardiovascular prevention. The source recommends intensive lipid lowering, blood-pressure control, individualized HbA1c goals, maximally tolerated ACE-I or ARB where indicated, an SGLT2 inhibitor at eGFR at least 20 mL/min/1.73 m2, and finerenone for defined albuminuric CKD phenotypes. Dual ACE-I plus ARB therapy is not recommended.

Peripheral arterial disease, type 1 diabetes, and person-centred care

LEAD care includes regular screening, foot education, ABI or alternative non-invasive testing, duplex imaging, and revascularization for limb salvage when feasible. Type 1 diabetes guidance emphasizes self-management within a multidisciplinary team, avoidance of hypoglycaemia, and conventional cardiovascular risk-factor control. Structured education and person-centred shared decision-making close the treatment framework.

Evidence gaps

Section 15 is unusually useful because it states where the guideline should not be over-read. The main unresolved areas are diabetes screening strategies, long-term exercise outcomes, continuous glucose-monitoring effects on macrovascular outcomes, combined GLP-1 plus SGLT2 therapy, blood-pressure targets in type 1 diabetes, optimal LDL-C targets in type 1 diabetes, primary-prevention antithrombotic selection, DAPT and triple-therapy duration, revascularization comparisons in selected CAD phenotypes, heart-failure screening, CKD combination therapy, vascular screening frequency, and personalized digital or interdisciplinary care.

Implementation implication. Class and level should not be collapsed into a single notion of certainty. Some clinically important Class I actions are Level C because the recommendation is based on consensus, indirect evidence, or evidence outside the exact diabetes subgroup.

Methods, declarations, and governance

The Task Force describes a formal review process with critical evaluation of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, predefined recommendation classes and evidence levels, and voting on every approved recommendation. Approval required at least 75% agreement among voting members. The ESC reports that the Task Force was financially supported entirely by the ESC without healthcare-industry involvement.

The separate declaration-of-interest packet lists yearly relationships reported by Task Force members and reviewers during guideline development. The declarations include, depending on the individual and year, speaker or consultancy payments, institutional payments, research support, employment, travel support, ownership, and professional affiliations. The existence of these disclosures is relevant to transparency and governance; it is not by itself evidence that a particular recommendation was biased.

Appraisal

Overall appraisal. This is a high-implementation-value guideline for integrated cardiovascular care in diabetes. Its strongest features are explicit recommendation grading, broad cardiovascular and kidney scope, direct incorporation of modern SGLT2 and GLP-1 outcome trials, supplementary risk tools, dedicated evidence-selection tables, and transparent disclosure infrastructure. Its main constraints are the evidence cutoff, substantial reliance on Level C recommendations for some monitoring and special-population decisions, incomplete direct evidence in type 1 diabetes, and the need to translate a large number of overlapping recommendations into patient-specific treatment sequences.

What the guideline does well

  • Separates cardiovascular efficacy from glucose-lowering efficacy and from cardiovascular safety.
  • Integrates ASCVD, heart failure, and CKD rather than treating them as isolated complications.
  • Uses quantitative risk stratification and explicit LDL-C, blood-pressure, eGFR, UACR, and HbA1c thresholds.
  • Places bleeding, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia, and heart-failure safety signals near relevant therapeutic decisions.
  • Provides algorithms and evidence tables that make implementation and provenance easier to audit.

Where caution is needed

  • Guideline recommendations summarize underlying studies. They should not be treated as primary-study data.
  • SCORE2-Diabetes has defined eligibility boundaries and should not be extrapolated mechanically outside them.
  • Many therapy combinations now used in practice had limited direct randomized combination evidence at the guideline cutoff.
  • Type 1 diabetes, frailty, sex-specific care, digital health, and several screening questions have thinner evidence.
  • The guideline is dense. Local implementation needs a sequencing strategy to avoid polypharmacy, monitoring failures, and contradictory application of disease-specific guidance.
