One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In GUSTO IIa, intensive hirudin and a somewhat more aggressive heparin regimen produced concerning intracerebral hemorrhage rates, especially when combined with thrombolytic therapy. The safety signal led to early trial termination and protocol dose reduction.

The trial randomized patients with acute coronary syndromes to intravenous heparin or recombinant desulfatohirudin. Heparin used a 5,000-U bolus and 1,000 or 1,300 U/h infusion according to weight, titrated to an aPTT of 60-90 seconds. Hirudin used a 0.6-mg/kg bolus and 0.2 mg/kg/h infusion, with no routine adjustment unless aPTT exceeded 150 seconds. Therapy lasted 72-120 hours; ST-elevation patients could receive accelerated tissue-type plasminogen activator (TPA) or streptokinase.

After 2,564 of 12,000 planned patients were enrolled, the trial stopped early because of intracerebral hemorrhage. Overall hemorrhagic stroke was 1.3% with hirudin versus 0.7% with heparin (P=.11). The clearest signal was the interaction with thrombolytic exposure: 1.8% with thrombolytic therapy versus 0.3% without (P<.001). In patients receiving thrombolysis, hemorrhagic stroke was 2.2% with hirudin and 1.5% with heparin (P=.34). Stroke rates were descriptively high with streptokinase in both antithrombin arms. Patients who developed hemorrhagic stroke had a higher 12-hour aPTT (110+/-46 versus 87+/-36 seconds; P=.031).

Primary limitation: The report does not answer the original efficacy hypothesis. Early termination, preliminary non-stroke data, and a safety-focused publication mean the evidence is much stronger for identifying a bleeding hazard than for comparing clinical efficacy.
Why this source matters: It is a direct historical example of why anticoagulant dose intensity, thrombolytic co-therapy, aPTT exposure, renal function, and prospective safety monitoring can materially change the risk profile of a regimen that looked promising in angiographic pilot studies.

Section condensation

Background / Introduction

Heparin was standard adjunctive therapy in acute coronary syndromes but has mechanistic limitations: dependence on antithrombin III, lack of activity against clot-bound thrombin, and neutralization by plasma proteins and platelet factor 4. Recombinant hirudin, a direct thrombin inhibitor, had shown encouraging pilot angiographic and clinical signals and more stable anticoagulation.

The study was designed as a definitive randomized comparison of heparin versus hirudin across acute coronary syndrome phenotypes, including thrombolytic-treated ST-elevation infarction and non-ST-elevation presentations.

Methods

Patients were randomized by the coordinating center and treated under a double-infusion-bag design to preserve blinding. aPTT was measured at 6, 12, and 24 hours and then at least daily. Heparin was actively titrated to 60-90 seconds; hirudin was generally not adjusted unless aPTT exceeded 150 seconds. Death, stroke, and MI were independently adjudicated by a committee blinded to treatment assignment.

The planned sample size was 12,000, assuming an 8.5% event rate and a 20% relative reduction with hirudin. A data and safety monitoring committee reviewed accumulating data. Intracranial hemorrhage required a new neurologic deficit with death or persistence beyond 24 hours plus imaging/autopsy evidence. The authors explicitly stated that this report contained only intracranial hemorrhage outcomes and that other clinical data were preliminary.

Results

The trial began September 1, 1993 and was terminated April 8, 1994 because of excess intracerebral hemorrhage. The final safety cohort for this report included 1,291 heparin patients and 1,273 hirudin patients. Compared with the prior GUSTO I intravenous-heparin cohort, GUSTO IIa patients were older and more often women; weight was similar.

Overall hemorrhagic stroke was numerically higher with hirudin (17/1,273; 1.3%) than heparin (9/1,291; 0.7%), but this comparison did not reach statistical significance (P=.11). Thrombolytic-treated patients had much higher stroke incidence than those without thrombolysis (1.8% vs 0.3%; P<.001). Within thrombolytic-treated patients, hirudin again had a numerically higher rate than heparin (2.2% vs 1.5%; P=.34). The 12-hour aPTT was significantly higher among patients who developed hemorrhagic stroke (110+/-46 vs 87+/-36 seconds; P=.031).

Discussion / Conclusions

The investigators interpreted the findings as evidence of a narrow safety margin for intensive antithrombin therapy combined with thrombolysis. They noted that the GUSTO IIa heparin regimen delivered approximately 20% more heparin than GUSTO I and that hemorrhagic stroke rates were substantially higher than historical GUSTO I rates under similar thrombolytic strategies. The authors considered aPTT a useful safety signal for both heparin and hirudin, while acknowledging assay variability and uncertainty about the optimal range.

The trial was reinitiated with lower doses: heparin returned to the prior GUSTO I regimen, hirudin was reduced to 0.1 mg/kg bolus plus 0.1 mg/kg/h, and the aPTT target returned to 60-85 seconds with active down-titration for marked prolongation. Exclusion criteria were tightened for prior stroke, uncontrolled hypertension, and renal dysfunction. The paper ends by emphasizing that safety studies must complement favorable angiographic pilot results.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin/antithrombin safety deep dive; do not use as a stand-alone contemporary practice directive.
9/10
Relevance

Directly addresses UFH dose intensity, aPTT exposure, thrombolytic co-therapy, renal concerns, and hemorrhagic stroke.

Limiting factor: historical ACS context and obsolete hirudin regimen. Would rise: not materially; already central to the historical question.

8/10
Novelty

The trial converted promising pilot direct-thrombin-inhibition data into a clinically important large safety signal.

Limiting factor: early report focused on safety rather than the full hypothesis. Would rise: complete efficacy/safety report in the same publication.

7/10
Method strength

Randomization, double blinding, multicountry enrollment, prospective monitoring, and blinded adjudication are strong features.

Limiting factor: early termination and preliminary non-stroke data. Would rise: full prespecified outcome reporting with final quality-controlled dataset.

7/10
Evidence strength

The intracranial-hemorrhage signal is directly observed and adjudicated, with denominators, confidence intervals, and key P values reported.

Limiting factor: small absolute event count (26 strokes), many subgroup comparisons, and no final efficacy analysis. Would rise: larger completed safety dataset and robust adjusted analyses.

6/10
External validity

Broad multinational recruitment supports generalizability to the era's ACS population.

Limiting factor: thrombolytic-era management, intensive historical regimens, and selection/exclusion criteria limit direct modern applicability. Would rise: replication under contemporary ACS care.

8/10
Implementation value

Highly useful for understanding anticoagulant safety boundaries: dose intensity, aPTT monitoring, renal function, co-therapy, and adaptive protocol governance.

Limiting factor: regimen-specific thresholds are not validated here for modern practice. Would rise: current guideline or validation linkage.

Validity and bias considerations

  • Strengths: randomized treatment assignment; double-blind treatment; multinational sites; prospective DSMB; blinded adjudication; explicit hemorrhage definition; intention-to-treat comparisons.
  • Major limitation: the trial stopped early at 2,564/12,000 planned participants because of safety, leaving the primary efficacy hypothesis unanswered in this article.
  • Historical-comparator caution: comparisons with GUSTO I are informative but not randomized contemporaneous comparisons; differences in population and dosing can confound cross-trial interpretation.
  • Risk-factor caution: the association between high 12-hour aPTT and hemorrhagic stroke is clinically important but observational within the randomized trial and based on only 26 events.
  • Population difference: the investigators themselves noted that GUSTO IIa patients were older and more often women than the GUSTO I reference cohort, potentially contributing to excess bleeding risk.
  • Reporting boundary: only intracranial hemorrhage outcomes were presented in detail; efficacy outcomes and detailed secondary outcomes were deferred.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use nowUse the paper as historical mechanistic and safety evidence that anticoagulant intensity cannot be judged independently of thrombolytic co-therapy and achieved coagulation response.
MonitoraPTT exposure was associated with hemorrhagic stroke in this trial; the study supports the general need to monitor achieved anticoagulant effect rather than relying only on nominal dose.
Renal safetyThe authors linked at least two hemorrhagic strokes to renal dysfunction and tightened creatinine criteria when restarting the trial, consistent with the renal clearance concern for hirudin.
Protocol governanceThe DSMB-triggered stop and dose/protocol revision are a useful model for safety surveillance when pilot efficacy signals are scaled to larger populations.
Do not overclaimThe paper does not establish that hirudin is more or less efficacious than heparin for the planned 30-day death/MI endpoint.
Do not transplant thresholdsThe specific historical aPTT targets and doses should not be adopted into current care from this source alone.
