Scope warning. This article can support recognition of a possible rare adverse-event signal. It cannot establish incidence, relative risk, or teriparatide as the sole cause of calciphylaxis.

One-page synthesis

The investigators assembled 12 reports of calciphylaxis after teriparatide exposure from EMBASE, PubMed, FDA reports, and FAERS. They limited inclusion to events following less than 2 years of teriparatide exposure. The median age was 81 years, 11 of 12 cases were female, all cases involved teriparatide for osteoporosis, and nine reported the labeled 20 mcg daily dose. Calciphylaxis began a median of 3.5 months after teriparatide initiation, with a reported range of 1 to 20 months. Eleven cases had confirmatory biopsy and/or imaging. Three patients were hospitalized and one died after progressive lesions.

The safety signal is difficult to separate from baseline risk. Every case had multiple calciphylaxis risk factors, averaging 4.5 per case. Seven patients used warfarin, 10 had autoimmune or other inflammatory disease, and 10 had no CKD. The authors argue that the mostly nonuremic pattern and timing after teriparatide are notable. They also state that spontaneous reports cannot establish teriparatide as the sole cause. This is the right boundary for the evidence. The paper is useful for recognition and hypothesis generation, not for estimating risk or making a stand-alone treatment rule.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract states the pharmacovigilance question, the combined literature and FDA search, the less-than-2-year exposure criterion, and the core 12-case findings. It reports the median age of 81 years, median onset of 3.5 months, 11 cases with biopsy or imaging support, three hospitalizations, one death, and multiple background risk factors.

The abstract's causal wording remains cautious. The authors say teriparatide exposure plus underlying risk factors may have triggered calciphylaxis. They call for prompt recognition because the event can be life-threatening.

Introduction

The introduction frames calciphylaxis as a rare small-vessel occlusive disorder classically linked to ESRD, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and an elevated calcium-phosphorus product. It contrasts this with nonuremic calciphylaxis, whose incidence is unknown, and lists recognized background risk factors such as kidney or liver disease, female sex, obesity, autoimmune disease, trauma, warfarin, corticosteroids, calcium, and active vitamin D.

The article's own research question begins where the background ends. A small number of published cases had described calciphylaxis after teriparatide, so the investigators reviewed postmarketing reports to characterize the pattern.

Methods

The investigators searched EMBASE and PubMed and reviewed cases reported to FDA, including FAERS, through March 31, 2021. They included calciphylaxis after less than 2 years of teriparatide exposure, matching the recommended treatment duration at the time the reports were submitted.

Case review covered risk factors, timing relative to teriparatide, diagnostic information, comorbidities, concurrent medications, demographics, and outcomes. The report describes no comparator group, exposure denominator, adjudication framework, or formal causal-inference method.

Results

Twelve cases were identified, four from published reports. All involved teriparatide for osteoporosis; nine reported 20 mcg daily. Median age was 81 years, 11 patients were female, and median onset was 3.5 months. Eleven cases had biopsy and/or imaging confirmation. Three patients were hospitalized and one died.

Confounding was substantial. All 12 cases had multiple risk factors, with a mean of 4.5. Seven used warfarin, 10 had autoimmune or inflammatory disease, 10 had no CKD, and two had stable CKD. Among 11 cases with reported interventions, four improved, two resolved, and five did not improve or resolve. Two improved or resolved after stopping teriparatide alone.

Discussion

The authors focus on the mostly nonuremic presentation. Ten of 12 cases had no CKD, and the two CKD cases were described as stable. They discuss biological plausibility through teriparatide's effects on calcium and phosphorus and a hypothesized RANK/NF-kB pathway, while also acknowledging that injection-related tissue injury could be another trigger. These mechanism arguments rely on cited pharmacology and prior literature, not new mechanistic data from this case series.

The limitations are decisive. FAERS reports can be incomplete, follow-up was missing in several cases, and each patient had competing risk factors. The authors explicitly state that the spontaneous-report design cannot establish teriparatide as the sole cause. Their practical conclusion is therefore signal-oriented: consider the association in the differential diagnosis and act quickly when calciphylaxis is suspected.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Read the methods, aggregate results, Table 1, and limitations. Use the paper as a pharmacovigilance signal and a reference map. Do not use it to estimate incidence or prove causality.

Relevance: 7/10

Directly relevant to pharmacovigilance and medication safety for a rare, severe event.

Evidence basis. The article focuses on teriparatide exposure and calciphylaxis in 12 postmarketing cases.

Main limit. The signal is narrow and does not answer routine osteoporosis efficacy questions.

Would raise/lower. A larger comparative safety study or robust pharmacovigilance signal analysis would raise the score.

Novelty: 7/10

The paper consolidates literature and FDA postmarketing cases into a focused teriparatide-associated calciphylaxis series.

Evidence basis. It identifies 12 cases, including four previously published cases, and emphasizes mostly nonuremic presentations.

Main limit. The design is descriptive and several cases predated the report by many years.

Would raise/lower. Demonstrating a reproducible disproportionality or cohort signal would raise the score.

Method strength: 3/10

Case ascertainment is explicit, but the design is a spontaneous-report case series without a comparator or exposure denominator.

Evidence basis. EMBASE, PubMed, FDA reports, and FAERS were searched through March 31, 2021; cases were reviewed for timing, risk factors, diagnostics, and outcomes.

Main limit. Reporting bias, missing data, uncertain duplicate capture, and confounding limit causal inference.

Would raise/lower. A prespecified comparative cohort with adjudicated cases and defined denominators would raise the score.

Evidence strength: 3/10

The case series supports a safety signal but does not estimate causal effect or incidence.

Evidence basis. Most cases had biopsy or imaging confirmation and onset followed teriparatide by a median of 3.5 months.

Main limit. All cases had multiple competing risk factors; seven used warfarin and 10 had autoimmune or inflammatory disease.

Would raise/lower. Replication in denominator-based data with adjusted comparisons would raise the score.

External validity: 3/10

The findings apply mainly as a signal in selected reported cases, not as a population risk estimate.

Evidence basis. The series was predominantly female, older, and treated for osteoporosis; 10 of 12 cases lacked CKD.

Main limit. Spontaneous reporting favors unusual or severe cases and cannot represent all teriparatide users.

Would raise/lower. Population-based data across broader ages, sexes, kidney function, and comorbidity profiles would raise the score.

Implementation value: 5/10

The article can improve diagnostic awareness but should not drive a stand-alone prescribing rule.

Evidence basis. It provides timing, risk-factor patterns, diagnostic support, treatments, and outcomes across 12 cases.

Main limit. It does not quantify absolute risk, identify a risk threshold, or test a prevention strategy.

Would raise/lower. Guideline, label, or comparative safety evidence linking the signal to an actionable rule would raise the score.

Clinical validity

Population: 12 reported calciphylaxis cases after teriparatide exposure of less than 2 years.
Exposure: Teriparatide for osteoporosis; 9 cases reported 20 mcg daily.
Comparator: None.
Outcome: Calciphylaxis, timing, diagnostic confirmation, hospitalization, death, intervention, and follow-up outcome.
Main bias risks: Spontaneous reporting, selection bias, missing data, confounding by warfarin and other risk factors, no denominator, no comparator.
Causal boundary: Signal detection only. The study cannot establish incidence, relative risk, or teriparatide as the sole cause.

Practice translation

For a patient taking teriparatide who develops painful violaceous lesions, nonhealing ulcers, or a clinical picture concerning for calciphylaxis, this paper supports keeping drug exposure in the medication history and differential diagnosis. It does not establish that teriparatide should be routinely avoided, stopped prophylactically, or considered causal without evaluating other risk factors and current guidance.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use now

Use the article as a rare-safety-signal reference and as a prompt to review teriparatide exposure when calciphylaxis is suspected.

Do not overclaim

Do not infer an incidence rate, relative risk, preventable fraction, or causal effect from 12 selected reports.

Read next

Prioritize the four included case reports, the nonuremic calciphylaxis systematic review, the teriparatide label cited for pharmacology, and the warfarin-associated nonuremic calciphylaxis series.

What would change confidence

A denominator-based comparative study with validated calciphylaxis outcomes, time-varying teriparatide and warfarin exposure, kidney function, autoimmune disease, corticosteroid use, calcium and vitamin D use, and adjusted risk estimates.
