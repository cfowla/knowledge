One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In this first reported real-world antenatal GLE/PIB series, 10 of 14 prescribed patients completed treatment. All 10 completers had undetectable HCV RNA at or by delivery; 9/9 with SVR4-or-better data and 5/5 with SVR12 data met those virologic endpoints. No preterm deliveries, new cholestasis, new GDM, obstetric hemorrhage, low birth weight, neonatal death/stillbirth, NICU admission, or perinatal HCV transmission were observed in the available denominators; one completer had nausea treated with oral antiemetics.

The strongest value of the report is novelty and clinical realism: the cohort included Medicaid-insured patients and substantial comorbidity/substance-use burden, and it documents both successful completion and real-world barriers to starting or continuing therapy. Four of 14 did not complete treatment; three stopped within the first week and one deferred to postpartum, with anxiety/fetal-safety concern contributing to two early discontinuations.

The evidence is nevertheless fragile. Only 10 patients completed therapy, SVR12 was available for 5, infant HCV screening for 6, and there was no comparator or pharmacokinetic sampling. Safety ascertainment was retrospective. A source-internal denominator discrepancy also exists for liver tests: Table 1 reports n=9 with 9/9 normal, whereas the Results narrative says tests were available in 8/10 with no elevations.

Section condensation

Abstract and introduction

The paper frames antenatal HCV treatment as an area where shared decision making is permitted but regimen-specific pregnancy data are limited. The authors state that pregnancy data existed for sofosbuvir-based regimens but not for GLE/PIB, motivating this retrospective series.

The study’s stated purpose is descriptive: report outcomes among pregnant patients with HCV who initiated GLE/PIB in the second or third trimester. The introduction positions the work as the first pregnancy outcome report for GLE/PIB rather than as a comparative efficacy study.

Methods

Beginning in March 2024, infectious diseases and maternal-fetal medicine clinicians offered prenatal HCV treatment after the first trimester or postpartum deferral through shared decision making. The review included adults with HCV-complicated pregnancies who started GLE/PIB at 14–32 weeks’ gestation through August 2025. The authors selected this gestational window because the GLE/PIB course is 8 weeks and would allow meaningful exposure before delivery.

Baseline data included demographics, insurance, comorbidities, pregnancy complications, HCV risk factors, HCV RNA, genotype, FIB-4, and gestational age at treatment start. Treatment outcomes included HCV RNA, liver tests, adverse effects, HCV RNA by delivery, SVR4 and SVR12. Pregnancy/neonatal outcomes included new ICP/GDM, preterm delivery (<37 weeks), gestational age at delivery, hemorrhage, low birth weight (<2500 g), stillbirth, NICU admission, and perinatal transmission defined by detectable infant HCV RNA at ≥2 months. The study had institutional review board approval.

Results

Fourteen patients were prescribed GLE/PIB, and 10 (71%) reported completion. All were Medicaid insured; mean treatment-start gestational age among 13 with data was 25 weeks (18–31). Baseline complexity included OUD in 8/14, nicotine use in 8/14, stimulant use in 4/14, serious mental illness in 3/14, and several preexisting pregnancy complications. None had HIV, hepatitis B, cirrhosis, or multifetal gestation.

All 10 completers had undetectable HCV RNA at or by delivery. On-treatment HCV RNA was undetectable in 8/8 tested; SVR4 or better occurred in 9/9 with data and SVR12 in 5/5. One patient had nausea managed with antiemetics. No preterm deliveries or listed new maternal/neonatal adverse outcomes were reported in the evaluable denominators, and 0/6 screened exposed infants had perinatal HCV transmission. The tenth completer had on-treatment undetectable HCV RNA but did not return for SVR testing. The liver-test denominator differs between narrative (8) and Table 1 (9).

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the series as early evidence that antenatal GLE/PIB may be effective, safe, and tolerated and as supportive of shared decision making around DAA treatment during pregnancy. They emphasize practical reasons GLE/PIB may be attractive in pregnancy, including the shorter treatment course and the challenge of postpartum loss to follow-up. They also argue that SVR4 may be a more feasible pregnancy-study endpoint than SVR12 when treatment begins later in gestation.

The authors explicitly acknowledge the retrospective design, incomplete follow-up, lack of pharmacokinetic data, small number of completers, and uncertainty about optimal gestational timing. They call for prospective trials and additional evidence rather than presenting the series as definitive.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as an early signal-generating report and a practical description of antenatal GLE/PIB uptake and follow-up. Do not use it alone to establish regimen safety, optimal timing, or comparative clinical effectiveness in pregnancy.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses antenatal HCV pharmacotherapy with a commonly used pangenotypic DAA.

Evidence basis: pregnancy-specific maternal, virologic, and infant outcomes.

Limiting factor: small descriptive cohort.

Would rise/fall with: broader multicenter data would raise confidence; a narrower population would reduce it.

Novelty
9/10

High because the authors identify this as the first antenatal GLE/PIB outcome report.

Evidence basis: novel regimen/pregnancy combination in real-world use.

Limiting factor: novelty does not imply evidentiary strength.

Would rise/fall with: confirmation that no prior comparable cohort exists would sustain this score; discovery of prior cohorts would lower it.

Method strength
3/10

Retrospective single-center chart review without comparator, prespecified prospective ascertainment, or PK sampling.

Evidence basis: n=14 prescribed, n=10 completed.

Limiting factor: selection, missing-data, and ascertainment bias.

Would rise/fall with: prospective multicenter enrollment, standardized follow-up, and comparator data would raise it.

Evidence strength
4/10

The observed virologic outcomes are consistent within available completers, but the sample and follow-up are too small for confident safety inference.

Evidence basis: 10/10 undetectable by delivery, 9/9 SVR4+, 5/5 SVR12, 0/6 transmissions.

Limiting factor: missing denominators and no control group; internal liver-test denominator discrepancy.

Would rise/fall with: complete prospective maternal/infant follow-up and confidence intervals would raise it.

External validity
3/10

The medically and socially complex cohort improves real-world relevance, but the sample is single-center and all patients were Medicaid insured.

Evidence basis: substance use, OUD, mental illness, and preexisting pregnancy complications were represented.

Limiting factor: very small and geographically narrow cohort.

Would rise/fall with: multicenter replication across insurance, racial/ethnic, and clinical settings would raise it.

5/10

Useful for identifying real-world barriers, follow-up timing, and outcomes worth tracking, but insufficient to define a treatment protocol.

Would rise/fall with: prospective protocolized workflows and validated maternal/infant monitoring data would raise it.

Implementation and practice takeaways

What this addsEarly real-world evidence that virologic suppression/cure can occur after antenatal GLE/PIB in treatment completers, with no major maternal/neonatal safety signal observed in this very small cohort.
What not to overclaimZero observed events in denominators of 6–10 do not establish fetal or maternal safety, and completer outcomes do not resolve treatment-selection or adherence bias.
Operational insightEarly discontinuation due to anxiety/fetal concern and medication loss highlights counseling, medication continuity, and follow-up as implementation targets.
Research priorityProspective, adequately sized studies with standardized maternal, infant, pharmacokinetic, SVR, and longer-term safety follow-up are needed.
