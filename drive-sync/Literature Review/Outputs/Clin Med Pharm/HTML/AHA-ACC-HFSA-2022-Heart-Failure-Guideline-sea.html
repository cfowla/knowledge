One-page synthesis

Core use. This is the main 2022 U.S. guideline for prevention, diagnosis, phenotyping, longitudinal management, hospitalization, advanced therapies, comorbidities, and goals of care in heart failure. Its most practice-defining change is the four-class HFrEF medication framework that includes SGLT2 inhibitors, alongside new SGLT2 inhibitor recommendations for HFmrEF and HFpEF.

The guideline separates disease stage from LVEF phenotype. It defines HFrEF at LVEF at or below 40%, HFmrEF at 41% to 49%, HFpEF at or above 50%, and HFimpEF as previous HFrEF followed by LVEF above 40%. HFmrEF and HFpEF require objective support for increased filling pressures. This distinction matters because treatment evidence is strongest in HFrEF, becomes more selective in HFmrEF, and is more phenotype-dependent in HFpEF.

The source contains 177 numbered COR/LOE recommendations and 12 economic value statements. Of the recommendations, 81 are Class 1, 50 are Class 2a, 21 are Class 2b, 16 are Class 3 Harm, and 9 are Class 3 No Benefit. Level A supports 26 recommendations; most guidance rests on B-R, B-NR, or C-level evidence. That pattern fits the breadth of the document: several pharmacologic and device questions have large randomized trials, while diagnostics, implementation, advanced HF, special populations, and goals of care often depend on observational evidence or expert opinion.

Best use: foundational HF reference, protocol design, medication and device eligibility logic, inpatient pathways, specialty referral criteria, and CDS content. Boundary: the main evidence review runs through September 2021 and guideline harmonization through December 2021. No external 2026 currency verification was performed in this run.

The writing committee included cardiologists, HF specialists, internists, interventionalists, an electrophysiologist, surgeons, a pharmacist, an advanced nurse practitioner, and two lay or patient representatives. The guideline uses ACC/AHA Class of Recommendation and Level of Evidence. The Online Data Supplement holds the detailed evidence tables used by the writing committee.

Evidence-selection boundary. The guideline states that published references are representative rather than all-inclusive. The supplement expands the evidentiary record, but the guideline did not commission an independent formal systematic review for every recommendation.

The comprehensive disclosure supplement reports relationships with industry and other entities during development, including relationships not deemed relevant to the document, and lists section-specific voting recusals. Section-by-section condensation

Definitions and diagnostic frame

The guideline organizes heart failure by both disease stage and LVEF phenotype. Stage A is risk without structural disease, symptoms, or qualifying biomarkers. Stage B is pre-HF. Stage C is symptomatic HF, and Stage D is advanced HF. HFrEF is LVEF at or below 40%. HFimpEF requires prior HFrEF followed by LVEF above 40%. HFmrEF is LVEF 41% to 49%, and HFpEF is LVEF at or above 50%; the latter two categories require objective evidence of increased filling pressures. Figures 1 through 4 make this framework explicit and keep disease stage separate from EF phenotype.

Initial and serial evaluation

Sections 4.1 through 4.8 cover history and examination, laboratory testing, natriuretic peptides, genetic evaluation, imaging, invasive testing, remote monitoring, exercise testing, and risk scores. BNP or NT-proBNP has Class 1, Level A support for diagnosis or exclusion of HF in dyspnea, chronic-HF risk stratification, and admission prognosis. The source also recommends a 3-generation family history in cardiomyopathy and directs clinicians to seek disease-specific causes when the presentation suggests amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis, endocrine disease, tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy, or other treatable causes.

Stage A and Stage B

Primary prevention includes blood-pressure control and SGLT2 inhibition in type 2 diabetes with established cardiovascular disease or high cardiovascular risk. Stage B management emphasizes ACE inhibition for LVEF at or below 40%, statins after MI or ACS, evidence-based beta blockers after MI with reduced LVEF, and selected primary-prevention ICD therapy. The guideline also identifies therapies that can worsen outcomes in asymptomatic LV dysfunction, including thiazolidinediones and negative-inotropic nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers in the specified low-LVEF populations.

Stage C HFrEF

The treatment model centers on four medication classes with outcome benefit: renin-angiotensin system inhibition, preferably ARNi where indicated, an evidence-based beta blocker, an MRA when renal function and potassium permit, and an SGLT2 inhibitor. Diuretics treat congestion. Hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate, ivabradine, digoxin, and vericiguat occupy narrower roles. Figure 6 provides the main treatment map, Figure 7 adds selected adjunctive therapies, and Tables 14 and 15 summarize doses and expected benefits. The source is explicit about harm boundaries, including ARNi plus ACE inhibitor coadministration, a 36-hour ACE inhibitor washout around ARNi use, angioedema history, and MRA discontinuation when potassium cannot be kept below the stated threshold.

Devices, revascularization, and valve disease

ICD recommendations are tied to LVEF, ischemic or nonischemic cause, symptom class, time from MI, GDMT, and expected meaningful survival. CRT recommendations depend on LVEF, rhythm, QRS morphology and duration, symptoms, and pacing needs. Figure 8 maps CRT indications. Revascularization and treatment of secondary mitral regurgitation are addressed in the context of coronary anatomy, symptoms, ventricular function, GDMT, and procedural suitability rather than as isolated procedures.

HFmrEF, HFimpEF, and HFpEF

SGLT2 inhibition receives Class 2a support in HFmrEF and HFpEF. In HFmrEF, HFrEF therapies such as beta blockers, ARNi, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and MRAs have weaker Class 2b support, especially toward the lower end of the LVEF range. HFimpEF patients should continue HFrEF therapy to prevent relapse. In HFpEF, blood-pressure control and AF management are central, while MRAs, ARBs, and ARNi have selected Class 2b roles. Routine nitrates or PDE-5 inhibitors to improve activity or quality of life are designated no benefit.

Cardiac amyloidosis

The diagnostic pathway combines monoclonal-protein assessment, bone scintigraphy, and genetic testing to distinguish transthyretin from light-chain disease. Tafamidis has Class 1, Level B-R support in selected wild-type or variant transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis with NYHA class I to III symptoms. The guideline also addresses anticoagulation in cardiac amyloidosis with AF and makes treatment selection contingent on disease stage and expected noncardiac survival.

Advanced and hospitalized HF

Stage D management starts with timely referral to an HF specialty team when consistent with goals of care. The team reviews GDMT and candidacy for LVAD, transplantation, palliative care, and palliative inotropes. For acute decompensation, the guideline emphasizes continuing and optimizing preexisting GDMT when possible, starting GDMT after clinical stability, prompt IV loop diuretics for significant congestion, escalation when diuresis is inadequate, and a discharge plan that supports medication adjustment and transition of care. Cardiogenic shock receives separate hemodynamic and clinical classification guidance.

Comorbidities and special populations

The guideline addresses iron deficiency, anemia, hypertension, sleep-disordered breathing, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, coronary disease, cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction, pregnancy, disparities, and vulnerable populations. IV iron in HFrEF with iron deficiency has Class 2a support for functional status and quality of life, while erythropoiesis-stimulating agents are designated harmful for improving HF outcomes. DOACs are preferred over warfarin in eligible HF patients with AF. Cancer therapy decisions are framed as multidisciplinary risk-benefit decisions, and pregnancy management is organized across preconception, pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum care.

Quality, goals of care, and research gaps

The final sections connect guideline implementation to performance measurement, palliative and supportive care, advance care planning, hospice referral when appropriate, patient-reported outcomes, and explicit research gaps. Figure 15 depicts the changing intensity of disease-directed and supportive therapies across the clinical course. The source treats palliative care as concurrent with disease-modifying treatment rather than as a late substitute for it.

Strengths

  • Broad, clinically coherent scope that connects prevention, diagnosis, phenotype, chronic therapy, hospitalization, advanced HF, comorbidities, and goals of care.
  • Explicit COR/LOE framework and a large Online Data Supplement with section-specific evidence tables.
  • Major HFrEF drug recommendations are supported by large randomized trials with hard clinical outcomes.
  • Algorithms and tables translate eligibility conditions, contraindications, monitoring, and treatment sequencing into implementable clinical logic.
  • The writing group included multiple disciplines and lay or patient representation, with public disclosure and recusal processes.

Limitations and uncertainty

  • The evidence base is uneven. Only 26 of 177 recommendations have Level A evidence, while 59 are B-NR, 39 are C-LD, and 7 are C-EO.
  • HFpEF and HFmrEF recommendations rely more heavily on hospitalization outcomes, subgroup analyses, and evidence gradients across LVEF than the core HFrEF recommendations.
  • Several diagnostic, advanced-HF, special-population, and implementation questions cannot be supported by large randomized trials and therefore have lower LOE.
  • The source's development window ends in 2021. Later studies, regulatory changes, and newer guidelines are outside this appraisal unless separately verified.
  • The guideline is U.S.-focused. Resource availability, device access, specialty infrastructure, and drug cost assumptions may not transfer evenly to other systems.
Verdict. Read first as a foundational HF guideline. It is highly useful for clinical pathways and CDS, but confidence should follow each recommendation's COR, LOE, population, contraindications, and development date rather than the document's authority alone.
