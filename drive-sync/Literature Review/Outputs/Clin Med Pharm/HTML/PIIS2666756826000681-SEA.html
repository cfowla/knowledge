One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In 1160 adults aged ≥75 years without established ASCVD who had been taking statins for primary prevention, stopping statins met the trial's prespecified non-inferiority criterion for 3-year all-cause mortality. The observed mortality difference was -0.68 percentage points (95% CI -3.95 to 2.60; discontinuation minus continuation). Secondary cardiovascular outcomes, overall adverse events, and SF-12 quality of life were not clearly different between groups.

The result is clinically important because it directly randomised a population for whom continued primary-prevention statin benefit is uncertain. However, the trial randomised 1180 participants rather than the planned 2430, and mortality was 7.6% rather than the assumed 15%. The investigators explicitly state that the original 5% non-inferiority margin was not acceptable relative to the observed mortality; they note that the observed confidence intervals excluded a 3.5 percentage-point mortality difference. That distinction should govern interpretation: the trial supports a shared-decision option to stop, but it is not strong evidence that stopping is risk-free or that every older primary-prevention patient should discontinue.

Stopping statins produced the expected biological effect: by month 3, mean LDL cholesterol was 170.6 mg/dL after discontinuation versus 111.6 mg/dL with continuation, and the separation persisted through 36 months. Despite this lipid divergence, the trial did not show a corresponding clear excess in mortality or major cardiovascular events during 3 years. Quality of life also did not improve, weakening a blanket deprescribing argument based on presumed symptom or wellbeing benefit in long-term statin-tolerant patients.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction / research context

The source frames a major evidence gap: older adults are underrepresented in primary-prevention statin trials, and observational evidence on discontinuation is vulnerable to confounding. The trial therefore tests a clinically concrete question—whether patients aged ≥75 years already taking statins for primary prevention can stop without an unacceptable increase in 3-year mortality.

The research-in-context panel reports that the authors' PubMed search through May 31, 2025 identified no randomised trial of continuing statins for primary prevention specifically in people aged ≥75 years, apart from a deprescribing trial in patients near the end of life. The intended contribution is direct randomised evidence to inform patient-clinician decisions rather than a claim that statins lack biological effect.

Methods

Eligible participants were ≥75 years, had no documented ASCVD, had been prescribed a statin for at least 1 year for primary prevention, regularly saw a general practitioner in France, and could consent. Key exclusions were progressive disease with life expectancy ≤3 months, dementia, homozygous/double-heterozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia, and inability to consent. Participants were randomised centrally 5:4 to continuation or immediate discontinuation and followed at months 3, 12, 24, and 36; the trial was open-label.

The primary estimand used a treatment-policy strategy in participants meeting key eligibility criteria and randomised within 90 days of inclusion. The trial assumed 15% 3-year mortality and planned 2430 participants for 90% power with a 5% absolute non-inferiority margin. Missing primary outcomes were handled by 20 group-stratified multiple imputations combined with Rubin's rules. Secondary analyses included a treatment-adherent principal stratum, cardiovascular outcomes, adverse events, SF-12, and prespecified subgroups; safety was analysed as treated.

Results

Among 1180 randomised participants, 1160 entered the primary-estimand analysis (639 continuation, 521 discontinuation). Median age was 80 years (IQR 78–84); 66.8% were women, 29.5% had diabetes, 77.2% hypertension, 61.7% had taken statins >10 years, and 65.7% had SCORE2-OP ≥15%. At 36 months, observed deaths were 48/604 (7.9%) in continuation and 35/484 (7.2%) in discontinuation; after imputation the absolute difference was -0.68 points (95% CI -3.95 to 2.60), meeting the prespecified 5% non-inferiority criterion.

Major cardiovascular events were 4.4% versus 5.0% (difference 0.61 points, 95% CI -1.84 to 3.06), and the death-or-major-event composite was 11.4% versus 12.0% (difference 0.56, -3.29 to 4.41). SF-12 physical and mental scores were similar. Overall adverse events were 73.1% versus 74.0%, serious adverse events 44.4% versus 44.0%, and non-cardiovascular adverse events 72.3% versus 72.7%. LDL and total cholesterol rose markedly after discontinuation and remained higher through follow-up.

Discussion

The authors interpret the trial as supporting statin discontinuation as a reasonable option for selected older adults receiving statins solely for primary prevention, with decisions made jointly by patients and clinicians. They emphasize that the trial did not show a quality-of-life advantage to stopping and suggest that asymptomatic, adherent patients comfortable with therapy may have little tangible reason to discontinue.

The discussion also materially tempers the headline non-inferiority claim. Recruitment reached 1180 rather than 2430 and mortality was about half the assumed rate, limiting precision. The authors state the original 5% margin is not acceptable relative to observed mortality. Additional limits include open-label design, missing primary outcomes, heterogeneous statin types/doses, possible recruitment of healthier older adults, and absence of ethnicity data.

Conclusion

The source conclusion is that, among adults ≥75 years receiving statins for primary prevention—including long-term users—discontinuation was non-inferior to continuation for 3-year all-cause mortality and did not improve quality of life. Further trials are explicitly requested to clarify longer-term cardiovascular outcomes and higher-risk subgroups.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: a high-value randomized anchor for conversations about continuing versus stopping long-term statins used solely for primary prevention after age 75. Do not use it as proof that discontinuation is harmless, as a mandate for routine deprescribing, or as evidence about secondary prevention.

Validity and interpretation

Strengths: randomized comparison of the actual deprescribing decision; pragmatic primary-care setting; objective mortality endpoint; central event assessment; low crossover (57/1160, 4.9%); prespecified estimands; imputation strategy; supporting treatment-adherent analysis; supplement documents lipid separation and concomitant therapy.
Principal threat to inference: the trial was designed around 15% 3-year mortality and 2430 participants but observed 7.6% mortality and recruited only 1180. That combination widens uncertainty and changes the clinical meaning of a fixed 5-point non-inferiority margin. The headline statistical result is valid under the prespecified rule, but the clinical interpretation should be more conservative than the word “non-inferior” alone suggests.

Secondary cardiovascular outcomes are directionally reassuring but not definitive. They are relatively infrequent, confidence intervals encompass clinically relevant differences, and the authors caution that secondary analyses were not multiplicity-adjusted. Subgroup analyses should not be used to make strong group-specific decisions; many are underpowered and cross the non-inferiority boundary.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use this trial to support an explicit option to discontinue statins in adults ≥75 years who have no ASCVD history and are taking them only for primary prevention—especially when pill burden, preferences, goals, or competing priorities favor stopping.

Do not overclaim

Do not interpret the study as proving no cardiovascular cost, and do not generalize to secondary prevention, familial hypercholesterolaemia, or patients outside the studied eligibility framework.

Set expectations

Stopping will predictably raise LDL/total cholesterol. In this trial, that biological change was not accompanied by a clear 3-year mortality or major-event signal, but uncertainty remains.

Deprescribing rationale

Quality of life and overall adverse-event burden did not improve after discontinuation. For a patient tolerating statins well and comfortable continuing, the trial does not provide a strong symptom-benefit reason to stop.

Higher-risk subgroups

Be cautious in diabetes, impaired kidney function, extreme age, and other smaller subgroups: wide confidence intervals mean subgroup-specific non-inferiority was often not established.

Monitoring / follow-up

The trial allowed statin restart after an ASCVD event and followed participants longitudinally. A real-world stop decision should remain revisable if risk status, preferences, or clinical events change.
