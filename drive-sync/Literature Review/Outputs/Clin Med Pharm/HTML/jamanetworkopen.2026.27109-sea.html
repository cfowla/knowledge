One-page synthesis

Core finding. Among 24,024 adults hospitalized with firearm injuries, 1,236 patients, 5.1%, developed a bacterial infection. Most infections appeared during the index hospitalization. Early infection occurred in 4.3% and late infection within 90 days in 1.3%. Patients with infection received a median 9 days of antimicrobial therapy, compared with a median 1 day of antibiotics in the full cohort. The most frequently identified organisms were Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and enterococci.

The adjusted analysis points toward operative anatomy as the most useful risk signal. Head, neck, or spine surgery had the largest association with early infection, OR 5.18, and remained associated with late infection, OR 2.30. Lower abdominal surgery was associated with early infection, OR 1.77. Liver surgery had the strongest association with late infection, OR 3.83. Index-hospitalization infection was not independently associated with mortality among patients who survived at least one night, OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.16.

Stewardship value. The study supports risk stratification and close diagnostic follow-up more than prophylaxis expansion. It shows that routine antibiotic exposure was short in the overall cohort and that infection risk clustered in specific operative groups. It does not show that extending prophylaxis prevents those infections.

Section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame bacterial infection as a recognized complication of firearm injury against a background of guidance favoring less than 24 hours of prophylaxis for most injuries. They aimed to identify risk factors for infection and test whether infection was associated with mortality. Their prespecified hypothesis was that firearm injuries requiring lower abdominal surgery would carry the highest infection risk.

Methods

The study used the all-payer Premier Healthcare Database and included adult firearm-injury hospitalizations from January 2019 through May 2021. Infection required a positive culture plus antibiotics started within two days of the culture and continued at least four days or until discharge. Early infection occurred during the index admission; late infection occurred within 90 days. Fungal cultures were excluded.

Multivariable logistic regression among patients surviving at least one day modeled early and late infection separately. Covariates included demographics, insurance, firearm type, injury intent, New Injury Severity Score, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, and surgery by body region. Surgical location and injury score were proxies for acute severity. The study followed STROBE.

Results

The cohort included 24,024 patients from 323 hospitals. Mean age was 32.9 years, 85.3% were male, 24.1% underwent surgery, and overall mortality was 8.4%. Median antibiotic duration for the full cohort was 1 day. Infection developed in 5.1%, with 4.3% early infection and 1.3% late infection. Infected patients received a median 9 days of antimicrobial therapy. E coli accounted for 20.9% of infected patients, while S aureus and enterococci each accounted for 12.4%.

Early infection was most strongly associated with head, neck, or spine surgery, OR 5.18, followed among the highlighted factors by lower abdominal surgery, OR 1.77. Handgun injury and extremity surgery were associated with lower early-infection odds. Late infection was associated with liver surgery, OR 3.83, and head, neck, or spine surgery, OR 2.30. Infection during the initial hospitalization was not independently associated with mortality in survivors beyond the first night.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret infections as mainly an index-hospitalization problem, with comparatively few new infections during follow-up. They emphasize higher risk in patients requiring head, neck, spine, or colon-related procedures and note low antibiotic exposure among patients without infection.

Limitations are substantial enough to block causal treatment conclusions. The database depends on administrative coding, infection was defined by a specific culture-plus-treatment rule, procedure type and New Injury Severity Score only approximate acute severity, and follow-up may be incomplete. Residual confounding is likely, especially because anatomy requiring surgery is tightly linked to wound contamination, severity, and procedural complexity.

Source consistency issue. The article text reports 102 deaths among 1,134 patients with infection as 8.3%. That fraction does not equal 8.3%, and 1,236 patients are reported elsewhere as having infection. The adjusted mortality estimate is internally usable, but the unadjusted infected-group mortality denominator should be checked against a corrected source or author clarification before reuse.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High-value descriptive evidence for trauma and stewardship discussions, but not a basis for changing prophylaxis duration or spectrum.

Main strengths

  • Large geographically diverse US cohort with microbiologic data.
  • Explicit culture-plus-antibiotic infection definition reduces misclassification from nonspecific empiric therapy.
  • Separates index-hospitalization and 90-day follow-up infection.
  • Reports adjusted estimates by operative body region.

Main limitations

  • Retrospective administrative database with diagnosis-code dependence.
  • Surgery location and NISS are incomplete proxies for wound contamination and acute severity.
  • Follow-up may be incomplete and late infections may be undercaptured.
  • No antibiotic prophylaxis strategy comparison, microbiologic susceptibility analysis, resistance outcome, or source-control detail.
  • At least one unadjusted mortality denominator is internally inconsistent in the published text.

Stewardship and practice takeaways

Do not extend prophylaxis from this study alone.
The study identifies risk, not benefit from longer antibiotics.
Use anatomy to sharpen surveillance.
Head/neck/spine, lower abdominal, and liver surgery groups had the clearest adjusted risk signals.
Separate prophylaxis from treatment.
Median antibiotic exposure was 1 day overall but 9 days among infected patients. Those are different clinical phases and should not be blended in stewardship metrics.
Late infection was uncommon.
A 1.3% reported late-infection rate argues against assuming routine prolonged post-discharge antibiotics are needed.
What this should not change. It should not by itself change local prophylaxis duration, broaden empiric coverage, or create automatic post-discharge antibiotics for high-risk anatomy. Those decisions require intervention-level evidence, source-control context, and local microbiology.
