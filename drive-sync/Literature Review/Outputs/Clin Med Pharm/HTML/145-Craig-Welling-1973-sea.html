One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Cefazolin achieved substantially higher and more prolonged total and free serum concentrations than cephalothin, and renal impairment markedly prolonged cefazolin persistence because renal elimination was dominant. Reduced protein binding in some uremic patients altered apparent distribution, but meaningful free-drug and urinary concentrations were still observed.

The study directly compared cefazolin with cephalothin after equivalent 500 mg intramuscular doses in six healthy volunteers and four uremic patients, while an additional seven renal-impaired patients received cefazolin alone. Serum and urine were sampled serially; antibiotic concentrations were measured by microbiologic cup-plate assay, protein binding by equilibrium dialysis, and elimination parameters were related to creatinine clearance. Six healthy volunteers and two renal-impaired patients also underwent short multiple-dose regimens.

Key reported magnitudes were large: in normal subjects, total serum cefazolin concentrations were four- to sixfold higher than cephalothin and free concentrations about threefold higher. Mean serum half-life was 1.91 ± 0.23 h for cefazolin versus 0.58 ± 0.11 h for cephalothin. Cefazolin half-life rose steeply as creatinine clearance fell below 20 mL/min. Normal-subject protein binding was 83.9% ± 1.4% for cefazolin versus 65.2% ± 4.3% for cephalothin; binding fell in some uremic patients and was associated with a larger apparent volume of distribution. Hemodialysis shortened cefazolin half-life only partially in the two patients studied. Normal volunteers recovered an average 74.2% of cefazolin in urine by 12 h; even patients with creatinine clearance ≤15 mL/min reached urinary peaks of 175-400 µg/mL.

Best use: historical mechanistic grounding for cefazolin renal elimination, protein binding, and the rationale for extending dose intervals in severe renal impairment.

Section condensation

Background and objective

Cefazolin was presented as a newer cephalosporin with higher and more prolonged blood concentrations than other agents in the class and predominantly renal elimination. The authors therefore expected renal failure to prolong exposure.

The study aimed to define the effect of renal impairment on serum persistence and urinary cefazolin concentrations, with additional comparison to cephalothin, assessment of serum protein binding, and observations during hemodialysis.

Methods

Six healthy volunteers and 11 patients with varying renal impairment were enrolled after informed consent. All healthy participants and four uremic patients received cefazolin 500 mg IM and cephalothin 500 mg IM at least one week apart; seven other renal-impaired patients received cefazolin only. Serial blood and urine sampling extended to 48 h in participants with significant impairment. Two chronic hemodialysis patients were studied during and between dialysis sessions.

Concentrations were measured with a cup-plate assay using Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538P. Protein binding was measured by equilibrium dialysis. Creatinine clearance was calculated from serum and urine creatinine. Half-life was estimated from the semilog decline in concentration; apparent volume of distribution was fit with a one-compartment open model. Six normal subjects received cefazolin 500 mg IM every 8 h for four days, while two renal patients received a 500 mg load followed by 250 mg every 12 or 24 h.

Results

After equal doses in normal subjects, total cefazolin concentrations were four- to sixfold higher and free concentrations about threefold higher than cephalothin. Mean half-life was 1.91 ± 0.23 h for cefazolin versus 0.58 ± 0.11 h for cephalothin, and cefazolin half-life rose markedly as renal function declined. The cefazolin elimination-rate relationship with creatinine clearance was linear (y = 0.02 + 0.003x; r = 0.984). In the two dialysis patients, half-life decreased from 45.8 to 27.8 h and from 32.0 to 20.5 h during dialysis.

Normal protein binding was 83.9% ± 1.4% for cefazolin versus 65.2% ± 4.3% for cephalothin. Cefazolin binding in renal impairment ranged from 61.6% to 84.7%; the lowest-binding group had a larger apparent volume of distribution. Normal volunteers recovered an average 74.2% of cefazolin in urine by 12 h. Patients with creatinine clearance ≤15 mL/min had peak urinary concentrations of 175-400 µg/mL. With repeated dosing in normals, mean peak serum concentration was 52.8 µg/mL and maximal/minimal urinary concentrations averaged 2,200/400 µg/mL. Two uremic patients maintained serum concentrations of 36.9-78.0 µg/mL without significant accumulation on reduced-frequency regimens.

Discussion and authors' interpretation

The authors attributed cefazolin's higher and longer serum exposure to its high protein binding and lower renal clearance. They viewed cefazolin as a promising cephalosporin because free concentrations remained higher than cephalothin despite greater binding. They also noted that clinical trials and safety experience were still needed to define optimal normal-renal-function dosing and the drug's final therapeutic role.

For renal impairment, the paper described three general adjustment strategies and favored a loading dose followed by one-half the dose each drug half-life for relatively safe beta-lactams. This is an author recommendation derived from the pharmacokinetic data and should be understood as historical dosing logic rather than contemporary validated dosing guidance.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper is a concise, historically important PK study with strong mechanistic signal, but it should not be used alone for contemporary dosing or dialysis decisions.
Relevance
8/10

Directly addresses cefazolin PK in renal impairment, protein binding, urinary exposure, and dialysis.

Evidence basis: serial serum/urine sampling across normal and impaired renal function.

Limiting factor: narrow historical study population. Higher if linked to modern PK/PD targets and current renal-replacement modalities.

Novelty
4/10

Foundational historically, but the core finding that renal function strongly determines cefazolin elimination is no longer conceptually novel.

Evidence basis: original 1973 renal-PK comparisons.

Limiting factor: age of evidence. Higher only for historical-methodology questions.

Method strength
5/10

Prospective PK sampling, within-subject comparator exposure, protein-binding measurement, and explicit regression methods are strengths.

Limiting factor: only 17 participants, no randomized treatment comparison, microbiologic rather than modern analytic assays, and only 2 dialysis patients.

Evidence strength
6/10

Large, internally coherent PK gradients support the mechanistic claims about renal elimination and prolonged half-life.

Limiting factor: sparse precision estimates, small subgroups, and limited clinical outcome/safety evidence. Higher with larger PK cohorts and external validation.

External validity
4/10

The renal-function gradient is biologically plausible and spans severe impairment, but generalizability to modern populations and dialysis settings is uncertain.

Limiting factor: small single-center historical cohort and dated dialysis technology.

Implementation value
4/10

Useful for understanding why cefazolin intervals must be adjusted as renal function declines, but the specific dosing suggestions are historical extrapolations.

Limiting factor: no modern PK/PD target-attainment analysis or contemporary outcome validation.

Validity and bias

  • Selection: small convenience-like clinical sample; selection process is not described in detail.
  • Measurement: microbiologic assay and equilibrium dialysis were appropriate for the era but less specific than contemporary analytical methods.
  • Comparator: the within-person cefazolin/cephalothin comparison in normals and four uremic patients strengthens pharmacologic contrast.
  • Dialysis: only two patients; removal estimates are imprecise and technology-specific.
  • Clinical outcomes: the study is pharmacokinetic, not an efficacy trial; therapeutic adequacy is inferred from concentrations.
  • Safety: tolerability statements are limited; the paper itself states that major toxicity still needed evaluation.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use

Use this paper to explain the renal dependence of cefazolin elimination, the steep half-life extension in severe renal impairment, and the effect of uremia on protein binding/distribution.

Do not use

Do not use the paper's specific half-dose/half-life adjustment strategy as the sole basis for current patient dosing.

Key historical signal

Cefazolin showed much slower renal clearance and longer half-life than cephalothin, while maintaining higher free-drug concentrations.

Dialysis caution

The conclusion that hemodialysis only modestly affected cefazolin persistence rests on two patients and historical dialysis equipment.
