One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Cefazolin produces higher and more persistent serum concentrations than cephalothin, is highly protein bound, and becomes markedly prolonged as renal function deteriorates; renal impairment also alters protein binding and urinary recovery.

The study directly measured cefazolin and cephalothin after intramuscular dosing in six healthy volunteers and 11 patients with renal impairment. The most durable finding is the tight dependence of cefazolin elimination on creatinine clearance. In normal subjects, serum half-life averaged 1.91 hours for cefazolin versus 0.58 hours for cephalothin. Cefazolin half-life rose sharply at low creatinine clearance, and the elimination-rate relationship with creatinine clearance was strongly linear (y = 0.02 + 0.003x; r = 0.984). Two hemodialysis patients showed only partial shortening of the very prolonged half-life with the historical dialysis system.

Protein binding was also central. Normal-subject cefazolin binding averaged 83.9%, versus 65.2% for cephalothin. In renal impairment, binding ranged from 61.6% to 84.7%; severe azotemia with low binding was associated with an enlarged apparent volume of distribution. Urinary recovery fell with renal dysfunction, yet even patients with creatinine clearance ≤15 mL/min could achieve peak urine concentrations of 175-400 µg/mL. Four-day multiple-dose observations suggested that dose/interval adjustment could avoid obvious accumulation, but only two uremic patients were tested with that approach and clinical efficacy outcomes were not studied.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract reports four- to sixfold higher total cefazolin serum levels and about threefold higher free cefazolin levels than cephalothin at equivalent doses. It attributes the difference largely to greater protein binding and lower renal clearance, and emphasizes the marked prolongation of cefazolin in renal failure.

It also notes reduced cefazolin binding in uremia despite adequate albumin, a normal half-life near 1.9 hours versus values as long as roughly 35 hours in severe uremia, only modest removal by hemodialysis, and preserved high urinary concentrations at low creatinine clearance. Tolerability and lack of apparent accumulation are stated qualitatively.

Introduction / rationale

Cefazolin is presented as a new cephalosporin with higher and more prolonged blood levels than existing derivatives. Because it is primarily renally eliminated, the authors expected substantial retention in renal failure.

The explicit objectives were to quantify the effect of renal impairment on serum persistence and urinary concentration, compare selected findings with cephalothin, characterize protein binding, and observe removal by hemodialysis.

Methods

Six healthy volunteers and 11 patients with renal impairment were studied after informed consent. All normal subjects and four uremic patients received 500 mg IM cefazolin and 500 mg IM cephalothin at least one week apart; seven additional renal-impaired patients received cefazolin only. Two chronic hemodialysis patients were observed both during and between dialysis sessions. Blood and urine were serially collected through 12 hours in routine studies and through 48 hours in significant renal impairment.

Drug concentrations were measured by cup-plate bioassay using S. aureus ATCC 6538P. Protein binding was measured by equilibrium dialysis. Creatinine clearance was measured on successive days. Half-life came from least-squares regression of semilog serum concentrations; elimination rate was ln(2)/half-life; apparent volume of distribution was obtained from a one-compartment open-model nonlinear fit. A four-day multiple-dose component studied six normal subjects at 500 mg IM q8h and two uremic patients after a 500 mg loading dose followed by 250 mg q12h or q24h.

Results

Cefazolin had higher total and free serum exposure than cephalothin and a much stronger renal-function dependence. In normal subjects the serum half-lives were 1.91 ± 0.23 h and 0.58 ± 0.11 h, respectively. Cefazolin half-life rose precipitously when creatinine clearance fell below 20 mL/min; the elimination-rate regression against creatinine clearance had r = 0.984. In two dialysis patients, half-life decreased from 45.8 to 27.8 h and from 32.0 to 20.5 h during dialysis.

Normal cefazolin protein binding averaged 83.9 ± 1.4%; renal-impaired values ranged 61.6-84.7%. Low binding in azotemia was associated with increased apparent volume of distribution. Normal urinary recovery averaged 74.2% by 12 h; 8-h recovery strongly tracked creatinine clearance (r = 0.926). Patients with creatinine clearance ≤15 mL/min still had peak urinary concentrations 175-400 µg/mL. Four-day multiple dosing produced stable normal-subject peaks (mean 52.8 µg/mL); the two adjusted-dose uremic patients maintained 36.9-78.0 µg/mL without significant accumulation.

Discussion

The authors interpret cefazolin's prolonged exposure as a product of high protein binding and relatively low renal clearance. They argue that pharmacologic properties, rather than antimicrobial spectrum, may distinguish cephalosporin derivatives and propose cefazolin as a promising agent because of higher free-drug levels and less painful IM administration.

For renal impairment, the paper discusses three dose-adjustment strategies and favors a loading dose followed by one-half dose each drug half-life for relatively safe beta-lactams. That proposal is mechanistically coherent with the data, but the supporting multiple-dose renal experience includes only two patients. The authors explicitly state that optimal dosing still required clinical trials and that a complete PK analysis would be reported separately.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best used as a foundational historical PK source and as provenance for cefazolin renal-adjustment concepts. Do not use it alone for current dosing, dialysis removal, or clinical efficacy decisions.
Relevance
8/10

Foundational renal-function/pharmacokinetic evidence for cefazolin and a clear historical link to renal dose adjustment.

Evidence basis: Direct serum, urine, protein-binding, and dialysis measurements in normal and renal-impaired subjects.

Principal limit: Clinical practice has changed substantially since 1973.

What would change the score: Would rise with a specific historical-PK or dose-adjustment research question; would fall for a purely current-practice question.

Novelty
5/10

Historically novel, but most concepts are now established and incorporated into modern labeling.

Evidence basis: Demonstrates renal-function dependence, altered protein binding, and early dosage-adjustment logic.

Principal limit: Contemporary novelty is low.

What would change the score: Would rise if used to trace the origin of cefazolin renal dosing or protein-binding observations.

Method strength
5/10

The study uses direct serial sampling, bioassay, equilibrium dialysis, creatinine clearance, and explicit PK calculations, but sample sizes are very small.

Evidence basis: Six healthy volunteers, 11 renal-impaired patients, repeated within-subject comparison in a subset, and quantitative figures/tables.

Principal limit: Only two dialysis patients; no randomization, no modern assay, limited formal statistical reporting, partial PK analysis deferred.

What would change the score: A larger prespecified cohort with modern analytical assays, full PK model reporting, and contemporary dialysis characterization would raise the score.

Evidence strength
6/10

The main pharmacokinetic direction and magnitude are supported by convergent serum, urine, binding, and creatinine-clearance data.

Evidence basis: Strong renal-function gradients; r=0.984 for elimination rate versus creatinine clearance; numerical half-lives and binding results.

Principal limit: Many claims rely on small subgroups or graphical data; safety is qualitative and clinical outcomes were not tested.

What would change the score: Replication with larger cohorts and uncertainty estimates for all key parameters would raise the score.

External validity
4/10

The renal dependence of cefazolin clearance is biologically transferable, but the exact estimates are tied to a 1970s cohort, intramuscular dosing, and historical dialysis technology.

Evidence basis: Includes normal renal function through severe uremia and two hemodialysis patients.

Principal limit: Small single-center sample, old dialysis apparatus, no modern high-flux dialysis, and no broader demographic description.

What would change the score: Contemporary IV studies across modern dialysis modalities and diverse patients would raise the score.

Implementation value
4/10

Useful for mechanistic understanding and historical rationale, but not sufficient as a current dosing protocol.

Evidence basis: Provides explicit dose/interval concepts and links elimination to creatinine clearance.

Principal limit: Experimental regimens and dialysis findings should not be applied directly to current care.

What would change the score: Alignment with current labeling, modern PK/PD targets, and contemporary dialysis data would be required for direct implementation.

Main strengths

  • Direct serial serum and urine sampling across a wide renal-function range.
  • Within-subject cefazolin/cephalothin comparison in a subset.
  • Protein binding measured rather than assumed.
  • Creatinine clearance linked quantitatively to elimination and urinary recovery.
  • Figures and tables expose raw relationships rather than relying on prose alone.

Main limitations

  • Very small study: 6 normal subjects, 11 renal-impaired patients, and only 2 hemodialysis patients.
  • Historical intramuscular dosing and dialysis technology limit transfer to contemporary IV practice and modern dialysis membranes.
  • Bioassay-based drug measurement predates modern analytic methods.
  • No clinical infection outcomes, modern PK/PD targets, or robust safety endpoint reporting.
  • Many subgroup conclusions are descriptive; uncertainty estimates are incomplete.
  • The authors explicitly deferred complete pharmacokinetic analysis to a separate report.

Confidence in appraisal: High for what this paper itself reports; moderate for practice translation because the study is over five decades old.

Current-practice check

External verification (current label): A current DailyMed cefazolin-for-injection label still uses creatinine-clearance-stratified adjustment after an initial loading dose: recommended dose every 8 hours or longer for CLcr 35-54 mL/min, half the recommended dose every 12 hours for CLcr 11-34 mL/min, and half the recommended dose every 18-24 hours for CLcr ≤10 mL/min. This supports the enduring principle that renal impairment requires dose/interval adjustment, but it does not validate the exact experimental schedules or dialysis findings in this 1973 paper for contemporary care.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Retain

Use as historical primary evidence that cefazolin elimination, half-life, urine recovery, and protein binding change materially with renal function.

Do not operationalize directly

Do not convert the two-patient dialysis experience or the 1973 IM regimens into a present-day dosing protocol.

Useful CDS concept

The paper supports a renal-function-aware dosing architecture: loading dose concept, clearance-stratified maintenance logic, and explicit distinction between serum exposure, urinary exposure, and dialysis removal.
