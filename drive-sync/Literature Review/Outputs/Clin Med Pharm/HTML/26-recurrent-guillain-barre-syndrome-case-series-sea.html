One-page synthesis

Core finding. Among 404 patients with GBS seen over six years, 13 (3.2%) met the study definition of recurrent GBS after exclusions. All 13 had two attacks. AIDP predominated in both attacks, but 23.1% changed GBS variant at recurrence. Mean disability scores were similar between attacks, while IVIg use was much more frequent during recurrence.

The recurrent group was 76.9% male. Mean age was 40.8 years at the first attack and 53.6 years at the second, with a mean inter-episode interval of 12.9 years. AIDP accounted for 76.9% of first attacks and 84.6% of recurrent attacks. Severe disability at nadir occurred in 84.6% versus 76.9%, and severe disability at discharge in 23.1% after each attack. Several paired comparisons were reported only as P > 0.05, without confidence intervals or exact p-values.

The paper is most useful as a rare-disease phenotype description. Its strongest feature is complete paired case-level reporting in Table 1 and explicit follow-up exclusion of five patients reclassified as acute-onset CIDP. Its major weakness is precision: only 13 recurrent cases were analyzed, recurrence criteria are referenced rather than restated, two exclusions for “comorbidities” are unexplained, and the marked change in treatment patterns between attacks confounds interpretation of disability comparisons.

Interpretation guardrail. “P > 0.05” is evidence that the study did not detect a statistically significant difference; it is not evidence that first and recurrent episodes are equivalent.

Section condensation

Background and objective

The authors frame GBS as usually monophasic but note prior recurrence estimates of 1–6%. They sought to identify previous GBS episodes and characterize recurrent GBS in a large adult GBS cohort.

Methods

Over six years, 404 GBS patients were hospitalized at seven tertiary centers in three Balkan countries. GBS diagnosis used standard NINDS criteria; subtype assignment used clinical and electrophysiological findings. Medical records supplied age, sex, preceding events, nadir/discharge disability, CSF findings, and therapy for current and previous episodes. Recurrent GBS was defined according to Kuitwaard et al.; those criteria are not reproduced in this paper.

Disability was measured on the GBS disability scale (0=no symptoms; 6=lethal outcome), with scores above 3 labeled severe. The analysis was descriptive, using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum.

Results

Twenty of 404 patients had a previous acute polyradiculoneuritis episode. Five were reclassified after follow-up as acute-onset CIDP and two were excluded because of comorbidities, leaving 13 recurrent GBS patients (3.2%). Ten were men and three women; all had two attacks. Mean first- and second-attack ages were 40.8 ± 22.5 and 53.6 ± 12.5 years; mean interval was 12.9 ± 15.1 years.

AIDP was the most common variant in both attacks (76.9% and 84.6%); 23.1% changed variant. Mean nadir GDS was 4.0 versus 3.8 and discharge GDS 2.9 versus 2.9, with the source reporting P > 0.05. Severe disability at nadir occurred in 84.6% versus 76.9%; at discharge it was 23.1% after both attacks. IVIg use rose from 38.5% in first attacks to 92.3% in recurrent attacks; plasma exchange fell from 23.1% to none.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret recurrence near 3% as consistent with prior literature and note a strong male predominance. They emphasize that different triggers and different GBS variants can occur across attacks. Proposed immunologic and genetic mechanisms are hypotheses; the study did not test them.

The conclusion is that recurrent GBS occurred in about 3% of this cohort, nearly one-quarter changed variant on recurrence, disability was similar across attacks, and immunomodulatory therapy was used more often during recurrent episodes. The authors also speculate that greater IVIg/plasma-exchange use may partly explain why recurrent episodes were not more disabling.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Worth reading for recurrent-GBS phenotype, differential-diagnostic context, and the complete paired case table. Do not use it to infer treatment efficacy or a precise causal recurrence-risk model.
Relevance8/10

Directly addresses recurrent GBS frequency and paired episode phenotype.

Evidence basis: A dedicated recurrent-GBS cohort nested within 404 GBS patients.

Limiting factor: Only 13 recurrent cases.

Would change the score: A larger contemporary recurrent-GBS cohort with predefined clinical questions would raise this score.

Novelty6/10

Adds multicenter paired-episode detail for a rare phenotype.

Evidence basis: Seven centers across three Balkan countries with patient-level episode comparisons.

Limiting factor: The core recurrence range and phenotype had prior literature.

Would change the score: Prospective recurrence ascertainment or new biomarkers/mechanistic testing would raise it.

Method strength4/10

Methods are coherent for a case series but remain descriptive and retrospective.

Evidence basis: NINDS diagnosis, clinical/electrophysiologic subtyping, defined GDS, follow-up exclusion of acute-onset CIDP.

Limiting factor: Small n, medical-record abstraction, unexplained exclusion of 2 patients, no inferential model or power calculation.

Would change the score: Prospective design, explicit recurrence criteria, prespecified analyses, and robust comparator methods would raise it.

Evidence strength4/10

Direct descriptive data support phenotype statements, but comparative uncertainty is poorly quantified.

Evidence basis: Patient-level Table 1 plus aggregate means/proportions.

Limiting factor: No confidence intervals and only “P > 0.05” for several comparisons; absence of significance does not establish equivalence.

Would change the score: Effect estimates with confidence intervals and adequate power would raise it.

External validity4/10

Multicenter enrollment helps, but the recurrent sample is very small and geographically concentrated.

Evidence basis: Seven tertiary centers in three countries.

Limiting factor: Only 13 recurrent cases and tertiary-care sampling; recurrence ascertainment completeness is not described.

Would change the score: Larger population-based or multinational replication would raise it.

Implementation value5/10

Useful for recognizing recurrence patterns and distinguishing recurrent GBS from acute-onset CIDP, but not for treatment selection.

Evidence basis: Observed variant switching, paired disability data, follow-up exclusion of A-CIDP.

Limiting factor: Treatment exposure differed strongly between attacks and treatment efficacy was not tested.

Would change the score: Comparative treatment evidence or validated recurrence-risk tools would raise it.

Validity

Strengths: multicenter ascertainment; recognized diagnostic criteria; clinical/electrophysiologic subtype classification; explicit GDS; follow-up reclassification of five apparent recurrences as acute-onset CIDP; patient-level paired data.

Limitations: only 13 recurrent cases; retrospective medical-record abstraction; unclear completeness of longitudinal recurrence capture in the 404-patient cohort; recurrence definition not restated; two exclusions attributed only to “comorbidities”; no confidence intervals; no exact p-values for paired comparisons; no multivariable or causal analysis; major treatment differences between attacks.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use for
Recognizing that recurrent attacks can differ in subtype and antecedent event, and keeping acute-onset CIDP in the differential when an apparent recurrence is identified.
Do not use for
Choosing IVIg versus plasma exchange, estimating a patient-specific recurrence probability, or concluding that attack severity is equivalent.
Chart review cue
When evaluating a claimed recurrence, recover prior episode phenotype, electrodiagnostic classification, GDS/severity, trigger, and treatment rather than assuming stereotyped recurrence.
