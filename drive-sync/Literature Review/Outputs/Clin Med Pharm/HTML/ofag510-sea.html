One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In 178 M. genitalium-positive residual specimens collected from six Japanese cities in February-August 2025, NGS with Sanger supplementation found very high frequencies of study-defined resistance-associated mutations. Among 164 specimens with complete three-gene genotypes, none had wild-type gyrA, parC, and 23S rRNA simultaneously; 147/164 (89.6%) carried both fluoroquinolone- and macrolide-associated mutations.

The study's main empirical value is current, multi-city molecular surveillance. NGS success exceeded 90% for each gene before Sanger supplementation: 91.6% for gyrA, 94.4% for parC, and 94.9% for 23S rRNA. Final analyzable denominators were 166, 174, and 176 respectively, with complete three-gene genotypes in 164 specimens. Study-defined mutation prevalence was 22.3% for gyrA, 92.5% for parC, and 96.0% for macrolide resistance-associated 23S rRNA mutations.

The strongest limitation is interpretive rather than technical: the surveillance endpoint is genotype. The authors explicitly classified all substitutions at GyrA residues 95/99 and ParC residues 83/87 as fluoroquinolone resistance-associated for surveillance even while noting that reduced-susceptibility associations are unestablished for some substitutions at those positions. The study also lacked symptom status, testing indication, prior antimicrobial exposure, phenotypic susceptibility, and treatment outcomes. Thus, the prevalence estimates are credible descriptions of the sampled genotypes, but they should not be equated automatically with clinical failure probabilities.

Section condensation

Background and objective

The report frames M. genitalium as an STI pathogen for which macrolide effectiveness has declined with 23S rRNA resistance-associated mutations and fluoroquinolone treatment failures have emerged with parC/gyrA mutations. The rationale for NGS is practical surveillance: culture-based phenotypic testing is difficult, while PCR plus Sanger sequencing across multiple resistance loci is labor- and time-intensive.

The stated objective was twofold: assess NGS-based multi-gene analysis as a practical surveillance approach and define the contemporary Japanese prevalence of mutations in 23S rRNA, gyrA, and parC.

Methods

Residual M. genitalium-positive specimens were collected February-August 2025 from six Tokyo clinics and one clinic each in Kawasaki, Yokohama, Nagoya, Osaka, and Fukuoka. One specimen per patient was included: 178 total, comprising 64 male urine and 114 female cervical swab specimens. Tokyo contributed 106/178 (59.6%). Specimens were fully anonymized; symptom status, testing indication, prior antimicrobial exposure, and treatment outcomes were unavailable.

Target regions of 23S rRNA, gyrA, and parC were amplified by in-house multiplex PCR and sequenced on Illumina MiSeq; incomplete NGS results were supplemented with Sanger sequencing. Sex comparisons used Fisher exact tests and city comparisons used Pearson chi-square tests with p<0.05. For surveillance, substitutions at GyrA 95/99 and ParC 83/87 were classified as fluoroquinolone resistance-associated; A2071G, A2071T, and A2072G were classified as macrolide resistance-associated. Full primer and protocol details were deferred to unavailable supplementary material.

Results

NGS alone yielded sequences in 163/178 (91.6%) for gyrA, 168/178 (94.4%) for parC, and 169/178 (94.9%) for 23S rRNA; after Sanger supplementation, final denominators were 166, 174, and 176. Complete three-gene genotypes were available in 164. Study-defined mutation prevalence was 37/166 (22.3%) for gyrA, 161/174 (92.5%) for parC, and 169/176 (96.0%) for macrolide resistance-associated 23S rRNA mutations. The most common specific variants were gyrA Met95Ile (9.0%), parC Ser83Ile (62.6%), and 23S rRNA A2072G (63.6%).

Among the 164 complete genotypes, 37 (22.6%) had resistance-associated mutations in all three genes, 110 (67.1%) had mutant parC and 23S rRNA with wild-type gyrA, 4 (2.4%) had mutant parC only, and 13 (7.9%) had mutant 23S rRNA only. No fully wild-type pattern was observed (0/164), and 147/164 (89.6%) carried both fluoroquinolone- and macrolide-associated mutations. Global resistance-pattern distributions did not differ significantly by sex (p=0.458) or city (p=0.318), although several individual variants had nominally significant sex or city differences.

Discussion and interpretation

The authors interpret the sequencing yield as evidence that NGS is suitable for high-throughput, multi-locus surveillance and potentially more scalable than Sanger-only workflows. They place the high mutation prevalence in the context of earlier Japanese and international surveillance and argue that resistant strains are widely disseminated across Japan.

Clinical implications in the Discussion are inferential rather than directly tested. The paper argues that macrolide empirical therapy is unlikely to remain effective given the 96% 23S rRNA mutation prevalence and that dual gyrA/parC mutation patterns may threaten fluoroquinolone effectiveness. However, this study did not measure drug MICs or clinical outcomes and therefore cannot quantify regimen-specific failure risk.

Limitations and conclusion

The authors acknowledge a relatively small sample, unequal city contributions, and absence of detailed patient-level clinical data including prior antibiotics and treatment outcomes; they advise caution in interpreting mutation-frequency differences. Additional appraisal concerns are the concentration of specimens in Tokyo, many unadjusted variant-level comparisons, and genotype-to-phenotype uncertainty for some substitutions included in the surveillance definition.

The paper concludes that NGS is practical for multi-gene surveillance and that fully susceptible M. genitalium appears nearly absent in this sample because no triple-wild-type genotype was found. The authors call for revised treatment strategies and new therapeutics; those treatment-policy conclusions extend beyond what this observational molecular dataset can itself establish.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High-value contemporary AMR surveillance for M. genitalium in Japan, with a striking no-triple-wild-type finding. Best used to understand molecular resistance epidemiology and surveillance design, not as standalone evidence for patient-level treatment selection.
8/10
Relevance

Directly relevant to infectious diseases, antimicrobial resistance, and pharmacotherapy surveillance.

Evidence: current 2025 Japanese multi-city M. genitalium specimens. Limit: no treatment outcomes. Raise: linked clinical outcomes and susceptibility testing. Lower: if the surveillance population is less representative than assumed.

7/10
Novelty

The combination of current multi-city NGS surveillance and 0/164 fully wild-type genotypes is notable.

Evidence: high mutation prevalence across three loci. Limit: molecular resistance surveillance itself is established. Raise: longitudinal national sampling or phenotype-genotype mapping. Lower: if later broader surveillance shows this sample was atypical.

6/10
Method strength

Clear multi-locus sequencing workflow with Sanger rescue and explicit statistics.

Evidence: 178 specimens from six cities; sequencing success >90%. Limit: residual specimens, no individual clinical covariates, missing supplementary protocol, broad mutation definition. Raise: prespecified sampling frame, full supplement, phenotype validation. Lower: evidence of systematic sampling or sequencing bias.

7/10
Evidence strength

The paper strongly supports the reported genotype frequencies within the analyzed specimen set.

Evidence: direct sequence-derived counts and complete-pattern table. Limit: genotype is a surrogate for clinically expressed resistance, especially for some substitutions. Raise: MIC and treatment-response correlation. Lower: unresolved assay or classification misclassification.

5/10
External validity

Multi-city sampling improves geographic breadth, but generalizability is constrained.

Evidence: six Japanese cities. Limit: 59.6% of specimens from Tokyo, small/unequal city counts, testing population not clinically characterized. Raise: probability-based national surveillance with linked demographics. Lower: evidence of clinic-specific referral enrichment.

6/10
Implementation value

Useful for designing genomic AMR surveillance; only indirectly useful for treatment policy.

Evidence: high NGS yield and Sanger fallback. Limit: no cost data, turnaround data, full protocol supplement, or clinical decision outcomes. Raise: reproducible workflow, cost/throughput comparison, and prospective surveillance deployment. Lower: poor transferability to other laboratories.

Clinical-practice lens

Clinical question: What is the prevalence and distribution of resistance-associated gyrA, parC, and 23S rRNA variants in M. genitalium-positive specimens collected across Japan, and can NGS support surveillance?
Population: 178 residual M. genitalium-positive specimens; 164 complete three-gene genotypes.
Exposure/index method: multiplex-PCR NGS, with Sanger supplementation for incomplete NGS.
Comparator: sex and city strata for prevalence comparisons; no treatment comparator.
Outcomes: gene/variant prevalence and three-gene resistance-associated mutation patterns.
Not measured: phenotypic MICs, clinical cure/failure, adverse effects, regimen efficacy, symptom status, prior antimicrobial exposure.
Principal validity boundary. The paper's highest-impact clinical statements depend on interpreting molecular variants as resistance. That inference is well established for some variants but explicitly less certain for other substitutions included in the study's broad fluoroquinolone surveillance definition. Do not treat the reported 22.3% gyrA or 92.5% parC mutation prevalence as a direct measured clinical failure rate.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use now

Use the paper as a strong surveillance signal that resistance-associated M. genitalium genotypes were extremely common in this 2025 Japanese specimen set.

Do not overclaim

Do not convert genotype prevalence into regimen-specific failure probabilities or universal Japanese population prevalence without linked clinical/phenotypic validation.

Surveillance design

NGS plus Sanger rescue achieved high locus coverage and is a plausible multi-gene surveillance architecture, though full implementation reproducibility is limited by missing supplementary methods.

Research next

Pair sequence surveillance with MICs, treatment outcomes, prior antibiotic exposure, symptom/indication data, and a more representative national sampling frame.
