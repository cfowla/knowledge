One-page synthesis

Core thesis: TB therapeutics have moved toward shorter, safer, all-oral and more individualized regimens, especially for drug-resistant disease, while the next wave of progress depends on novel drug classes, better dose optimization, resistance stewardship, predictive biomarkers, and implementation.

The strongest practice-facing content is the synthesis of regimen-shortening evidence: Study 31 supports four-month rifapentine-moxifloxacin therapy for selected drug-susceptible TB; SHINE supports four months for nonsevere pediatric TB; TB-PRACTECAL, endTB, and BEAT-TB reinforce shorter all-oral drug-resistant TB options; and TRUNCATE-TB explores strategy-based ultra-short treatment with surveillance and retreatment. PredictTB provides an important negative signal, with relapse/treatment failure of 12% after four months of RHZE versus 1.5% after six months despite low-burden selection.

The drug-development section is broad and clinically useful. It covers next-generation diarylquinolines and diarylpyridines, DprE1 inhibitors, gabfeborole, telacebec, newer oxazolidinones, alpibectir-enhanced ethionamide, later-generation fluoroquinolones, carbapenems/oral β-lactams, and intensified rifamycin strategies. Recurring constraints are QT risk, linezolid/oxazolidinone toxicity, Rv0678-linked cross-resistance, uncertain translation from EBA to durable cure, and limited feasibility of complex monitoring or expensive new agents in high-burden settings.

Section condensation

Purpose, recent findings, and summary

The review surveys recent and emerging tuberculosis therapeutics, repurposed agents, and shortened regimens for both drug-susceptible and drug-resistant disease. It highlights Nix-TB, ZeNix, TB-PRACTECAL, STREAM, Study 31/A5349, SHINE, TRUNCATE-TB, endTB, and BEAT-TB as pivotal evidence shaping treatment strategies.

The author frames the practical direction as shorter, safer, all-oral and more person-centred care, while emphasizing unresolved questions in dose optimization, toxicity, regimen construction, equitable access, treatment-response prediction, and implementation.

Introduction

The paper begins from the persistent burden of tuberculosis and the limitations of long treatment duration, drug toxicity, and drug resistance. It positions recent drug development and regimen-shortening trials as a transition point in TB therapeutics rather than a replacement for prevention, diagnostics, or health-system interventions.

Bedaquiline; delamanid and pretomanid

Bedaquiline is presented as a cornerstone of rifampicin-resistant TB because of ATP-synthase inhibition and activity against replicating and nonreplicating bacilli. The review simultaneously stresses Rv0678-mediated resistance, cross-resistance concerns with clofazimine, the drug’s long terminal half-life, and poor outcomes once bedaquiline resistance develops.

Delamanid and pretomanid are described as Ddn-activated nitroimidazole prodrugs with cell-wall and respiratory effects. The review reports relatively modest QT effects under monitored use, notes their central role in BPaL/BPaLM, and points to endTB evidence suggesting that some effective bedaquiline-containing regimens may not require a nitroimidazole.

The new tuberculosis drugs; structurally related agents

The review uses early bactericidal activity studies as the main bridge from Phase 1 development into later regimen trials. TBAJ-876 is highlighted for activity against Rv0678-mutant strains and lower QT liability, while sudapyridine is presented as a structurally related alternative advancing into Phase 3 for rifampicin-resistant TB. At the review date, NC-009 results for TBAJ-876 were not public.

DprE1 inhibitors

Quabodepistat, BTZ-043, and TBA-7371 are presented as leading DprE1 candidates. Quabodepistat combinations had reported early bactericidal activity and acceptable tolerability; preliminary Phase 2b/c culture conversion was described as comparable to six-month HRZE. BTZ-043 is notable for penetration into necrotic lesions and promising early bactericidal activity.

The most important class-level caveat is potential Rv0678-mediated acquired resistance shared with bedaquiline/clofazimine. The review argues that this could reduce the usefulness of DprE1-diarylquinoline combinations if resistant subpopulations are selected.

GSK-656 / gabfeborole; telacebec

Gabfeborole is a first-in-class benzoxaborole aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase inhibitor. The cited Phase 2a EBA work showed dose-dependent activity with acceptable PK/safety; 30 mg is discussed as a potentially favorable dose using bacteriologic and PET-CT signals. It is being evaluated in multi-drug Phase 2 programs and platform combinations.

Telacebec inhibits cytochrome bc1aa3. Phase 2 EBA doses of 100–300 mg were reported as safe and dose-responsive in sputum bacterial load, but the ability of Mtb to bypass the target through cytochrome bd oxidase makes the effect bacteriostatic and creates uncertainty about further TB development.

Novel oxazolidinones

Delpazolid, sutezolid, and contezolid are framed as attempts to retain oxazolidinone antimycobacterial activity with less mitochondrial and neurologic toxicity than linezolid. In DECODE, the 1200 mg delpazolid dose had the strongest additive activity, while 800 mg twice daily was associated with two drug-related serious adverse events. SUDOCU favored sutezolid 600 mg twice daily for sputum clearance without observed peripheral neuropathy over 12 weeks.

A quabodepistat/sutezolid/bedaquiline/nitroimidazole Phase 2b/c program was terminated after failing to identify a regimen capable of cure within three months or less, illustrating that promising EBA signals do not guarantee ultra-short curative combinations.

Old drugs, new purposes: linezolid and clofazimine

Linezolid moved from resistant gram-positive therapy into core drug-resistant TB regimens through Nix-TB and ZeNix. The review emphasizes that efficacy is counterbalanced by marrow suppression and peripheral/optic neuropathy; it cites contemporary strategies that start around 600 mg daily for 3–4 months and then reduce to 300 mg to limit toxicity.

Clofazimine is described as a recurrent component of STREAM, TB-PRACTECAL, TRUNCATE-TB, endTB, and BEAT-TB. Its major practical adverse effect is near-universal skin discoloration with prolonged use, while its exact independent contribution to combination efficacy remains difficult to isolate.

Ethionamide plus alpibectir; fluoroquinolones; carbapenems

Alpibectir is presented as a resistance-modifying partner that increases ethionamide bioactivation and may permit lower, better-tolerated ethionamide exposure. Moxifloxacin and levofloxacin remain potent TB drugs, but older four-month fluoroquinolone regimens relapsed more often; Study 31 succeeded by combining moxifloxacin with high-dose rifapentine. Sitafloxacin is noted as a possible next-generation option based on a single EBA study.

Meropenem-clavulanate has activity against susceptible, resistant, and anaerobic Mtb, but intravenous delivery and dependence on amoxicillin/clavulanate for clavulanate exposure are major implementation barriers. Sanfetrinem is highlighted as an oral β-lactam candidate intended to overcome those limitations.

High-dose rifampicin and rifapentine

Higher rifampicin exposure increases bactericidal activity, but recent pulmonary TB trials have not reliably converted that PK/PD signal into shorter treatment, and tolerability may worsen at higher doses. CNS-TB is treated as a distinct pharmacologic problem: standard 10 mg/kg rifampicin often produces undetectable CSF levels, whereas 20 mg/kg IV or 35 mg/kg oral dosing has achieved CSF concentrations above MIC in most studied participants.

The review also notes preliminary ESCMID 2025 data suggesting 35 mg/kg rifampicin did not reduce tuberculous meningitis mortality and increased hepatotoxicity. Rifapentine’s longer half-life and higher exposure are linked to Study 31’s four-month success, though emerging real-world data raise tolerability concerns for the rifapentine-moxifloxacin regimen.

Evidence that has changed practice

Study 31 is treated as the key drug-susceptible TB milestone because four months of rifapentine-moxifloxacin was noninferior to standard therapy and was adopted into WHO guidance; the review also highlights use among people living with HIV with CD4 counts ≥100 cells/µL. Post-hoc analyses suggest shorter regimens work best when baseline disease burden is low and drug exposure is adequate.

For drug-resistant TB, TB-PRACTECAL, endTB, and BEAT-TB support shorter, all-oral bedaquiline/linezolid-centered combinations. TRUNCATE-TB supports a strategy-based two-month approach with close follow-up and retreatment for relapse, while SHINE supports four months for nonsevere pediatric TB. PredictTB is the counterexample: four-month RHZE produced 12% relapse/treatment failure versus 1.5% with six months despite low-burden selection, reinforcing the importance of cavitation and residual inflammatory burden.

Rethinking the current TB paradigm

The author argues against treating tuberculosis as biologically homogeneous and proposes phenotype/endotype-aware care. Suggested components include therapeutic drug monitoring, immune-endotype-guided host-directed therapy, multiplex biomarkers, and treatment-duration decisions informed by disease burden rather than a single fixed schedule.

This is explicitly aspirational: complexity, biomarker validation, host-directed therapy evidence, and implementation in resource-limited programs remain unresolved. The review correctly separates the conceptual promise of precision TB care from its current operational maturity.

Platform and adaptive trials

Adaptive and platform structures are presented as infrastructure for evaluating multiple drugs and regimens efficiently. TBTC, UNITE4TB, and SMART4TB are used as examples of collaborative networks intended to harmonize protocols, pool expertise, and accelerate evidence generation.

Conclusion

The review’s final position is that two decades of therapeutic progress are enabling shorter, safer, more person-centred TB treatment, but drugs alone will not end the epidemic. It calls for parallel progress in vaccines, host-directed therapies, biomarkers, equitable access, treatment support, health-system capacity, and implementation science.

Implementation value

Rationale: The article is strong for orienting clinicians to which drugs, regimens, toxicities, and trial programs matter now.

Evidence basis: It preserves many doses, safety issues, regimen durations, trial identifiers, and practical barriers.

Principal limit: It is not a prescribing guideline and some pipeline/trial-status statements may already be dated.

Would change with: Linking each regimen to current guideline criteria, contraindications, monitoring, and procurement/access would make it directly operational.

Main strengths

Dense, current, clinically literate coverage of new drugs and regimen-shortening evidence; repeated attention to PK/PD, resistance mechanisms, dose/tolerability trade-offs, and implementation barriers; useful trial identifiers and drug-development context; balanced inclusion of negative or cautionary findings such as PredictTB, high-dose rifampicin tolerability, and failed ultra-short-regimen development.

Main limitations

The review does not describe a reproducible search or selection method, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or evidence-certainty framework. It mixes peer-reviewed randomized trials with observational studies, preprints, registries, conference data, and preliminary reports. Quantitative results are selectively presented rather than systematically tabulated, and rapidly changing trial/pipeline status may become outdated.

Confidence in appraisal

High for what the paper contains and how it is structured because the full PDF, Table 1, Figure 1, and references were inspected. Moderate for current practice currency because this artifact intentionally did not perform external guideline/trial-status verification.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now as orientation

Use the review to build a reading list around Study 31/A5349, TB-PRACTECAL, endTB, BEAT-TB, SHINE, TRUNCATE-TB, Nix-TB/ZeNix, and key drug-development programs.

Monitor toxicity and exposure

QT liability, oxazolidinone marrow/neurologic toxicity, rifamycin tolerability, and tissue/CSF exposure repeatedly determine whether theoretically potent regimens are clinically usable.

Do not equate EBA with cure

Early bactericidal activity is a screening signal. Later-phase efficacy, relapse, safety, resistance selection, and implementation remain necessary before regimen adoption.

Stratification is emerging

Disease burden, cavitation, microbiology, imaging, drug exposure, and eventually host endotypes may inform duration and regimen selection, but most precision approaches remain developmental.

Do not use for: direct regimen selection without checking current WHO/national/local guidance, drug availability, susceptibility results, interactions, organ function, and patient-specific monitoring requirements.
