One-page synthesis

Bottom line. The corrected main article reports a modest increase in infection with intravenous iron, RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.29, alongside higher hemoglobin and fewer RBC transfusions. The infection signal is clinically relevant, but its exact size is less secure than the pooled point estimate suggests because infection definitions were inconsistent, many trials had bias concerns, and the low-risk-of-bias sensitivity analysis crossed the null.

The review included 162 studies with 39,908 participants. The meta-analysis used 154 RCTs with 32,762 participants. For the primary infection analysis, 64 RCTs and 19,322 participants contributed data. The absolute estimate in the corrected main paper was 16 additional infections per 1,000 treated people, 95% CI 3 to 29. Inflammatory bowel disease was the one reported clinical subgroup with a statistically significant increase, RR 1.73, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.71, based on 6 RCTs and 908 participants.

The same evidence base shows benefit. Hemoglobin increased by a mean 0.57 g/dL. Treatment response was more likely, RR 1.46. The risk of requiring RBC transfusion fell, RR 0.83. Mortality and hospital length of stay did not show clear differences. This is therefore a tradeoff paper, not a simple harm paper.

Version issue. The 2021 supplement was not harmonized with later corrections in the main article. Its GRADE table still reports RR 1.17 and 17 more infections per 1,000. The current main PDF reports RR 1.16 and 16 more per 1,000. The SEA uses corrected main-article values and preserves the older supplement values only as a source-version warning.

Methods

The authors searched major bibliographic databases and trial registries for RCTs comparing intravenous iron with oral iron, no iron, or placebo. Nonrandomized studies were added to characterize infection safety where RCT adverse-event reporting might be incomplete. Screening, extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment were performed in duplicate. RCTs were pooled with random-effects models. Risk ratios were used for infection, mortality, and transfusion requirement, while mean differences were used for continuous outcomes.

The primary endpoint was infection. Secondary endpoints included hemoglobin, treatment response, RBC transfusion measures, short- and long-term mortality, and hospital length of stay. The authors prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses and used GRADE for the infection outcome. This is a methodologically serious review, but the safety endpoint inherited weak and inconsistent infection ascertainment from the underlying trials.

Limitations and source integrity

The authors' main limitation is the one that matters most for interpreting the safety signal. Infection was not measured consistently. Forty-nine RCTs did not provide an a priori infection definition, only one trial reported antibiotic-use data between groups, and none reported positive microbiology cultures. The authors also note variable study quality, repeated-testing multiplicity, and the absence of a direct health-related quality-of-life analysis for the observed hemoglobin gain.

Packet-level source warnings.
  • Results narrative states 311 full-text articles assessed, while the abstract and Figure 1 state 312. Figure 1 also shows 312 with 150 excluded and 162 included.
  • The main text reports 31 RCTs at low risk, 106 at high risk, and 22 with some concerns. Those counts total 159 despite 154 RCTs in the review, so the categories cannot be treated as a clean mutually exclusive partition without further source clarification.
  • The current main article reports infection RR 1.16, 19,322 participants, and 16 more infections per 1,000. The original 2021 supplement GRADE table reports RR 1.17, 19,480 participants, and 17 more per 1,000. The main PDF documents corrections in 2022, 2024, and 2025; the supplement retains 2021 values.
  • The prose says RBC transfusion requirement data were available from 54 RCTs with 12,116 participants, whereas Table 2 arm denominators sum to 12,296. The Table 2 arm counts were preserved in the quantitative atom and the discrepancy is not silently reconciled.
  • The prose reports 15 RCTs and 3,445 participants for short-term mortality, whereas Table 2 arm denominators sum to 2,590. The arm counts are retained as table-derived data and the prose count remains a source warning.
  • Supplement eTable 7 contains row totals that do not equal the two comparator columns for several infection-site rows, including genitourinary 6 + 7 shown with total 35. These values are treated as source-integrity problems, not corrected by inference.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use is as a high-value synthesis of the infection tradeoff around intravenous iron. Do not use the pooled RR as a universal patient-level contraindication or as proof that one current formulation or dose schedule is unsafe in every setting.

Relevance 9/10

Directly addresses a common safety question for intravenous iron across many clinical settings.

Evidence basis: A large pooled RCT evidence base with clinically important harms and benefits.

Limiting factor: The review does not resolve patient-specific infection risk.

Would change: More contemporary, prospectively adjudicated infection trials in specific populations.

Novelty 8/10

It updated prior reviews with a much larger RCT set, infection-focused synthesis, NRS review, subgroup analysis, and reporting-quality assessment.

Evidence basis: 154 RCTs and dedicated infection analysis.

Limiting factor: The underlying question and earlier meta-analyses predated this review.

Would change: Newer corrected individual-patient or harmonized endpoint analyses.

Method strength 8/10

Searches, duplicate review, Cochrane/ROBINS-I assessment, random-effects meta-analysis, prespecified sensitivity work, and GRADE are appropriate.

Evidence basis: Protocol registration and published protocol; multiple databases; duplicate extraction.

Limiting factor: Infection ascertainment was inconsistent and the article contains several internal count discrepancies.

Would change: Uniform endpoint definitions, reconciled corrected supplement, and lower-risk trials.

Evidence strength 7/10

The pooled infection signal is statistically significant and rated moderate certainty, but sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk trials crosses the null.

Evidence basis: RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.03-1.29; 19,322 participants; moderate certainty.

Limiting factor: Outcome definition inconsistency, risk-of-bias concerns, and corrected/stale supplementary values reduce confidence in the exact magnitude.

Would change: Prospective standardized infection adjudication with microbiology and low risk of bias.

External validity 8/10

The review spans obstetrics, surgery, CKD, IBD, oncology, heart failure, critical care, and other settings across continents.

Evidence basis: Broad settings and multiple iron preparations.

Limiting factor: A pooled average across heterogeneous populations may not translate cleanly to a given patient or regimen.

Would change: Population-specific analyses with current iron formulations and dosing strategies.

Implementation value 7/10

The review is useful for risk-benefit discussion and for designing monitoring or guideline language, but it does not establish a universal contraindication.

Evidence basis: Provides infection, hemoglobin, transfusion, mortality, and length-of-stay estimates.

Limiting factor: No standardized infection definition, pathogen data, or definitive causal subgroup rule.

Would change: Trials that pair efficacy outcomes with adjudicated infection and mechanistic microbiology endpoints.

Confidence in appraisal: moderate to high. The primary PDF and supplement were both inspected, main figures and tables were visually reconciled, and the source correction history was incorporated. Confidence is not maximal because the source itself contains several unreconciled count and supplement-version inconsistencies.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use the absolute scale.
The corrected estimate is 16 additional infections per 1,000 treated people, not a large absolute event increase in an undifferentiated population.
Keep the benefit side visible.
Hemoglobin response and transfusion reduction moved in favor of intravenous iron.
Do not overread subgroups.
The IBD signal is notable but does not substitute for a dedicated, adequately powered safety trial.
Endpoint quality is the weak link.
Future work should prespecify standardized infection definitions, microbiology, antibiotic exposure, and blinded adjudication where possible.
