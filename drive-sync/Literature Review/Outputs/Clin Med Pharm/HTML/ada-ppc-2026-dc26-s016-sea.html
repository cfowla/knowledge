One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Inpatient diabetes care should be protocolized but individualized: identify dysglycemia at admission, use moderate glycemic targets that avoid hypoglycemia, preserve basal insulin when physiologically required, integrate technology only with institutional safeguards, proactively prevent hypoglycemia, manage perioperative therapy and hyperglycemic crises with explicit safety pathways, and start discharge planning early.

The chapter’s highest-load-bearing recommendations establish treatment thresholds and targets (persistent glucose ≥180 mg/dL; ICU target 140–180 mg/dL; non-ICU target 100–180 mg/dL when safe), favor continuous IV insulin in critical illness and scheduled basal-containing regimens in noncritical care, discourage correction-only insulin for most patients, and require structured hypoglycemia and discharge systems. Personal CGM and insulin pumps/AID can continue when clinically appropriate, but confirmatory POC testing, staff competency, supplies, and institutional protocols remain central safeguards.

Perioperative guidance combines a preoperative A1C goal <8% with a 100–180 mg/dL perioperative glucose range, 3–4 day SGLT2 interruption before elective surgery, and individualized handling of GLP-1–based therapies because aspiration-risk mitigation evidence remains incomplete. DKA/HHS management is unusually operational: the chapter supplies diagnostic criteria, a fluid/insulin/potassium pathway, resolution thresholds, and a bridged transition to subcutaneous insulin. Discharge planning is treated as a clinical intervention rather than an administrative afterthought.

Hospital care delivery and glycemic goals

Admission, order sets, and diabetes teams

The source frames dysglycemia as an inpatient safety and outcomes problem. Admission evaluation should identify diabetes type when known, obtain recent glycemic context through A1C, assess self-management needs, and use validated order sets. Specialized diabetes/glucose-management teams are recommended when available; one cited virtual-management implementation reduced both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia by 30–40%.

Definitions and treatment targets

Hospital hyperglycemia is glucose >140 mg/dL, while hypoglycemia is <70 mg/dL with level 1 at 54–69, level 2 <54, and level 3 defined clinically by need for assistance. The treatment threshold is persistent glucose ≥180 mg/dL confirmed twice in 24 hours. Once treatment begins, most ICU patients target 140–180 mg/dL; non-ICU patients target 100–180 mg/dL if this can be achieved without significant hypoglycemia. Selected patients may need less aggressive targets.

The key evidence caution is the failure of very tight ICU control to improve outcomes consistently. NICE-SUGAR reported mortality 27.5% with an 80–110 mg/dL target versus 25% with a 140–180 mg/dL target, together with 10- to 15-fold more hypoglycemia in the intensive group. The chapter therefore prioritizes avoidance of both severe hyperglycemia and iatrogenic hypoglycemia rather than normoglycemia.

Glucose monitoring, diabetes technology, and treatment

POC glucose and CGM

POC monitoring is timed to nutrition and insulin exposure: before meals when eating, every 4–6 hours when not eating, and typically every 30 minutes to 2 hours for IV insulin. POC results inconsistent with the clinical picture should be repeated and, when needed, confirmed in the laboratory. Personal CGM may continue under protocol with confirmatory POC testing. The chapter describes a 20/20 validation rule and notes that inpatient CGM evidence is useful for hypoglycemia detection but heterogeneous for insulin adjustment.

Insulin pumps/AID

Continuation is supported only when the patient can self-manage and the hospital has supplies, trained personnel, competency checks, and policies. The evidence base for inpatient hybrid closed-loop systems remains limited; fewer than half of hospitals in one survey had relevant policies. Daily review/documentation and periodic cross-checking of CGM accuracy are emphasized.

Insulin and noninsulin therapy

Continuous IV insulin is favored in critical care. For noncritical care, basal-containing regimens are matched to oral intake: basal ± correction when intake is poor, and basal-prandial-correction when intake is adequate. Correction-only insulin is discouraged for most patients and is contraindicated as the sole approach in type 1 diabetes because basal insulin is physiologically required. Transition from IV insulin includes preadministration of basal insulin; low-dose basal insulin added during IV treatment may reduce rebound hyperglycemia and infusion duration.

Usual starting total daily insulin is described as 0.3–0.6 units/kg/day with several transition heuristics. SGLT2 inhibitors are not presented as routine inpatient glycemic agents but may be initiated/continued for heart failure when appropriate after acute illness and without contraindications. DPP-4 inhibitors may simplify treatment in selected mild-to-moderate hyperglycemia; GLP-1 RA inpatient data are limited and the chapter advises holding them in acutely ill hospitalized patients.

Hypoglycemia

The source treats inpatient hypoglycemia as both a clinical emergency and a system-quality signal. Every health system should have a nurse-initiated protocol, every event <70 mg/dL should trigger review of the prevention plan, and events should be documented and aggregated for quality improvement. Common drivers include insulin errors, kidney failure, changes in nutrition or steroid exposure, interrupted feeds, procedures, and missed monitoring.

Prevention appears actionable: cited bundled interventions reduced hypoglycemic events by 56–80%. Treatment uses 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate when appropriate or IV glucose/glucagon, with 15-minute rechecks until >70 mg/dL. Severe events often have warning signals—84% of people with glucose <40 mg/dL in one study had an earlier <70 mg/dL event during the same admission; in another, 75% did not have basal insulin adjusted before the next dose despite a prior severe event.

Medical nutrition therapy, self-management, and special situations

Nutrition and self-management

The ADA does not endorse one inpatient macronutrient composition. The practical emphasis is individualized nutrition and coordination of the glucose check, meal delivery, and prandial insulin—the “meal triad.” Selected patients may self-manage when cognitive/physical capacity, oral intake, insulin requirements, and institutional policies permit it; home medications and devices still require pharmacy/nursing/prescriber governance.

Enteral/parenteral feeding

Insulin plans should cover basal, nutritional, and correction needs and preserve basal insulin in type 1 diabetes even when feeds stop. The chapter describes roughly 1 unit per 10–15 g carbohydrate for enteral/parenteral coverage, regimen timing matched to feed delivery, and regular insulin in parenteral nutrition in selected cases. Attempting glucose <140 mg/dL during continuous nutrition substantially increases hypoglycemia risk.

Glucocorticoids

Glucocorticoid exposure is common and frequently hyperglycemic: cited estimates place consistent use at 10–15% of hospitalized patients and steroid-induced hyperglycemia at 56–86%. Insulin should match steroid pharmacology—NPH can pair with prednisone/prednisolone, whereas longer-acting or continuous steroid exposure may require basal insulin, with substantial prandial/correction dose escalation in higher-dose therapy and daily reassessment as steroid dosing changes.

Perioperative care

The perioperative framework targets risk reduction without making A1C a stand-alone gatekeeper. The chapter recommends preoperative A1C <8% within 3 months and allows 14-day GMI <8% and/or TIR >50%, but explicitly discourages postponing surgery based on A1C/GMI alone because interventional evidence is lacking. Blood glucose is generally maintained at 100–180 mg/dL, with monitoring at least every 2–4 hours while NPO. Basal insulin reductions and pump continuation require individualized planning.

SGLT2 inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors should be held 3–4 days before elective surgery. Nonelective surgery requires vigilance for euglycemic DKA. A retrospective emergency-surgery study reported DKA in 4.9% of SGLT2 inhibitor users versus 3.5% of nonusers; adjusted differences were not statistically significant, but the chapter notes possible underascertainment and cites other perioperative acidosis signals.

GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists

The source highlights delayed gastric emptying and residual gastric content as perioperative concerns but emphasizes uncertainty: a reliable withholding duration is unknown, and withholding semaglutide for up to 30 days in one retrospective study did not eliminate increased residual gastric content. The recommended strategy is personalized risk assessment based on symptoms, drug characteristics, procedure/anesthesia, and urgency; options include gastric ultrasound, full-stomach precautions, or 24-hour liquid nutrition in higher-risk situations.

Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state

DKA/HHS are treated as time-sensitive emergencies requiring fluids, insulin, electrolyte management, frequent laboratory reassessment, treatment of the precipitating cause, and a deliberate transition to subcutaneous insulin. The chapter notes that approximately 1% of hospitalizations among people with diabetes are for hyperglycemic crises and about 10% of DKA is euglycemic, so glucose alone cannot exclude DKA. One-third of hyperglycemic emergencies are described as hybrid DKA-HHS presentations.

Contemporary outcome data remain clinically important: cited inpatient DKA mortality ranges from 0.2% in type 1 diabetes to 1.0% in type 2 diabetes; HHS mortality among hospitalized people with type 2 diabetes fell from 1.44% in 2008 to 0.77% in 2018. After DKA discharge, the chapter reports a 1-year age-corrected mortality rate 13 times that of the general population. Mild uncomplicated DKA may be managed with frequent subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin in appropriate monitored settings, whereas severe DKA/HHS generally requires continuous IV insulin.

Transition to ambulatory care and readmission prevention

Discharge planning begins at admission and should match destination, diabetes type/severity, patient capabilities/preferences, access/affordability, and the receiving setting’s capacity. Minimum elements include medication reconciliation, structured communication, education, supplies, and scheduled follow-up. Follow-up within 1 month is advised after inpatient dysglycemia; when medications change or control is not optimal at discharge, 1–2 week follow-up is preferred.

The readmission burden is substantial: the chapter reports a 14–20% readmission rate among people with diabetes, nearly twice the rate without diabetes. Thirty percent of hospitalized people with diabetes have two or more days of hospital stays, accounting for over half of hospital costs for diabetes. No single standardized readmission-prevention protocol is established; the chapter instead supports risk identification, transitional care, home health/timely follow-up, education, and continuity of glucose-management technology and medications.

The Future

EHR decision support, practice advisories, hospital/personal diabetes devices, and dosing algorithms are positioned as the next major implementation layer. The limiting problem is not only technology availability but safe governance, performance validation, human training, and reliable transition between inpatient and outpatient systems.

Figure 16.1 — DKA/HHS treatment pathway

FLUIDS
• Severe hypovolemia: 0.9% NaCl or other crystalloid, 1.0 L/h.
• Mild hypovolemia: replace about 50% estimated deficit in first 8–12 h at a clinically appropriate rate.
• Cardiac compromise: hemodynamic monitoring / pressors as indicated.
• When glucose <250 mg/dL: add 5% or 10% dextrose.
• Euglycemic DKA: start dextrose with crystalloid at insulin initiation.

INSULIN
• Moderate/severe DKA: IV short-acting insulin 0.1 units/kg/h; consider 0.1 units/kg IV bolus if infusion setup is delayed.
• HHS: IV short-acting insulin 0.05 units/kg/h.
• Mild DKA: rapid-acting SC 0.1 units/kg bolus, then 0.1 units/kg q1h or 0.2 units/kg q2h.
• When glucose <250 mg/dL: reduce insulin and maintain DKA glucose 150–200 mg/dL or HHS 200–250 mg/dL until resolution.

POTASSIUM
• K <3.5 mmol/L: 10–20 mmol/L/h until >3.5; faster replacement requires central access.
• K 3.5–5.0: add 10–20 mmol/L to each liter as needed to keep K 4–5 mmol/L.
• K >5.0: start insulin without K; recheck K every 2 h.

MONITOR / TRANSITION
• Check electrolytes, kidney function, venous pH, osmolality, and glucose every 2–4 h until stable.
• DKA resolution: pH >7.3 or bicarbonate >18 mmol/L AND ketones <0.6 mmol/L.
• HHS resolution: osmolality <300 mOsm/kg, urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h, glucose <250 mg/dL.
• Bridge IV to SC insulin with 1–2 h overlap after SC dosing in the figure pathway.
• Consider bicarbonate only if pH <7.0; avoid phosphate unless specified severe deficiency/clinical compromise is present.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: source-of-truth clinical reference for inpatient diabetes protocols, order sets, technology governance, hypoglycemia prevention, perioperative planning, DKA/HHS pathways, and discharge design. Do not use it as a substitute for the cited primary studies or the separate ADA methodology document.

Relevance 10/10

Rationale: Directly addresses inpatient diabetes, hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, perioperative care, DKA/HHS, and discharge workflows.

Evidence basis: 21 formal recommendations plus operational treatment pathways and discharge standards.

Limiting factor: Applies to a broad domain rather than one narrowly defined patient group.

What would change it: Would fall only if the intended use were outside inpatient/transition diabetes care.

Novelty 7/10

Rationale: The annual chapter incorporates current device, SGLT2, GLP-1 perioperative, and crisis-management updates, but much of the inpatient framework is established practice.

Evidence basis: Updated 2026 recommendations, contemporary citations, and explicit technology/perioperative sections.

Limiting factor: Many foundational insulin, monitoring, and discharge principles are not new.

What would change it: Would rise with prospectively validated new inpatient technologies or materially different outcome-changing recommendations.

Method strength 8/10

Rationale: The chapter is an expert guideline synthesis with graded recommendations and extensive citation support.

Evidence basis: 198 references and explicit ADA recommendation grades across 21 recommendation identifiers.

Limiting factor: The exact section does not contain the full guideline-development methodology or evidence-grade definitions, which are in separate ADA material.

What would change it: Would rise if the governing methodology, evidence tables, and formal evidence-to-decision process were contained and directly auditable in this source.

Evidence strength 8/10

Rationale: Many central recommendations are grade A or B and are supported by randomized trials, systematic reviews, and consensus evidence summarized in the chapter.

Evidence basis: A/B recommendations dominate glycemic targets, insulin therapy, SGLT2 heart-failure use, and DKA/HHS management; the chapter preserves quantitative trial findings.

Limiting factor: Important areas remain C/E or explicitly limited, especially perioperative A1C/GMI details, technology implementation, and GLP-1 perioperative management.

What would change it: Would rise with stronger prospective inpatient/perioperative trials and direct external validation of emerging technology workflows.

External validity 8/10

Rationale: Recommendations are designed for broad hospital practice and span ICU, non-ICU, surgery, nutrition support, and discharge settings.

Evidence basis: Multiple settings and patient states are explicitly addressed with individualized exceptions.

Limiting factor: Implementation depends on local staffing, technology policies, competency, resources, and receiving-facility capabilities.

What would change it: Would rise with more multicenter implementation evidence across resource-variable hospitals and post-acute settings.

Implementation value 10/10

Rationale: The chapter provides concrete thresholds, monitoring frequencies, insulin strategies, hypoglycemia protocols, perioperative holds, DKA/HHS algorithms, and discharge actions.

Evidence basis: Figure 16.1, Tables 16.1–16.2, and the recommendation set are directly operationalizable.

Limiting factor: Local protocols still require institutional validation, staffing, order-set design, and governance.

What would change it: Would remain high; local implementation toolkits and machine-readable order-set specifications could increase execution ease further.

Main strengths

  • Broad inpatient scope with explicit operational thresholds and treatment pathways.
  • Strong recommendation coverage in glycemic targets, insulin therapy, and hyperglycemic crises.
  • Useful treatment of emerging technology and perioperative medication issues without hiding uncertainty.
  • Discharge and readmission prevention are integrated into the clinical pathway.

Main limitations

  • The exact chapter delegates guideline-development methods/evidence-grade definitions to separate ADA material.
  • Several quantitative findings are secondary summaries; primary-study certainty cannot be fully appraised from this chapter alone.
  • Technology and perioperative GLP-1 evidence remain heterogeneous or incomplete.
  • Implementation depends on local resources, order sets, staffing, device policies, and competency systems.

Confidence in appraisal: High for source coverage and internal interpretation; moderate-high for evidence-strength judgments because the chapter’s underlying primary studies were not independently re-appraised in this exact packet.
