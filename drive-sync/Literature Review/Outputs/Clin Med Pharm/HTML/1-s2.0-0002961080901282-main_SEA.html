One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this retrospective series, patients whose average activated coagulation time (ACT) was at least 180 seconds had faster relief of pain and no observed delayed responses or treatment failures, while lower-ACT cases contained all observed delayed responses and failures. Higher ACT was associated with more minor bleeding but not more major bleeding; recent operation was the strongest reported major-bleeding risk signal.

The investigators reviewed 121 patients with 156 episodes of clinically apparent DVT treated with heparin in 1977-1978. Only 71% of cases had objective diagnostic support. Heparin was almost always delivered by continuous IV infusion, usually monitored with ACT. The average dose was 21 units/kg/hour, but requirements varied widely; among patients within the nominal ACT target of 150-210 seconds, the dose ranged from 9 to 39 units/kg/hour. Warfarin was started in 63% of cases during the first five days and generally continued 3-6 months after discharge.

Therapeutic-response analysis was limited to objectively confirmed cases. Among 98 cases grouped around an ACT threshold of 180 seconds, delayed response occurred in 0/33 with ACT ≥180 versus 4/65 (6.2%) with ACT <180; treatment failure occurred in 0/33 versus 7/65 (10.8%). Mean response time was 2.0 versus 3.1 days. Across all 156 cases, any major/minor bleeding occurred in 27%, minor bleeding in 21%, and major bleeding in 6%. Table I shows minor bleeding 40% at ACT ≥180 versus 14% below 180, while major bleeding was 7% versus 6%. Recent operation was associated with major bleeding in 6/12 (50%) versus 4/144 (3%).

Main interpretive constraint. ACT level was not randomized. The high- and low-ACT groups may differ in clinically important ways, and the study cannot determine whether deliberately targeting ACT ≥180 causes better outcomes. The source also lacks systematic long-term follow-up and had incomplete objective diagnostic confirmation and infrequent platelet monitoring.

Section-by-section condensation

Introductory rationale

The authors state that although heparin had long been used for DVT, the optimal regimen and the relationship among anticoagulation intensity, efficacy, and complications were poorly defined. They therefore reviewed their recent institutional experience, emphasizing factors related to treatment response and bleeding risk.

Material and Methods

All inpatient records carrying a DVT diagnosis at UC Davis Medical Center in 1977-1978 were reviewed. Active, clinically apparent DVT treated with heparin was included; asymptomatic DVT and thrombosis caused by malignant venous obstruction were excluded. The recommended institutional regimen was reported as an IV loading dose of 50 units/kg/hour followed by continuous infusion at 25 units/kg/hour.

ACT was measured at least daily and heparin adjusted to 150-210 seconds, described as about 1.5-2.5 times normal. This was an observational chart review of routine care, not a randomized comparison of anticoagulation targets.

Results — cohort and diagnosis

The cohort contained 121 patients and 156 DVT episodes; 75 patients were men and 46 women, ages 17-80 years (mean 50). Multiple DVT episodes occurred in 20% of patients. Prior DVT was the most frequent reported predisposing condition (73 patients; 47%).

Objective methods supported the diagnosis in 71% of cases: phlebography 49%, iodine-123-fibrinogen scanning 19%, Doppler examination 1%, and autopsy 1%. Three patients presenting with DVT and pulmonary symptoms had pulmonary embolism documented by ventilation-perfusion scanning.

Treatment

Continuous IV heparin was used in 153 cases; one case received intermittent IV heparin and two received full-dose subcutaneous heparin. Monitoring was by ACT in 146 cases, PTT in 5, and no coagulation test in 5. The heparin dose averaged 21 units/kg/hour. Among patients whose average ACT was 150-210 seconds, dose requirements ranged from 9 to 39 units/kg/hour.

Heparin lasted 1-31 days (mean 9). Warfarin began in 63% of cases during days 1-5 of heparin therapy, with heparin stopped when prothrombin time reached twice control; slow responders could remain on heparin until pain improved. Warfarin was generally continued 3-6 months after discharge.

Results of therapy

Outcome analysis was restricted to objectively confirmed DVT. Significant pain relief was the principal response measure; needing at least 7 days to respond counted as delayed response. Treatment failure meant recurrent DVT during heparin, pulmonary embolism during heparin, or unresolved pain after at least a 1-week heparin course.

There were four delayed responses and seven treatment failures. All occurred among cases with average ACT <180 seconds. Of 33 cases at ACT ≥180, none had delayed response or treatment failure; among 65 below 180, 4 (6.2%) had delayed response and 7 (10.8%) failed treatment. Mean response time was 2.0 days at ACT ≥180 versus 3.1 days below 180 (p<0.05). The series included one recurrent DVT after 11 days and one fatal pulmonary embolism after 9 days of treatment.

Complications of treatment

Any major or minor bleeding occurred in 43 cases (27%). Minor bleeding occurred in 33 (21%) and major bleeding in 10 (6%); nine major bleeds required transfusion and one patient had a cerebrovascular accident. No patient died from anticoagulation, and heparin was discontinued early in nine cases because of bleeding.

Higher ACT was associated with more minor bleeding but not more major bleeding. Major bleeding was especially frequent in patients anticoagulated within two weeks of an operation. Women had numerically more major and minor bleeding and patients ≥70 had more minor bleeding, but these differences were not significant. Platelet monitoring was infrequent; two patients had clinically inconsequential thrombocytopenia while on heparin and a third case was attributed to chemotherapy.

Comments and authors’ interpretation

The authors interpret the observed ACT-response association as evidence that inadequate anticoagulation permits continuing thrombosis and that prompt pain relief may indicate more rapid arrest of clot propagation. They argue that some patients require substantially higher heparin doses to achieve adequate anticoagulation and that persistent pain after 24 hours should prompt concern for continuing clotting.

They advocate maintaining ACT above 180 seconds for most patients with established DVT, with dose escalation if symptoms persist, while emphasizing bleeding risk in postoperative and elderly patients. They explicitly call for a prospective controlled study with long-term follow-up and report that such a trial was underway.

Summary

The paper’s summary restates the central exposure-response finding: 33 cases with ACT >180 seconds had more rapid recovery and no recurrent DVT, embolism, or failure to improve, while 7 of 65 cases below 180 seconds had recurrence, embolism, or failure to improve. It also emphasizes that major bleeding was not increased above ACT 180 seconds, although minor bleeding was.

Printed conference discussion and author reply

Not primary study results. Bernard W. Thompson supported ACT monitoring but criticized the limited objective confirmation of DVT. Erich W. Pollak described higher bleeding rates in his own experience and reported therapeutic failures despite ACT >180 seconds, challenging the universality of the threshold. Jeffrey R. Saffle highlighted thrombocytopenia as an important heparin safety concern.

In closing, Kashtan agreed that objective diagnosis should precede heparin therapy, reiterated overlap of heparin and warfarin and 3-6 months of warfarin in their practice, and acknowledged that iodinated fibrinogen testing was cumbersome for clinical use.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon / skim deeply for historical context. This paper is useful for understanding the historical development of intensity-guided UFH therapy and why clinicians became concerned about subtherapeutic anticoagulation, but its treatment threshold and dosing proposals should not be imported directly into current practice.
Relevance
9/10

Directly relevant to a heparin deep dive and to the historical relationship between anticoagulation intensity, efficacy, and bleeding.

Evidence basis: The entire paper centers on UFH-treated DVT, treatment response, ACT thresholds, bleeding, and dose variability.

Limiting factor: Historical assay/regimen and nonrandomized design limit current bedside translation.

Would change with: Would fall if the review goal were only current guideline-concordant dosing; would remain high for mechanistic/historical synthesis.

Novelty
4/10

Historically notable, but the paper itself situates its findings within prior work linking inadequate anticoagulation to recurrence.

Evidence basis: Authors compare their results with prior heparin-monitoring literature and propose more aggressive dosing.

Limiting factor: The central association was not wholly new even at publication.

Would change with: Would rise with evidence that this was the first validated ACT-target strategy or with unique prospective confirmation.

Method strength
4/10

Clear operational definitions and complete bleeding accounting are positives, but the design is retrospective and exposure groups were not randomized.

Evidence basis: Chart review of 1977-1978 cases; objective confirmation in 71%; response analysis limited to objective cases.

Limiting factor: Confounding, selection bias, chart-based pain endpoint, incomplete diagnostic confirmation, small key subgroups.

Would change with: Would rise substantially with prospective randomization, standardized diagnostic confirmation, blinded outcome assessment, and prespecified analysis.

Evidence strength
4/10

The within-cohort associations are internally coherent but cannot establish that targeting ACT ≥180 causes better outcomes.

Evidence basis: 0/33 versus 4/65 delayed responses; 0/33 versus 7/65 treatment failures; 2.0 versus 3.1 days to response; reported p<0.05.

Limiting factor: No adjusted analysis, no confidence intervals, small event counts, observational achieved-ACT comparison.

Would change with: Would rise with randomized target assignment, effect estimates with uncertainty, and replication.

External validity
3/10

Applicability outside this historical single-center practice is limited.

Evidence basis: One academic center, late-1970s diagnostics and ACT monitoring, heterogeneous diagnostic confirmation and dosing.

Limiting factor: Assay technology, DVT diagnosis, anticoagulation practice, and supportive care have changed substantially.

Would change with: Would rise with multicenter prospective validation using standardized contemporary assays and management.

Implementation value
4/10

Useful for historical design principles—validate therapeutic intensity, monitor safety, and recognize variable heparin requirements—but not as a standalone dosing protocol.

Evidence basis: Detailed regimen, monitoring target, dose range, response definitions, and bleeding table.

Limiting factor: The actual target and dose proposals are not validated for current use by this source.

Would change with: Would rise with current guideline alignment, validated nomograms, contemporary laboratory targets, and implementation outcomes.

Main strengths

  • Explicit clinical response and failure definitions.
  • Reports actual heparin dose variability rather than assuming a fixed dose-response relationship.
  • Separates minor and major bleeding and identifies a strong postoperative safety signal.
  • Therapeutic-response analysis was at least restricted to objectively confirmed DVT cases.
  • Authors themselves call for prospective controlled confirmation rather than presenting the retrospective association as definitive proof.

Main limitations

  • Retrospective, nonrandomized, single-center design.
  • Only 71% objective diagnostic support across the full cohort.
  • Achieved ACT is both an exposure and a response to dose/patient biology; confounding is likely.
  • Clinical pain relief is a subjective outcome obtained from chart documentation.
  • Small numbers of treatment failures and postoperative patients produce unstable estimates.
  • No confidence intervals or adjusted effect estimates.
  • Platelet counts were infrequently monitored, weakening safety assessment for thrombocytopenia.
  • No dedicated long-term outcome assessment despite discussion of postphlebitic sequelae.

Practice translation

Use now? No, not as a dosing or monitoring standard. The source supports a historical hypothesis that insufficient heparin effect is associated with treatment failure, but it does not validate the ACT ≥180-second threshold or the reported high-dose regimens for contemporary care.
What remains conceptually usefulHeparin requirements vary substantially; treatment intensity and clinical response should be verified rather than assumed from dose alone.
Safety lessonBleeding risk is not captured solely by anticoagulation intensity; recent surgery produced the strongest major-bleeding signal in this cohort.
Evidence-design lessonAn achieved laboratory target associated with outcomes in retrospective data should be prospectively tested before becoming a therapeutic target.
What not to importThe ACT threshold, historical dose language, warfarin-transition details, and caval-ligation suggestion should not be treated as current recommendations on the basis of this paper.
