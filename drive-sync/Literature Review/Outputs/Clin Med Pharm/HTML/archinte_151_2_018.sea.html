One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon / historically important. The paper is a compact, operationally useful demonstration that protocolizing UFH management can markedly shorten time to therapeutic anticoagulation and reduce time outside the target range. Its main value is historical and implementation-oriented rather than as a modern dosing standard.

Core thesis. A fixed, standardized APTT adjustment nomogram can reduce delays and variability in UFH titration. In 50 consecutive acute VTE patients, the final nomogram was compared with 53 historical controls treated by physician-directed adjustments.

Key results. Therapeutic APTT achievement was 66% vs 37% by 24 h and 81% vs 58% by 48 h. Mean time to first therapeutic APTT was 24.3 vs 56.9 h, and subsequent APTTs were therapeutic 59.4% vs 33.9% of the time. The nomogram group also received more heparin and underwent more APTT testing.

Why it matters. The study made medication-use-system design measurable: standardized initial dose, explicit action thresholds, fixed recheck intervals, and interval-specific rule performance. This is the conceptual ancestry of contemporary anticoagulation protocols.

Why it is not practice-defining now. The comparator is historical, the groups used different therapeutic APTT ranges, clinical outcomes were underpowered, and the algorithm is fixed-dose and reagent-specific. Current UFH guidance has moved toward weight-based dosing and preferential anti-Xa monitoring while retaining aPTT as acceptable when locally calibrated.

Methods and population

Patients and Methods

Patients with objectively documented venous thrombosis and/or pulmonary embolism were referred to a specialized Thromboembolism Service. The final nomogram cohort was drawn from 1989; thrombolytic therapy, warfarin already present at heparin initiation, and heparin-level protocol participation excluded patients. Historical controls came from 1987-1988 and were treated before nomogram introduction.

Nomogram patients received a 5000-U IV bolus followed by calcium heparin 20,000 U/500 mL at 32 mL/h (1280 U/h). APTT was checked at 6 h. Actin FS was used; 60-85 s was the study therapeutic range and corresponded locally to 0.2-0.4 U/mL heparin by protamine neutralization. Historical controls had physician-selected infusion rates and a different target APTT range of 55-75 s.

Analysis

The primary endpoint was time to first therapeutic APTT, analyzed with Kaplan-Meier methods and Mantel-Haenszel comparison. The investigators also evaluated time to return to range after a nontherapeutic APTT.

A success event was a therapeutic APTT on the next scheduled determination after a dose adjustment. Success proportions were compared with chi-square testing. Analyses stopped at warfarin initiation.

Results

Nomogram development and cohort

An initial nomogram was pretested in 20 patients, modified to reduce excessive APTTs, tested in 10 more patients, then refined before final use in 1989. Of 83 consecutive 1989 heparin-treated patients, 50 were eligible for the nomogram cohort. The historical-control search identified 78 patients, of whom 53 were available after exclusions and missing records.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 9/10

Foundational for protocolized UFH dosing and directly relevant to heparin-management history.

Evidence basis: Direct evaluation of a standardized UFH nomogram in acute VTE.

Principal limit: The exact fixed-dose/aPTT protocol is not a current universal standard.

Would raise/lower: Higher if paired with later randomized weight-based nomogram evidence.

Novelty: 8/10

For 1991, formalizing a reproducible dose-adjustment algorithm addressed major practice variation.

Evidence basis: Iteratively developed table-based protocol with explicit monitoring intervals.

Principal limit: Related nomograms already existed locally and were adapted rather than invented de novo.

Would raise/lower: Higher with prospective randomized comparison against contemporaneous protocol alternatives.

Method strength: 5/10

Prospective consecutive application was pragmatic, but comparison used nonrandomized historical controls.

Evidence basis: Clearly specified regimen, laboratory method, outcomes, and statistical approach.

Principal limit: Historical controls, different target APTT ranges, different monitoring intensity, and different heparin dose exposure confound causal attribution.

Would raise/lower: Randomization, contemporaneous controls, identical therapeutic definitions, and prespecified clinical outcomes.

Evidence strength: 5/10

The process outcomes strongly favor the nomogram, but patient-centered efficacy and bleeding were not adequately tested.

Evidence basis: Large differences in time to therapeutic APTT and proportion of therapeutic APTTs with statistical testing.

Principal limit: Small sample, surrogate/process endpoints, and source-internal numeric inconsistencies.

Would raise/lower: Adequately powered VTE recurrence and major-bleeding outcomes with robust adjudication.

External validity: 3/10

The authors explicitly tie the therapeutic range to Actin FS and warn that the nomogram may not generalize to other reagents.

Evidence basis: Single institution with defined assay calibration.

Principal limit: Fixed dosing, reagent-specific APTT target, historical practice context, and no weight-based adjustment.

Would raise/lower: Multicenter validation across assays and patient-size distributions using locally calibrated or anti-Xa targets.

Implementation value: 8/10

The paper is unusually concrete: the entire operational nomogram, timing, hold rules, and escalation exception are given.

Evidence basis: Tables 1-2 plus interval-specific success rates make implementation mechanics reproducible.

Principal limit: Direct adoption today would be unsafe without modern local validation and monitoring standards.

Would raise/lower: Modernized weight-based protocol with current anti-Xa/aPTT calibration, safety outcomes, and electronic implementation testing.

Validity

Strengths: consecutive patients, explicit operational protocol, clearly described assay/reagent, prespecified process outcomes, transparent iterative development, and directly reproducible dose-adjustment tables.

Major threats: nonrandomized historical comparator; different therapeutic APTT ranges (60-85 s in the nomogram cohort vs 55-75 s in controls); more APTT testing and higher heparin exposure in the nomogram cohort; surrogate/process endpoints; small sample for recurrence or bleeding; and multiple internal numeric inconsistencies.

Causal interpretation. The study supports that a standardized system of dosing + faster response + more monitoring + higher heparin exposure was associated with better APTT control. It does not isolate the independent effect of the nomogram decision rules.

Bottom line

Use now? No, not as a literal dosing protocol. Use conceptually? Yes: it is strong historical evidence for explicit, standardized heparin-management workflows and local assay calibration.

Practice translation and current context

2026 external verification. Current ISTH SSC guidance recommends weight-based UFH nomograms, prefers anti-Xa monitoring over aPTT because of fewer interferences and no need to establish a local aPTT range, accepts aPTT when appropriately used, and emphasizes that a local therapeutic range is essential if aPTT monitoring is chosen. It does not endorse a single universal nomogram because outcome data remain limited.

This makes the 1991 paper historically important for three durable principles: standardize dose adjustment, define monitoring timing explicitly, and tie aPTT targets to the local assay/reagent. The literal fixed-dose increments and Actin FS thresholds are not portable to a contemporary institution without validation.

Modern implementation takeaway: preserve the architecture, not the exact numbers: validated initial weight-based dosing; explicit action thresholds; standardized repeat-lab timing; anti-Xa or locally calibrated aPTT monitoring; exception pathways for discordant/resistant responses; and audit of time in therapeutic range, bleeding, thrombosis, and protocol deviations.
