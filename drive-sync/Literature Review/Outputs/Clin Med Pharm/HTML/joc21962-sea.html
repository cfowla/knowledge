One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In older, high-risk adults with hypertension, amlodipine and lisinopril did not improve the primary endpoint of fatal coronary heart disease or nonfatal myocardial infarction compared with chlorthalidone. Chlorthalidone produced at least equivalent major coronary and mortality outcomes and fewer heart-failure events than amlodipine or lisinopril; lisinopril also had higher combined cardiovascular disease and stroke rates.

Section condensation

Background and objective

Antihypertensive therapy was known to reduce morbidity and mortality, but the optimal initial drug class remained uncertain as ACE inhibitors and calcium-channel blockers became common. ALLHAT was designed to compare newer first-step therapies with a thiazide-type diuretic in a high-risk hypertensive population.

The report focuses on amlodipine and lisinopril versus chlorthalidone after the doxazosin arm had been terminated and reported separately. The primary question was whether either newer therapy lowered fatal CHD or nonfatal MI; secondary questions included mortality, stroke, broader CHD/CVD composites, heart failure, renal outcomes, cancer, and selected safety events.

Methods

Participants were ≥55 years with stage 1 or 2 hypertension plus at least one additional CHD risk factor. Randomization was concealed, computer generated, center-stratified, and blocked; allocation to chlorthalidone, amlodipine, and lisinopril was 1.7:1:1. Step-1 drugs were encapsulated and double-masked. Goal BP was <140/90 mm Hg, with atenolol, clonidine, or reserpine as step 2 and hydralazine as step 3 when needed.

Analyses were intention-to-treat. Kaplan-Meier methods estimated cumulative rates and Cox models estimated relative risks; the trial was powered at 83% to detect a 16% primary-outcome reduction with alpha .0178 for the original three active comparisons. Outcomes were collected through study visits and administrative mortality/hospitalization sources, with medical-record documentation and targeted validation procedures.

Results — population, adherence, and intermediate measures

The 33,357 participants had a mean age of 67 years; 47% were women, 35% Black, and 36% diabetic. Mean follow-up was 4.9 years. At 5 years, use of the assigned drug or same class was 80.5% with chlorthalidone, 80.4% with amlodipine, and 72.6% with lisinopril; step-2/3 therapy was common in all groups.

At 5 years systolic BP was 133.9, 134.7, and 135.9 mm Hg for chlorthalidone, amlodipine, and lisinopril, respectively. Chlorthalidone produced more hypokalemia and more incident fasting-glucose-defined diabetes, whereas amlodipine showed a higher 4-year estimated GFR. These metabolic and renal intermediate differences did not translate into lower primary CHD or all-cause mortality with amlodipine or lisinopril.

Results — clinical outcomes and safety

The primary outcome was nearly identical across groups. Amlodipine was associated with substantially more heart failure than chlorthalidone, including hospitalized/fatal heart failure. Lisinopril was associated with more combined CVD and heart failure, plus a nominally higher stroke rate; hospitalized/treated angina and coronary revascularization were also higher or borderline higher with lisinopril.

ESRD, cancer, and gastrointestinal-bleeding hospitalization did not differ significantly in the principal pairwise comparisons. Angioedema was markedly more frequent with lisinopril (38/9054, 0.4%) than chlorthalidone (8/15255, 0.1%), with one fatal angioedema event in the lisinopril group.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpreted chlorthalidone as superior for preventing one or more major forms of CVD and less expensive, emphasizing the heart-failure advantage and the absence of a primary CHD or survival advantage for amlodipine or lisinopril. They argued that thiazide-type diuretics should be considered first for pharmacologic therapy and included in multidrug regimens when possible.

They also acknowledged unequal BP reduction, an artificial step-up regimen, and lack of evaluation of newer agents. Direct evidence applies to the three specific drugs; extension to broader drug classes was explicitly an inference. The trial's subgroup findings were generally stable for amlodipine, while lisinopril showed race interactions for stroke and combined CVD and a categorical age interaction for combined CHD.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-value landmark comparative-effectiveness trial because it directly tests patient-centered cardiovascular outcomes in a very large randomized population and materially separates chlorthalidone from amlodipine and lisinopril on heart failure and selected secondary outcomes.
Relevance
10/10

Rationale: directly addresses first-step antihypertensive pharmacotherapy and major clinical outcomes.

Evidence basis: 33,357-patient head-to-head RCT. Limiting factor: historical treatment landscape. Would raise/lower: current-guideline alignment would raise implementation relevance; substantial shifts in present therapy would lower it.

Novelty
9/10

Rationale: unusually large direct comparison of newer drug classes with a thiazide-type diuretic.

Evidence basis: three major first-step strategies, long follow-up, hard outcomes. Limiting factor: class inference extends beyond the tested agents. Would raise/lower: direct testing of more agents/classes would raise it.

Method strength
9/10

Rationale: randomized, double-blind, active-controlled, multicenter, concealed allocation, intention-to-treat, excellent follow-up.

Evidence basis: explicit randomization, masking, power, Cox/Kaplan-Meier methods. Limiting factor: unequal achieved BP, treatment crossover, and a somewhat artificial add-on regimen. Would raise/lower: equal BP control and more neutral cointerventions would raise it.

Evidence strength
9/10

Rationale: hard clinical endpoints with large event counts and narrow confidence intervals support the main claims.

Evidence basis: 2,956 primary events and large secondary-event totals. Limiting factor: multiplicity among secondary/subgroup analyses; some nominal findings are less robust than the primary/null and HF findings. Would raise/lower: prespecified multiplicity control for all secondary/subgroup claims would raise confidence.

External validity
8/10

Rationale: diverse, practice-based enrollment across 623 North American centers supports broad applicability to older high-risk hypertensive adults.

Evidence basis: substantial representation of women, Black participants, and people with diabetes. Limiting factor: age ≥55, high-risk inclusion, symptomatic HF/LVEF<35% exclusion, and older treatment combinations. Would raise/lower: replication in younger/lower-risk populations and contemporary regimens would raise it.

Implementation value
9/10

Rationale: clear doses, BP target, add-on strategy, safety signals, and outcome tradeoffs make the trial highly actionable as historical evidence.

Evidence basis: explicit dosing and clinically interpretable event rates. Limiting factor: current practice status was not verified in this task. Would raise/lower: linking to current guideline recommendations and contemporary comparators would raise present-day use value.

Validity and bias considerations

  • Randomization/masking: strong. Concealed computer-generated randomization and double-masked step-1 drugs reduce selection and performance bias.
  • Follow-up: strong. Vital status and person-years were highly complete, although study-drug discontinuation and crossovers were substantial by year 5.
  • Outcome ascertainment: good but not uniform full central adjudication. Investigator diagnoses were supported by records and targeted validation; double blinding reduces differential ascertainment risk.
  • Co-intervention/BP separation: important. Lisinopril achieved higher systolic BP, especially in Black participants; statistical adjustment only partially reduced stroke/HF differences and cannot fully remove treatment-strategy confounding created after randomization.
  • Multiplicity: important for interpretation of P=.02 stroke and subgroup interactions. The primary null result and large heart-failure differences are more robust than isolated secondary/subgroup signals.
  • Correction handling: corrected Table 6 changes cause-of-death counts but explicitly does not change overall results or conclusions; corrected total deaths align with the all-cause mortality counts in Table 5.

Funding and conflicts

NHLBI contract NO1-HC-35130 supported the trial and NHLBI was involved in data collection, analysis, interpretation, and the submission decision, though not direct center operations. Pfizer, AstraZeneca, and Bristol-Myers Squibb supplied study medications, Pfizer provided financial support, and numerous author financial relationships with pharmaceutical companies were disclosed. These disclosures warrant transparency but do not by themselves override the randomized design or the direction of the observed results.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What the trial supportsChlorthalidone was not inferior on the primary CHD endpoint or survival and had fewer HF events than amlodipine or lisinopril in this population.
Most durable signalThe heart-failure disadvantage of amlodipine and lisinopril versus chlorthalidone is large, consistent across key analyses, and clinically important.
Interpret lisinopril carefullyHigher stroke and combined-CVD rates occurred alongside higher achieved systolic BP and poorer BP response in Black participants.
Metabolic tradeoffChlorthalidone produced more hypokalemia and fasting-glucose-defined diabetes, but these did not translate into worse primary CHD or mortality during trial follow-up.
Do not overgeneralizeDirect evidence is for chlorthalidone, amlodipine, and lisinopril under this trial's add-on regimen; broader class conclusions are partly inferential.
Do not use alone for current practiceThis artifact does not establish current first-line hypertension guidance or contemporary comparative effectiveness versus agents not tested in ALLHAT.
