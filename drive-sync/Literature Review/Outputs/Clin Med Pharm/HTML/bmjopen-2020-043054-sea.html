One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Forty-two RCTs with 11,305 participants were pooled. Probiotics reduced AAD versus any control (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.54-0.73; p<0.00001), with an author-reported NNT of 20. Statistical heterogeneity was substantial (I²=60%), and study methods varied in probiotic species/strain, dose, formulation, timing, antibiotic exposure, populations, and AAD definitions.

Modifiers of effect. Benefit differed by comparator: placebo-controlled trials had RR 0.69 (0.60-0.80), whereas no-treatment controls had RR 0.35 (0.27-0.47). Four direct dose-comparison trials favored higher doses (RR 0.54, 0.38-0.76). Multiple Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacillus, and S. boulardii subgroups were significant, but several species were not. AAD reduction was absent in the low-baseline-risk subgroup (RR 1.05, 0.87-1.27), while moderate- and high-risk subgroups benefited. In the six studies rated low risk of bias in all domains, the pooled effect was not statistically significant (RR 0.78, 0.57-1.07).

Bottom line: The review supports a class-level signal that selected probiotic regimens can reduce AAD in adults, especially where baseline AAD risk is moderate or high. Confidence in a universal practice recommendation is limited by substantial heterogeneity, strain/dose mixing, outcome-definition variability, a null result in the strictly low-risk-of-bias subset, and incomplete safety reporting. The source is most useful for identifying effect modifiers and candidate strains rather than for treating “probiotics” as a single interchangeable intervention.

Section condensation

Introduction

The review frames AAD as a common consequence of antibiotic-mediated microbiome disruption and a source of morbidity, longer hospital stays, and cost. Probiotics are proposed to restore microbial balance, inhibit pathogens, and influence host immune responses. The authors distinguish AAD from C. difficile-associated diarrhoea and target all-cause AAD because a causative organism is often not identified.

The primary objective was to assess the protective effect of probiotics against AAD in adults; secondary objectives were dose-response and species-specific effects.

Methods

Eligible studies were English-language RCTs in adults receiving antibiotics for any indication or duration, comparing any probiotic strain/dose/formulation with placebo, alternative probiotic dose or strain, or no treatment. The primary outcome was AAD incidence as defined by each trial during antibiotic therapy or follow-up. A title search of CINAHL Plus, EMBASE, MEDLINE (Ovid), Web of Science, and Google Scholar covered database inception to May 2021, supplemented by trial registries, grey literature, and reference-list searching.

Screening and extraction were duplicated among three reviewers; Covidence was used. Risk of bias used the Cochrane tool, and GRADE assessed evidence quality. All analyses used random-effects risk ratios with 95% CIs; NNT and I² were reported. Dose, species, and risk of bias were planned heterogeneity variables; baseline AAD risk and H. pylori eradication were post hoc subgroup analyses. The supplemental protocol specified age ≥18, while the final paper reports an included age range of 15-85.

Results

The paper reports 42 RCTs and 11,305 participants. Thirty-three trials used placebo controls and eight used no treatment; one study included both comparator types. Only six trials were rated low risk of bias, while 36 were high or unclear. The primary pooled RR was 0.63 (0.54-0.73), NNT 20, with I²=60%.

Placebo-controlled trials showed a smaller effect than no-treatment trials. Higher dose versus lower dose of the same probiotic favored higher dose in four trials. Species effects were heterogeneous. H. pylori eradication trials favored probiotics (RR 0.55). Benefit was absent at low baseline AAD risk but present at moderate/high baseline risk. Restriction to six low-risk-of-bias studies removed statistical significance. Thirty-two trials reported adverse events; no serious adverse events were reported among those studies.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the evidence as a clinically meaningful protective effect, strengthened by a dose-response gradient and some consistent species signals. They emphasize that the low-baseline-risk subgroup did not benefit and that the overall effect may therefore not apply to settings where AAD is uncommon.

Major limitations reported by the authors include reliance on AAD incidence alone rather than duration/severity/hospitalisation/quality of life, inability to stratify by antibiotic because antibiotic details were often missing, English-only eligibility, and use of a title search. Independent appraisal also places substantial weight on the null low-risk-of-bias sensitivity analysis and on the difficulty of assigning species-specific causality when many trials used multi-species products.

Conclusions

The authors conclude that probiotics have a moderate protective effect against AAD and report an NNT of 20. They recommend caution in low-baseline-risk clinical settings and suggest that higher doses, including >10 billion CFU/day, may be more effective.

They highlight L. acidophilus, L. casei, and S. boulardii because these had moderate effects across more trials than several species with larger point estimates but sparse evidence. This source does not establish interchangeability across strains or formulations.

Supplemental material

The supplemental file contains the original review protocol, detailed title-field search strategy, and 28 subgroup/sensitivity forest plots. The protocol confirms ≥18-year eligibility and planned RCT-only inclusion. The forest plots provide denominators and event counts for comparator, dose, species, H. pylori, baseline-risk, and risk-of-bias analyses.

Two source-level inconsistencies are notable: the PRISMA diagram shows 352 database records plus 4 additional records (356 total), while its caption states 352 total initial articles; and the protocol specifies participants ≥18 years while the final results report an overall age range starting at 15 years.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. The review is useful for understanding the adult AAD probiotic evidence base and for identifying effect modifiers, but it should not be converted into a universal “use any probiotic” practice rule.

Relevance — 8/10

Directly addresses prevention of a common antibiotic-related adverse effect with practical inpatient and outpatient implications.

Evidence basis: 42 RCTs, 11,305 participants, NNT and clinically interpretable subgroup effects.

Principal limit: Product/strain heterogeneity prevents a single formulary-ready intervention definition.

Would raise/lower: Standardized strain-specific trials linked to antibiotic class and baseline risk would raise applicability.

Novelty — 6/10

The review updates prior meta-analytic evidence through May 2021 and adds dose, species, baseline-risk, and H. pylori subgroup analyses.

Evidence basis: Five studies were published after the cited 2017 Cochrane review, and 18 additional studies were included relative to that review.

Principal limit: The central class-level efficacy signal was already established in earlier syntheses.

Would raise/lower: Stronger strain-by-antibiotic interaction evidence would add novelty.

Method strength — 6/10

Duplicate screening/extraction, Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment, random-effects pooling, trial-registry/grey-literature searching, and GRADE are strengths.

Evidence basis: Explicit methods and supplemental protocol/search strategy.

Principal limit: English-only title searching may reduce retrieval; baseline-risk and H. pylori analyses were post hoc; protocol/final age criteria are not fully reconciled.

Would raise/lower: Keyword/full-text search strategy, registered protocol with explicit analytic commitments, and resolved eligibility discrepancies would strengthen methods.

Evidence strength — 6/10

The pooled effect is statistically robust overall and in many subgroups, but certainty is reduced by substantial heterogeneity and risk-of-bias sensitivity.

Evidence basis: Overall RR 0.63 (0.54-0.73), I²=60%; only 6/42 trials low risk, and low-risk-only RR 0.78 (0.57-1.07).

Principal limit: The most methodologically restrictive sensitivity analysis is null.

Would raise/lower: More low-risk, adequately powered, strain-specific RCTs with consistent AAD definitions would raise confidence.

External validity — 6/10

The evidence spans inpatient/outpatient settings, multiple ages, antibiotic indications, and probiotic regimens, but that breadth also creates heterogeneity.

Evidence basis: 42 RCTs with varied settings and populations; effect depended strongly on baseline AAD risk.

Principal limit: Results do not generalize cleanly to low-risk patients or to unstudied strains/formulations.

Would raise/lower: Better reporting by antibiotic class and pragmatic baseline-risk-stratified trials would raise generalizability.

Implementation value — 6/10

The source provides NNT, dose-response, species signals, and baseline-risk thresholds that can inform protocol design.

Evidence basis: NNT 20 overall; low-risk subgroup null; four direct dose comparisons favored higher doses.

Principal limit: The review cannot identify one universally effective product, and safety reporting was incomplete.

Would raise/lower: Reproducible strain/formulation specifications, antibiotic-specific effects, and stronger safety ascertainment would raise implementation value.

Systematic-review validity

Strengths
  • 42 RCTs and 11,305 participants.
  • Duplicate screening and extraction.
  • Cochrane risk-of-bias and GRADE frameworks.
  • Random-effects analyses with heterogeneity reporting.
  • Trial registries/grey literature and hand searching.
  • Supplemental protocol and detailed forest plots available.
Major limitations
  • Only 6/42 studies low risk of bias.
  • Low-risk-only sensitivity analysis not significant.
  • I²=60% overall plus substantial clinical heterogeneity.
  • Variable AAD definitions and mixed multi-species products.
  • English-only title search.
  • Post hoc baseline-risk and H. pylori analyses.
  • Safety not reported in 10/42 trials.
  • Protocol ≥18 years versus reported included age range beginning at 15.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What the source supports: Selected probiotic regimens can reduce AAD in adult antibiotic recipients, with the clearest applicability in populations whose baseline AAD risk is moderate or high.

What it does not support: Treating all probiotics as interchangeable, assuming benefit in low-risk populations, choosing a product solely from a genus-level subgroup result, or inferring robust safety from the absence of serious adverse events when 10 trials did not report adverse events.

Clinical translation from this source alone: Any protocol built from this review should be strain/formulation specific, target patients with meaningful baseline AAD risk, and avoid overinterpreting the >10 billion CFU/day suggestion because it derives from only four direct dose-comparison trials with differing dose contrasts. Current guideline or formulary alignment requires separate contemporary verification.
