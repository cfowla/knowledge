One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Early recognition, dental source control, selective imaging, and prompt airway escalation are the central management principles for odontogenic infections. Most uncomplicated disease can be evaluated clinically or with POCUS, while contrast CT and inpatient treatment are reserved for complex spread, high-risk patients, or airway concern.

The review links clinical behavior to anatomy: tooth-root position and fascial planes determine whether infection remains localized or spreads into sublingual, submandibular, deep neck, sinonasal, mediastinal, or bony compartments. Polymicrobial oral flora supports broad initial antimicrobial coverage, but the source repeatedly emphasizes that antibiotics alone do not replace extraction or drainage when a dental nidus or abscess remains.

The most consequential escalation point is airway compromise. Ludwig angina, deep-space extension, and necrotizing soft-tissue infection require immediate recognition, controlled airway assessment, IV therapy, and surgical involvement. The chapter is useful as an orientation and triage reference, but its practice authority is limited by narrative methods, lack of dosing detail, and absence of explicit evidence grading.

Section condensation

Introduction

The chapter frames odontogenic infection as a common dental-origin process that can extend along fascial planes into facial and deep neck spaces. In adults, odontogenic disease is described as the leading source of deep neck infection, making early recognition and interprofessional management important.

Anatomy

Spread is governed by tooth-root position and fascial anatomy. Maxillary canine and molar roots can seed canine, buccal, or superficial facial spaces. For mandibular teeth, the mylohyoid line is a key boundary: infection above it tends toward the sublingual space, while roots below it favor the submandibular space; communication among sublingual, submandibular, and submental spaces facilitates extension.

Microbiology

The oral cavity contains diverse aerobic and anaerobic organisms. Streptococcus species are prominent, with additional gram-positive and gram-negative flora including Prevotella and Fusobacterium. The chapter emphasizes that odontogenic infections are usually polymicrobial, supporting broad initial antimicrobial coverage rather than narrow therapy based only on a single isolate.

Etiology

Dental caries is presented as the leading antecedent, with periapical abscess as the most common odontogenic infection. Treatment combines antimicrobial therapy with source control; definitive dental extraction is described as the standard source-control strategy, although selected teeth may be preserved with endodontic treatment.

Epidemiology

The source reports a high lifetime burden of dental caries and describes odontogenic infections as more common in men and in adults aged 20-50 years. Tobacco use and diabetes are highlighted as common comorbid associations. These are secondary summaries rather than estimates generated by this chapter.

History and Physical

Typical presentation is one to two weeks of worsening dental pain followed by facial or neck swelling, often with fever, malaise, and reduced oral intake. Odynophagia, trismus, neck or facial pain, dyspnea, sialorrhea, ocular symptoms, or paresthesias can indicate extension and should broaden the examination, including neck and cranial nerve assessment.

Evaluation

Straightforward early cellulitis can often be diagnosed clinically. Point-of-care ultrasound is the preferred adjunct when abscess is uncertain. CT with intravenous contrast is reserved for situations where ultrasound is limited or where complex abscess or deep-space spread is suspected. Stridor, dyspnea, marked oral/neck swelling, or respiratory distress triggers controlled airway evaluation, with awake flexible laryngoscopy emphasized for assessing impending obstruction.

Treatment / Management - Cellulitis

The chapter recommends broad-spectrum antibiotics for polymicrobial odontogenic cellulitis and lists amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin in penicillin-allergic patients as common choices. It gives no dosing, renal/hepatic adjustment, duration, susceptibility framework, or comparative trial evidence. Timely extraction of the offending necrotic tooth is emphasized as definitive source control.

Treatment / Management - Abscess

Fluctuance, mass effect, or suppurative sockets favor abscess. Minor collections can often be identified by POCUS without CT. Drainage is recommended for source control and symptom relief, with transoral drainage preferred when feasible; aspiration, incision and drainage, and temporary drains may be combined. Cultures are suggested but are described as infrequently management-changing because of polymicrobial flora. Extensive disease, immunodeficiency, or important comorbidity favors IV antibiotics, resuscitation, and inpatient monitoring.

Ludwig Angina

Ludwig angina is presented as rapidly spreading bilateral cellulitis of the submandibular, submental, and sublingual spaces with potential tongue elevation and airway catastrophe. Respiratory distress, drooling, and a bullfrog neck appearance are classic findings. Airway assessment and stabilization come first, followed by broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and admission; surgical airway management or infectious drainage/debridement may be necessary.

Odontogenic Sinusitis

The source reports that odontogenic disease accounts for nearly 40% of acute bacterial maxillary sinusitis. Unilateral isolated maxillary sinusitis is described as a useful clue to dental origin. Management centers on extraction of an infected tooth or implant, antimicrobial therapy, and endoscopic sinus surgery when required; persistent oroantral fistula repair is generally deferred until infection has resolved.

Differential Diagnosis

Recurrent infection in an otherwise healthy patient, lack of a clear infected tooth, or questionable dental history should prompt consideration of malignancy and non-dental adjacent sources such as sinonasal, temporal-bone, neck-mass, or aerodigestive disease. Recurrent soft-tissue infections should also raise concern for immunodeficiency.

Prognosis

The chapter describes prognosis as generally favorable when infection is recognized and treated. It summarizes a 200-patient emergency-department cohort in which more than half were discharged the same day, fewer than half were admitted, and about half of those admitted required surgery. These figures are secondary reporting and cannot be appraised as if generated by this review.

Complications

Uncontrolled infection can extend through deep neck spaces into the mediastinum or chest, producing mediastinitis or empyema and potentially requiring thoracic surgery. Chronic osteomyelitis is associated with delayed treatment, poor nutrition, poor oral hygiene, and tobacco use; CT may show sequestration, osteolysis, or necrosis, and treatment is described as prolonged culture-directed antibiotics plus debridement.

Emergencies Secondary to Odontogenic Infections

Airway compromise is the dominant emergency. Conservative adjuncts such as oxygen, monitoring, IV access, selected airway adjuncts, corticosteroids, or nebulized racemic epinephrine may temporize some obstruction, but they do not replace definitive airway control. Awake fiberoptic intubation is described as a preferred option when the glottis can be visualized; severe anatomy or distress may require cricothyrotomy or tracheostomy. Necrotizing fasciitis requires immediate surgical debridement and airway assessment.

Deterrence and Patient Education

Prevention focuses on routine oral hygiene, regular brushing and flossing, twice-yearly dental visits, and counseling around tobacco, alcohol, highly acidic or sweet foods, and bruxism. Professional plaque removal and prompt treatment of caries are framed as secondary-prevention measures.

Enhancing Healthcare Team Outcomes

The chapter favors bedside clinical assessment and POCUS for many presentations, with contrast CT used selectively rather than routinely because of radiation, contrast-related risk, and incidental findings. Collaboration among dental, emergency, primary-care, oral-surgery, and otolaryngology teams is positioned as central to early recognition and appropriate source control.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Recognize dental-source symptoms; prioritize dental extraction/drainage; use POCUS for equivocal abscess; reserve contrast CT for complex/deep spread; escalate rapidly for airway signs.
Medication boundary
The chapter names amoxicillin-clavulanate and clindamycin but does not supply doses, durations, renal/hepatic adjustment, or local-resistance guidance. Those details require a current drug/guideline source.
Escalation boundary
Ludwig angina, respiratory distress, deep-space infection, necrotizing infection, significant comorbidity, or immunodeficiency shifts management toward controlled airway assessment, IV therapy, admission, and surgical consultation.
Do not overclaim
The reported epidemiologic and outcome percentages are secondary summaries and should not be treated as independently validated estimates from this review.
