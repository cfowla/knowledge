One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon for a heparin deep dive; do not use alone for current practice.
The paper is historically useful because it directly examines whether adjusted subcutaneous heparin can match or outperform continuous IV heparin in initial DVT treatment. Its pooled efficacy estimate favored subcutaneous dosing, but the signal is fragile: the 95% CI barely excludes 1.0, efficacy heterogeneity was significant, individual trial results pointed in opposite directions, doses were not route-matched in most studies, and efficacy endpoints mixed clinical and subclinical progression.

Core result. In the prespecified primary analysis of six studies rated methodologically strong, the pooled RR for efficacy failure was 0.62 (95% CI 0.39-0.98) for subcutaneous versus continuous IV heparin. Major hemorrhage was not clearly different: RR 0.79 (95% CI 0.42-1.48). Adding the two moderately strong studies yielded efficacy RR 0.63 (0.42-0.94) and safety RR 0.79 (0.43-1.46).

Interpretive constraint. The efficacy homogeneity test was significant (P<0.001). The Hull trial strongly favored IV therapy and was discussed as an outlier plausibly related to early subtherapeutic APTT response in the subcutaneous arm. The paper’s most reusable lesson is therefore not simply “SC is better,” but that route comparisons are inseparable from dose selection, early anticoagulant response, and the outcome definition used.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction / rationale

Continuous IV heparin was the conventional inpatient approach, but the authors emphasized practical disadvantages including hospitalization, immobilization, infusion-cannula risks, and treatment costs. Several direct comparisons with subcutaneous heparin had produced contradictory findings, motivating a systematic overview and quantitative synthesis.

The stated goal was to compare safety and efficacy of the two administration routes in objectively confirmed venous thrombosis, incorporating both clinical and subclinical endpoints.

Methods - identification and eligibility

The search included MEDLINE (1966-1991), Current Contents, Science Citation Index, cited references, meeting proceedings/abstracts, and attempts to obtain full manuscripts for abstract-only reports. Eligible studies directly compared SC heparin with continuous IV infusion and required objective VTE confirmation at baseline.

Eight studies met the systematic-overview criteria. The review imposed common definitions for efficacy failure and major hemorrhage to permit pooling across heterogeneous source studies.

Methods - quality assessment and statistics

Six a priori method standards assessed randomization, patient-group description, treatment description, blinded objective outcome assessment, outcome ascertainment in all patients, and dropout reporting. Three investigators rated studies independently and resolved disagreement by consensus. Five or six standards = strong; four = moderately strong; fewer than four = weak.

The primary meta-analysis was prespecified to use strong studies. Relative risks and 95% CIs were calculated and pooled with Mantel-Haenszel methods; logistic regression provided common odds ratios and homogeneity testing.

Results - systematic overview

Seven of eight studies were randomized; six were rated strong and two moderately strong. Outcome measurement varied considerably: repeat venography, mixed vascular tests, lung scanning, and objectively confirmed symptomatic recurrence were all used. Heparin doses ranged roughly 24,000-37,000 IU/day.

Dose balance was imperfect: three studies used similar daily doses between routes, four used higher SC doses (by about 2,100-5,000 IU/day), and one used a higher IV dose. Within individual studies, coagulation testing and dose-adjustment criteria were the same between groups.

Results - meta-analysis

For the six strong-design studies, pooled efficacy failure favored SC heparin (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.39-0.98). Major hemorrhage did not differ clearly (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.42-1.48). Inclusion of all eight studies produced similar estimates. Logistic regression yielded efficacy ORs around 0.58 and safety ORs around 0.78.

Crucially, efficacy effects were heterogeneous (P<0.001), while safety heterogeneity was not detected (P>0.6). Thus, the pooled efficacy estimate summarizes discordant trial-level effects rather than a uniformly consistent benefit.

Discussion / interpretation

The authors interpreted the pooled efficacy effect as evidence that subcutaneous heparin was more effective and at least as safe as IV infusion. They highlighted the Hull trial as the major source of efficacy heterogeneity; early subtherapeutic APTT responses in the SC arm were proposed as the likely reason for its IV-favoring result.

They also acknowledged wide efficacy confidence intervals, inability to harmonize APTT subgroup analyses across other trials, and nonsignificant safety differences. They proposed that SC administration could simplify care and enable future home/self-administration research.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance9/10

Direct fit for a heparin deep dive and route/dose-response history.

Limit: narrow historical question. Would rise: if current UFH route selection were the main research target.

Novelty7/10

For its time, a focused quantitative synthesis of contradictory SC-vs-IV heparin trials.

Limit: contribution is now primarily historical. Would fall: if judged only by current therapeutic novelty.

Method strength6/10

Broad search, explicit eligibility, prespecified quality criteria, three-reviewer quality rating, and sensitivity analysis support credibility.

Limit: heterogeneous outcomes, common-effect pooling despite efficacy heterogeneity, no modern risk-of-bias framework, and informal publication-bias conclusion. Would rise: with modern systematic-review methods and heterogeneity modeling.

Evidence strength5/10

The pooled efficacy effect is statistically borderline and supported by discordant individual trials; safety is imprecise.

Limit: P<0.001 efficacy heterogeneity, dose imbalance, mixed surrogate/clinical endpoints. Would rise: with consistent large RCTs using matched dosing and patient-centered outcomes.

External validity4/10

Populations span calf-only and predominantly proximal DVT, but treatment, monitoring, and downstream anticoagulation reflect an early-1990s context.

Limit: dated care pathways and heterogeneous study methods. Would rise: with contemporary VTE populations and treatment comparators.

Implementation value4/10

Useful for understanding historical UFH route selection and the importance of rapid therapeutic anticoagulation.

Limit: it does not establish the preferred modern initiation agent. Would rise: only if paired with current guidance and current UFH-specific evidence.

Main strengths: clinically direct comparison; explicit efficacy and bleeding definitions; broad source search for its era; prespecified quality threshold; individual trial results and CIs are transparent; sensitivity analysis with all eight studies is directionally similar.
Main limitations: significant efficacy heterogeneity; mixed clinical/subclinical endpoints; route and dose are partially confounded because SC doses were often higher; six “strong” trials are still small by modern standards; safety CIs are wide; no formal modern risk-of-bias or certainty framework; publication-bias absence is asserted from search completeness rather than demonstrated.

Bottom line

Use this paper to understand the historical evidence that adjusted SC UFH can be effective and the reason early anticoagulant response matters. Do not treat RR 0.62 as a universal causal advantage of the SC route. The study-level inconsistency and dose/outcome heterogeneity make that stronger interpretation unjustified.

Current-practice context - external verification

This section is external to the 1992 paper. It is included only to prevent a historically accurate summary from being mistaken for current treatment guidance.

The 2024 CHEST compendium of VTE guidance still lists IV UFH and subcutaneous UFH among parenteral initiation options when VKA therapy is used, but suggests LMWH or fondaparinux over either IV UFH or subcutaneous UFH when a parenteral agent is chosen. This places the 1992 route-comparison result in a historical/supportive role rather than making it the current choice-of-agent standard.
