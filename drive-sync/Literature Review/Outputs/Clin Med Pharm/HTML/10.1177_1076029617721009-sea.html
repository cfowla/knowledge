One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Replacing nonstandardized, provider-driven UFH titration with institution-wide, nurse-driven, weight-based nomograms during an EHR transition was associated with faster achievement of minimum-threshold therapeutic anticoagulation and a larger proportion therapeutic by 24 hours, without a detected increase in the study's surrogate safety endpoint of aPTT ≥120 seconds.

The implementation was more than a dosing table. It combined two noncustomizable EHR nomograms, protocolized aPTT ordering, nurse-directed titration, optional weight-based starting boluses, smart-pump library updates, pharmacist/nurse surveillance, multidisciplinary education, conversion-day safety checks, and ongoing postimplementation review. The 60-80 second nomogram was used for acute PE/DVT and the 50-70 second nomogram for acute ACS; aPTT was checked 6 hours after dose changes and every 6 hours until two consecutive values were therapeutic, then every 12 hours.

In the main comparative analysis, mean time to threshold therapeutic anticoagulation fell from 18.7 to 11.7 hours (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.22-2.08; P<.0005), and therapeutic anticoagulation by 24 hours rose from 74.4% to 88.5% (OR 2.97; Table 2 P=.002). Table 2 reports critically supratherapeutic aPTT in 49.3% versus 46.8% (OR 0.92; P=.73). The postnomogram aPTT trajectory started high (mean first aPTT 94.16 seconds) and moved toward goal by subsequent draws; 69% were reportedly in range by the fourth aPTT. No major or fatal bleeding events were reported in the nomogram period, but bleeding was not the primary safety endpoint.

Main interpretive limit: This is a single-center retrospective pre/post study with nonconcurrent controls, unequal group sizes, baseline indication differences, incomplete nomogram adoption, and surrogate laboratory endpoints. The implementation signal is useful; causal claims about clinical benefit or safety are substantially weaker.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the intervention as an institution-wide standardization effort during an EHR transition and identifies time to minimum-threshold therapeutic anticoagulation as the primary endpoint. Secondary measures were therapeutic anticoagulation by 24 hours, aPTT ≥120 seconds, and the first four postnomogram aPTT results.

It reports improved time to therapeutic anticoagulation and 24-hour therapeutic attainment without an increase in critical aPTT values. One numerical inconsistency appears here: the prenomogram critical aPTT rate is printed as 49.9%, differing from Results prose and Table 2.

Background

The authors motivate UFH standardization around its narrow therapeutic range, short half-life, and need for laboratory-guided titration. They contrast aPTT with anti-Xa monitoring and review prior literature supporting weight-based and nurse-driven nomograms, pharmacist involvement, and technology-assisted monitoring.

The article's stated objective is operational: describe conversion from nonstandardized provider-driven UFH titration to an institution-wide, nurse-driven, weight-based approach and evaluate whether standardization, speed to therapeutic anticoagulation, and safety goals were achieved.

Program Description

BWH used two noncustomizable adult UFH nomograms: 60-80 seconds for acute PE/DVT and 50-70 seconds for ACS, with clinician discretion for selected other indications. Patients unsuitable for either could receive a custom weight-based infusion with provider-guided titration. An as-needed aPTT order was embedded in the order set; monitoring occurred 6 hours after adjustments, every 6 hours until two consecutive therapeutic values, then every 12 hours.

Optional initial boluses were 80 units/kg for the 60-80 second nomogram and 60 units/kg for the 50-70 second nomogram. Implementation used multidisciplinary content development, approximately 6 months of education, a mandatory nursing module, smart-pump library updates, unit-based education, nurse-pharmacist conversion safety checklists, and daily postimplementation surveillance/education.

Methods

The study retrospectively compared two 3-month internal quality-assurance periods from September-November 2013 and 2014 with a September 2015 postnomogram cohort after a 3-month wash-in. Inclusion required continuous UFH infusion. Exclusions included UFH <24 hours, outside-hospital initiation, postimplementation nonnomogram provider titration, and prenomogram UFH without a specified aPTT goal.

The primary endpoint was time to the lower bound of the chosen therapeutic range (50 or 60 seconds). Secondary endpoints were therapeutic anticoagulation within 24 hours and aPTT ≥120 seconds; the first four postnomogram aPTTs were described. The reported power was 93% for a 30% change in the primary endpoint at alpha .05. Analyses used Kaplan-Meier/log-rank methods for time-to-event data, chi-square tests for categorical variables, Student t tests for continuous parametric data, and descriptive statistics for the aPTT trajectory.

Results

Figure 1 shows 1,256 evaluated, 844 excluded, and 412 included (317 pre, 95 post); Results prose instead states 1,255 evaluated and 411 included. The post group was heavier (85.8 vs 79.7 kg; P=.019) and had more VTE and ACS but less atrial fibrillation as the UFH indication, indicating baseline case-mix differences.

Mean time to threshold therapeutic anticoagulation improved from 18.7 to 11.7 hours (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.22-2.08; P<.0005). Therapeutic anticoagulation at 24 hours increased from 74.4% to 88.5% (Table 2 OR 2.97; P=.002). Table 2 reports aPTT ≥120 seconds in 49.3% versus 46.8% (OR 0.92; P=.73). Mean postnomogram aPTTs were 34.18 seconds at baseline, then 94.16, 78.68, 72.53, and 72.49 seconds for the first four draws. The authors report 69% in goal by the fourth draw, 84.6% overall nomogram compliance, and no major or fatal bleeding events during the nomogram period.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the program as evidence that institution-wide nurse-driven UFH standardization can be implemented safely during an EHR transition. They emphasize multidisciplinary governance, education, monitoring, and continued intervention after go-live. They note that the first aPTT was frequently supratherapeutic: 58.9% supratherapeutic, 17.9% subtherapeutic, and 7.3% persistently supratherapeutic for at least 24 hours.

Limitations include retrospective design, documentation inaccuracy, inability to fully capture provider noncompliance, unequal groups, nonconcurrent controls, chronological and patient-difference bias, baseline indication imbalance, no stratification by critical illness, and incomplete adoption because some services retained provider-driven custom infusions. The final claim is therefore strongest as an implementation finding, not as definitive evidence of clinical superiority.

Declarations

Jean Connors reported consulting and advisory relationships with Bristol Meyer Squibb and Boehringer Ingelheim. The authors reported no financial support for the research, authorship, or publication. Supplementary material is stated to exist online but is absent from the retrieved PDF.

UFH monitoring / titration workflow

Input: Adult patient selected for 50-70 s or 60-80 s aPTT nomogram.
Start: Optional weight-based bolus (60 or 80 units/kg depending on nomogram) + continuous infusion.
Monitoring: Draw aPTT 6 h after initiation/adjustment.
Decision: Nurse titrates infusion per noncustomizable nomogram after each result.
Repeat: q6h until 2 consecutive aPTTs are in target range.
Stable monitoring: Extend aPTT to q12h.
Oversight: Unit pharmacists + nurse educators + EHR reports + computerized surveillance.
Implementation controls: education, smart-pump library, safety checklist, post-go-live daily review.
Failure/exception path: selected services/patients could use provider-driven custom weight-based infusions rather than the standard nomograms.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for heparin-protocol implementation and medication-use-system design; do not treat as stand-alone evidence for current clinical dosing targets or patient-centered superiority.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses UFH nomogram standardization, nurse titration, EHR integration, pharmacist surveillance, and anticoagulation process outcomes.

Evidence basis: institution-wide implementation with detailed workflow and outcomes. Limit: older single-center practice model. Higher/lower: higher with current multicenter validation; lower if only dosing efficacy is of interest.

Novelty
7/10

The distinctive contribution is institution-wide deployment simultaneous with enterprise EHR transition rather than the concept of weight-based heparin nomograms itself.

Evidence basis: authors position this as the first such institution-wide report. Limit: core nomogram strategy was established previously. Higher/lower: higher with comparative implementation science; lower if viewed only as another nomogram study.

Method strength
5/10

Clear endpoints and reasonable statistical methods are offset by retrospective pre/post design, nonconcurrent controls, unequal groups, baseline imbalance, and incomplete adoption.

Evidence basis: predefined endpoints and power analysis. Limit: confounding and chronological bias. Higher/lower: higher with prospective concurrent or randomized comparison.

Evidence strength
5/10

The process outcome signal is sizable and statistically clear, but endpoints are mostly laboratory surrogates and source reporting contains several internal numerical inconsistencies.

Evidence basis: HR/OR results and aPTT trajectory. Limit: no robust patient-centered safety/effectiveness endpoint. Higher/lower: higher with adjudicated bleeding/VTE outcomes and consistent reporting.

External validity
5/10

Institution-wide use across a large academic hospital is broader than a single unit, but local EHR, staffing, assay, nomogram design, and academic resources constrain transferability.

Evidence basis: 793-bed hospital-wide rollout. Limit: one center. Higher/lower: higher with multicenter replication across different operational settings.

Clinical-practice appraisal

PICO / clinical questionAmong adult inpatients receiving therapeutic continuous-infusion UFH at a tertiary academic center, did institution-wide nurse-driven, weight-based nomograms integrated with a new EHR improve time to aPTT-defined therapeutic anticoagulation compared with prior provider-driven practice without increasing critically supratherapeutic aPTT values?
ValidityRetrospective pre/post design; no concurrent control; baseline indication and weight imbalance; incomplete adoption; documentation/noncompliance uncertainty; no critical-illness stratification; internal reporting inconsistencies.
Patient-centered outcomesNot the primary evidence base. Major/fatal bleeding was reported as absent in the nomogram group, but the main safety endpoint was aPTT ≥120 seconds rather than adjudicated bleeding. Recurrent thrombosis was not a primary comparative outcome.
Practice translationStrongest for designing a controlled implementation process: standardized order sets, explicit monitoring intervals, nurse titration authority, pharmacist surveillance, smart-pump integration, education, and post-go-live auditing.
Bottom lineUseful implementation evidence. It supports the plausibility that a standardized institution-wide UFH workflow can improve anticoagulation process metrics, but it does not by itself validate current targets, bolus doses, assay choice, or net clinical benefit.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build as a system, not a nomogramOrder-set logic, lab timing, nursing authority, pump libraries, pharmacy surveillance, education, and exception handling were all part of the intervention.
Make monitoring cadence explicitThe paper used q6h checks after changes until two consecutive therapeutic aPTTs, then q12h. Whatever local policy is selected, the operational rule should be unambiguous and machine-representable.
Plan for nonadoption and exceptionsSome services retained custom provider-driven infusions. A safe CDS implementation needs a documented exception path and auditability rather than assuming universal adherence.
Audit transition errorsConversion-day safety checks and daily post-go-live review are notable controls for high-risk medication workflow changes.
Do not overread laboratory surrogatesFaster aPTT target attainment is a process outcome; it does not establish lower thrombosis, lower bleeding, or better survival in this design.
Preserve data-quality warningsThe article contains several internally inconsistent counts/percentages/P values; downstream extraction or CDS knowledge bases should retain those discrepancies rather than normalize them silently.
