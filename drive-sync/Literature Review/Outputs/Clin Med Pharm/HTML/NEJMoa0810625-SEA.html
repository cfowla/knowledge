One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a large international ICU RCT, targeting near-normoglycemia (81-108 mg/dL) increased 90-day mortality compared with a conventional strategy targeting ≤180 mg/dL, while markedly increasing severe hypoglycemia and providing no benefit in ICU/hospital length of stay, mechanical ventilation, or renal-replacement therapy.

The trial achieved clear glycemic separation: time-weighted glucose averaged 115 mg/dL in the intensive group versus 144 mg/dL in the conventional group, and intensive-control patients received substantially more insulin. At 90 days, mortality was 27.5% versus 24.9% (OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02-1.28; P=0.02); the adjusted OR was also 1.14 (95% CI 1.01-1.29; P=0.04). Severe hypoglycemia occurred in 6.8% versus 0.5% (OR 14.7, 95% CI 9.0-25.9; P<0.001). Cardiovascular proximate causes comprised a larger fraction of deaths in the intensive group, but the trial was not designed to establish mechanism.

Methodologically, NICE-SUGAR is strong: randomized allocation, concealed assignment before randomization, prespecified analysis, high follow-up completeness, clinically important patient-centered primary outcome, multicenter international enrollment, and independent data/safety oversight. Key limits are open-label treatment after randomization, subjective eligibility based on expected ICU duration, incomplete attainment of the intensive target in some patients, and limited mechanistic/cost data. The result is practice-defining evidence against routine ICU targeting of 81-108 mg/dL.

Section condensation

Background and objective

Hyperglycemia is common in critical illness and had been associated observationally with worse outcomes, but prior tight-control trials and meta-analyses were inconsistent. The investigators designed NICE-SUGAR to test whether intensive glucose control reduced 90-day mortality.

Methods — design and intervention

Adults in 42 ICUs were randomized to intensive glucose control (81-108 mg/dL) or conventional control (≤180 mg/dL). Randomization was stratified by operative versus nonoperative admission and geographic region and implemented through a secure web-based minimization algorithm; allocation was concealed before randomization but not afterward. Both strategies used intravenous insulin and web-accessible treatment algorithms. The intervention stopped with eating, ICU discharge, death, or 90 days, and resumed after ICU readmission within the 90-day window.

The study initially planned 4,000 patients and increased enrollment to 6,100 after newer data, providing 90% power to detect a 3.8-percentage-point mortality difference assuming 30% baseline mortality. Analyses were intention-to-treat without imputation. The primary outcome was unadjusted 90-day all-cause mortality; prespecified secondary/tertiary outcomes included survival, cause-specific death, ICU/hospital stay, mechanical ventilation, renal-replacement therapy, organ failure, cultures, and transfusion. Six predefined subgroup pairs were tested with interaction terms.

Results — population and intervention fidelity

6,104 patients were randomized; primary-outcome status at 90 days was available for 3,010 intensive-control and 3,012 conventional-control patients. Baseline characteristics were similar. Assigned study treatment was delivered to 99.5% of patients with study data. Intensive control produced lower glucose (time-weighted 115 vs 144 mg/dL) and much greater insulin exposure (97.2% vs 69.0% treated; mean 50.2 vs 16.9 units/day).

Nutritional delivery was predominantly enteral and total nonprotein calorie delivery was similar overall; IV glucose calories were slightly higher with intensive control. Corticosteroid exposure was modestly more frequent in the intensive group (34.6% vs 31.7%).

Results — mortality and safety

At 90 days, 829/3,010 intensive-control patients (27.5%) died versus 751/3,012 conventional-control patients (24.9%), OR 1.14 (95% CI 1.02-1.28; P=0.02), absolute difference 2.6 percentage points (95% CI 0.4-4.8). The adjusted OR remained 1.14 (95% CI 1.01-1.29; P=0.04), and time-to-death analysis yielded HR 1.11 (95% CI 1.01-1.23; P=0.03). Mortality at day 28 was not significantly different (22.3% vs 20.8%; P=0.17).

Severe hypoglycemia (≤40 mg/dL) was 6.8% with intensive control versus 0.5% with conventional control, OR 14.7 (95% CI 9.0-25.9; P<0.001). No long-term sequelae of severe hypoglycemia were reported. ICU and hospital stay, mechanical-ventilation duration, renal-replacement-therapy duration, new organ failures, positive blood cultures, and red-cell transfusion did not differ significantly.

Discussion and conclusion

The investigators emphasized the trial’s size, multicenter execution, near-complete follow-up, prespecified analysis, standardized algorithmic glucose management, and clear treatment separation. They acknowledged subjective expected-ICU-stay eligibility, inability to blind clinicians/staff, imperfect attainment of the intensive target, and absent mechanistic/cost data. They also noted that most nutrition was enteral, which may affect generalizability to prior studies with more parenteral nutrition.

The authors concluded that a target below 180 mg/dL produced lower mortality than 81-108 mg/dL and advised against the lower target in critically ill adults. They explicitly stated that the mechanism of harm was uncertain.

Supplementary appendix reconciliation

Appendix A — Eligibility criteria

Inclusion required anticipated ICU treatment extending beyond the day after admission plus an arterial line present or imminently planned. Major exclusions included age <18 years, imminent death without full supportive intent, DKA/hyperosmolar state, early expected oral intake, prior unrecovered hypoglycemia, unusually high hypoglycemia risk, prior study enrollment, specified inability to obtain consent, and ≥24 hours already spent in an ICU during the admission.

Appendix B — Nutrition

Day-by-day nonprotein calories were reported by total, enteral, parenteral, and IV-glucose routes. Averaged over days 1-14, total calories were 891 vs 872 kcal/day (P=0.1 in appendix table), enteral 624 vs 623 kcal/day, parenteral 173 vs 162 kcal/day, and IV glucose 93 vs 87 kcal/day (small statistically significant difference). These data support that major outcome differences were not explained by large differences in total nutrition delivery.

Appendix C — Cause-of-death and treatment-withdrawal methodology

The supplement specified one proximate cause plus all applicable underlying causes, with explicit instructions to classify the pathological process driving treatment withdrawal rather than the final physiologic mode of dying. This improves interpretability of the reported cause-of-death distribution but does not eliminate subjectivity in clinical attribution.

Appendix D — Limitation of potentially life-sustaining treatment

Treatment-limitation status was known for 1,557/1,580 deaths. Potentially life-sustaining treatment had been withheld or withdrawn before death in 91.4% of intensive-control and 90.3% of conventional-control deaths (OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.81-1.62; P=0.44), arguing against a large between-group imbalance in end-of-life limitation as an explanation for the mortality difference.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a landmark, practice-defining critical-care trial with a patient-centered primary endpoint, strong multicenter execution, clear exposure separation, and a clinically consequential harm signal.
Relevance — 10/10Directly addresses ICU insulin targets and mortality. Evidence basis: large ICU RCT. Limiter: target strategy is from 2009. Would fall only if the question were unrelated to critical-care glycemic management.
Novelty — 10/10At publication, it decisively challenged near-normoglycemia as routine ICU care. Limiter: later evidence/guidelines now incorporate the finding. Historical novelty remains exceptional.
Method strength — 9/10Randomized, concealed allocation before randomization, prespecified analysis, high follow-up, multicenter. Limiter: open-label after allocation and subjective expected-ICU-stay eligibility. Double-blinding was not practical.
Evidence strength — 9/10Mortality effect is directly measured with confidence intervals and corroborated by adjusted/time-to-event analyses; hypoglycemia signal is large. Limiter: mechanism of excess mortality remains unresolved.
External validity — 9/10Large international medical/surgical ICU population across academic and community sites. Limiter: most participants were Australia/New Zealand and nutrition was predominantly enteral; specific exclusions narrow applicability.
Implementation value — 10/10Provides a clear safety boundary against routine 81-108 mg/dL targeting. Limiter: does not itself define the optimal modern 140-180 mg/dL operational protocol.

Clinical effect interpretation

Model-calculated from reported event rates: absolute excess 90-day mortality was 2.6 percentage points, corresponding to an approximate number needed to harm of 38.5 (~39) for the intensive strategy over 90 days. Absolute excess severe hypoglycemia was 6.3 percentage points, approximate NNH 15.9 (~16). These NNH values are calculated from unadjusted reported rates and were not reported by the authors.

Main limitations: unblinded care after allocation; subjective duration-based eligibility; some intensive patients remained above target; mechanism of harm uncertain; no dedicated cost/resource analysis; some subgroup interaction P values were borderline but not definitive and should not be treated as evidence of benefit in those subgroups.
Main strengths: large sample; patient-centered mortality outcome; international multicenter enrollment; robust glycemic separation; nearly complete primary follow-up; prespecified statistical analysis; independent safety monitoring; detailed supplement addressing eligibility, nutrition, death classification, and withdrawal-of-therapy concerns.

Practice translation and external currency verification

Source-derived: NICE-SUGAR supports avoiding routine near-normoglycemic ICU targets of 81-108 mg/dL because the strategy increased 90-day mortality and severe hypoglycemia without improving major resource or organ-support outcomes.

External verification (current, not part of the trial): the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026 recommends initiating/intensifying insulin for persistent hyperglycemia at ≥180 mg/dL in most critically ill adults and, once treated, a glucose goal of 140-180 mg/dL for most ICU patients, with more stringent individualized goals only when achievable without significant hypoglycemia. The 2026 standard explicitly cites NICE-SUGAR among evidence showing increased mortality/hypoglycemia with more aggressive targets.
