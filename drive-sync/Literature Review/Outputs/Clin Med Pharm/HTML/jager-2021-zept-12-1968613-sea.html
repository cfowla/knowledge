Scope warning

One-page synthesis

Bottom line. The paper reports a short course of EMDR targeted at emotionally disturbing memories associated with misophonia. Seven participants completed post-treatment assessment. AMISOS-R improved by a reported mean 6.14 points in completers and 5.37 points in the eight-person LOCF analysis. Two of eight met the paper's full-response definition. The design is too weak to distinguish EMDR effects from expectancy, regression to the mean, time, therapist effects, or other nonspecific factors.

The intervention was structured. The therapist used standard eight-phase EMDR, a time-line strategy to identify symptom-onset and symptom-worsening memories, 60- to 90-minute sessions, and SUD ratings to track target-memory distress. Treatment ended when indexed target memories reached SUD 0. Among completers, mean treatment exposure was 2.6 sessions. The main self-reported endpoint was AMISOS-R, with CGI-I as a co-primary observer rating.

Safety reporting needs caution. The authors state that no adverse events occurred, but they also report treatment-emergent suicidal ideation in one participant, fatigue or headache in three, and misophonia symptoms during sessions in three. The article also contains two numerical reporting problems: the abstract uses n=8 for the completer result that Table 2 assigns to n=7, and the text claims a 33% SCL-90 decrease although the Table 2 means imply about 10.4%. These issues lower confidence in the precision of the report.

Section condensation

Background

The authors frame misophonia as a disabling condition marked by anger or disgust to specific trigger sounds and note incomplete response to CBT. They propose that some patients may have emotionally disturbing memories linked to symptom onset or worsening and use EMDR as a trauma-focused method to reprocess those memories.

Methods

Fourteen patients were considered. Ten reached baseline evaluation, six from the CBT waiting list and four prior CBT nonresponders. Inclusion required misophonia and age 18 to 65. Patients with several primary psychiatric disorders, recent substance-related disorder, recent CNS disorder or stroke, current benzodiazepine or stimulant use, suicide risk, or language barriers were excluded. Eight of the ten baseline participants had identifiable misophonia-related target memories and received EMDR.

EMDR followed the standard eight-phase protocol. Sessions lasted 60 to 90 minutes. The primary measures were AMISOS-R and CGI-I. Secondary measures were SCL-90-R and SDS. The analysis used paired t-tests. A LOCF intention-to-treat analysis included all eight treated participants. No formal sample-size calculation was performed.

Results

Seven participants completed treatment and post-treatment assessment. Table 2 reports AMISOS-R means of 30.43 pre-treatment and 24.29 post-treatment in seven completers, with reported mean change -6.14, SD 5.34, P=.023. Table 3 reports the LOCF analysis in eight treated participants, 30.50 to 25.13, mean change -5.37, SD 5.40, P=.026. Two of eight met full-response criteria. Two additional participants were partial responders. Secondary outcomes did not meet the .05 significance threshold.

Fourteen target memories were identified across eight treated participants. Starting SUD values ranged from 5 to 10 and fell to 0 for treated targets. The paper reports no adverse events, but also describes transient fatigue or headaches in three participants, treatment-session misophonia symptoms in three, and suicidal ideation with intrusive suicide thoughts in one participant after the first session.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the symptom decrease as preliminary support for EMDR, especially for patients with clear emotionally disturbing target memories or prior CBT nonresponse. They compare the brief treatment exposure favorably with a longer group-CBT program, but the comparison is indirect and based on another study.

The authors acknowledge the lack of a control group, small sample, no follow-up, mixed subpopulations, one therapist, unblinded evaluator, and absence of fidelity measures. They call for a larger randomized trial comparing EMDR with CBT and a waiting list, or testing EMDR as an add-on, with longer follow-up.

Source-integrity findings

Completer denominator. The abstract says n=8 for the -6.14-point AMISOS-R result. The Results section and Table 2 assign that result to n=7 completers.
SCL-90 percentage. The Results text says SCL-90 means decreased by 33%. Table 2 gives 172.83 to 154.83, a calculated decrease of about 10.4%.
Safety classification. The authors write "No adverse events were reported" and then describe suicidal ideation during treatment plus fatigue/headache and symptom provocation. The report does not define why these events were classified outside adverse events.
Participant terminology. The Results section calls 10 patients included, then says two without target memories were excluded. Figure 1 provides the clearest sequence: 10 baseline evaluations, 8 treated, 7 post-treatment assessments.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. The study is useful as an early feasibility signal and for trial-design context. Do not use it as evidence that EMDR is an established treatment for misophonia.
4/10
Relevance

Directly relevant to misophonia psychotherapy, but peripheral to most medication-focused clinical practice.

Evidence basis: The source studies EMDR in a narrowly defined misophonia sample.
Limiting factor: The clinical question and population are narrow.
Would change score: Would rise if the review question were specifically about misophonia psychotherapy or trauma-focused treatment.

7/10
Novelty

The authors present the first clinical-sample study of EMDR for misophonia.

Evidence basis: The paper applies a structured trauma-focused intervention to a poorly studied condition and reports patient-level treatment data.
Limiting factor: Novelty is intervention-specific and does not compensate for weak causal evidence.
Would change score: Would fall if earlier controlled EMDR evidence in misophonia were identified or rise with a genuinely new mechanistic or comparative contribution.

3/10
Method strength

Prospective pre/post measurement is useful, but there is no control group, only eight treated patients, one therapist, no blinded evaluator, and no fidelity check.

Evidence basis: The design is an uncontrolled pilot case series with paired analyses and a LOCF sensitivity analysis.
Limiting factor: No randomization or concurrent comparator, plus very small N.
Would change score: A randomized controlled design with blinded assessment, prespecified fidelity, and adequate sample size would raise this substantially.

3/10
Evidence strength

AMISOS-R improved in completers and ITT analyses, but causal attribution is weak and the source contains denominator, percentage, and safety-classification inconsistencies.

Evidence basis: Two pre/post AMISOS-R analyses met the paper's significance threshold; secondary outcomes did not.
Limiting factor: Small uncontrolled sample and internal reporting inconsistencies.
Would change score: Replication with adequate power, a control group, follow-up, and cleaner reporting would raise confidence.

3/10
External validity

The sample came from one specialty clinic, was 90% female, and only patients with identifiable target memories received EMDR.

Evidence basis: Ten patients had baseline evaluation, eight were treated, and seven completed post-treatment assessment.
Limiting factor: Single-center selection and treatment eligibility based on target-memory identification.
Would change score: Multisite recruitment, broader eligibility, and replication in more diverse patients would raise confidence.

4/10
Implementation value

The protocol and session duration are described well enough to understand the intervention, but the evidence does not justify routine clinical use.

Evidence basis: The paper describes standard eight-phase EMDR, time-line target selection, 60- to 90-minute sessions, and stopping when target SUD reached 0.
Limiting factor: The study does not establish comparative benefit, durability, or a reliable harm profile.
Would change score: Controlled comparative evidence, follow-up, fidelity data, and clearer safety characterization are needed before practice adoption.

Clinical validity

PICO. Adults with misophonia from one specialty clinic, including CBT waiting-list patients and prior CBT nonresponders; short-course EMDR targeted at misophonia-related disturbing memories; no concurrent control; outcomes were AMISOS-R, CGI-I, SCL-90-R, SDS, and exploratory SUD/IES measures.

Bias and uncertainty. The absence of a control group is the main problem. Selection into treatment depended on identifying target memories. The outcome evaluator was not blinded, the therapist was the same clinician for all participants, fidelity was not measured, and the sample was too small for reliable subgroup or predictor analysis. LOCF does not solve these design limitations. Internal reporting inconsistencies add uncertainty.

Practice and research takeaways

Use this source for
Feasibility context, intervention description, patient-level trajectories, and hypotheses for future EMDR trials in misophonia.
Do not use this source for
Claims of comparative efficacy, durable benefit, incidence of harms, or routine treatment recommendations.
Trial design lesson
A confirmatory study needs randomization, an active/control arm, blinded outcome assessment where feasible, prespecified safety definitions, fidelity monitoring, and follow-up.
Reporting lesson
Reconcile denominators and percentage calculations across abstract, prose, tables, and safety reporting before downstream evidence synthesis.
