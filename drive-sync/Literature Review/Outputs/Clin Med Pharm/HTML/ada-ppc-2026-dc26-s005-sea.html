One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-implementation-value Standards chapter that translates behavioral, nutrition, activity, psychosocial, cognitive, and sleep evidence into specific diabetes-care actions.

The chapter’s central argument is that diabetes outcomes depend on more than medication selection: effective care requires recurring DSMES, individualized nutrition therapy, safe movement and fasting plans, tobacco/cannabis risk reduction, routine psychosocial screening with referral capacity, attention to cognition, and sleep-health assessment. The recommendations repeatedly emphasize person-centered design, cultural/social context, shared decision-making, and access barriers.

For practice, the most reusable elements are the timing rules for DSMES and psychosocial screening, nutrition and activity targets, religious-fasting risk assessment and medication planning, tobacco/vape cessation treatment, cannabis/DKA counseling, behavioral-health referral triggers, antipsychotic metabolic monitoring, cognitive-capacity surveillance, and sleep screening. The main evidence-use limitation is source type: trial and meta-analysis findings quoted in the chapter are secondary reports until the cited publication is read directly.

Section-by-section condensation

DSMES (S89–S92)

DSMES is treated as a continuing process that begins at diagnosis and is revisited at least annually, when goals are not met, when complicating medical/functional/psychosocial factors arise, and during care or life transitions. The chapter emphasizes person-centered, culturally and socially responsive education, shared goals, strengths-based communication, motivational interviewing, problem-solving, and coordination with the diabetes care team.

Evidence summarized by the chapter links DSMES with improved knowledge, self-care, A1C, weight, quality of life, coping, service use, mortality risk, and cost. Greater contact time (>10 hours over 6–12 months), personalization, psychosocial attention, and ongoing support are associated with better outcomes. Telehealth/digital delivery can improve reach and satisfaction, but access barriers and reimbursement remain implementation constraints.

Medical nutrition therapy (S93–S99)

Nutrition care is individualized rather than organized around a single “diabetes diet.” Referral to a diabetes-experienced RDN is recommended; food quality, total energy, metabolic goals, culture, preferences, resources, and sustainability guide the eating plan. The chapter favors minimally processed foods, vegetables, whole fruits, legumes, whole grains, lean proteins, nuts/seeds and lower-fat dairy/alternatives while minimizing refined grains, added sugars, sugar-sweetened beverages, red meat, and processed/ultraprocessed foods.

Load-bearing targets include at least 5–7% weight loss for overweight/obesity, sodium <2,300 mg/day when appropriate, and at least 14 g fiber/1,000 kcal. Carbohydrate reduction can be considered for some adults, but ketogenic patterns are discouraged in people using SGLT inhibition who are at DKA risk. Alcohol, supplements, nonnutritive sweeteners, macronutrient effects on insulin dosing, Mediterranean-pattern elements, saturated fat, and long-term weight-maintenance support are addressed with graded recommendations.

Fasting and timing of intake (S99–S103)

The chapter separates nonreligious fasting/time-restricted eating from religious fasting. For religious fasting, clinical safety depends on prefasting assessment, diabetes-specific risk stratification, education, and advance adjustment of treatment dose and timing. Figure 5.1 explains why religious fasting cannot simply be treated as an intermittent-fasting protocol.

Table 5.3 provides a multidomain fasting-risk score and Table 5.4 translates risk into medication considerations. The practical focus is prevention of hypoglycemia, dehydration, hyperglycemia, and ketoacidosis while respecting the individual’s informed choice to fast. Evidence and implementation are particularly context-sensitive because fasting duration, climate, fluid restriction, treatment regimen, pregnancy, frailty, monitoring, and prior fasting experience alter risk.

Physical activity (S102–S107)

Physical activity recommendations span baseline assessment, sitting interruption, age-specific aerobic targets, resistance training, flexibility/balance, and lean-mass preservation during obesity treatment. Most adults are counseled toward ≥150 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity over at least 3 days with no >2 consecutive inactive days, plus 2–3 weekly resistance sessions; children/adolescents are counseled toward ≥60 minutes/day.

Exercise planning is individualized around insulin/secretagogue exposure, baseline glucose, food intake, exercise type/intensity/duration, fitness, complications, and hypoglycemia risk. The chapter also addresses retinopathy, neuropathy, autonomic neuropathy, CKD, and other conditions that can change exercise safety. Figure 5.2 frames activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep as an integrated 24-hour behavior system.

Tobacco, vaping, and cannabis (S107–S108)

The chapter recommends routine tobacco/vape screening, complete avoidance, and combination cessation counseling plus pharmacotherapy when appropriate. Tobacco exposure is framed as a major modifiable cardiovascular and microvascular risk factor in diabetes.

Cannabis receives a diabetes-specific safety recommendation: recreational cannabis is discouraged in type 1 diabetes and other forms of diabetes with DKA risk. This is a notable implementation point because cannabis screening and counseling may not be embedded in standard diabetes workflows.

Psychosocial care and positive health behaviors (S108–S114)

Psychosocial care is defined as routine diabetes care. The chapter recommends standardized, validated screening at least annually and during major health/treatment/life changes, with referral pathways for clinically important findings. Diabetes distress, anxiety, fear of hypoglycemia, depression, and disordered eating are treated as distinct but overlapping problems that can impair quality of life, engagement, glycemia, and safety.

Table 5.5 operationalizes referral triggers, while Tables 5.6 and 5.7 organize psychosocial concerns for type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The strongest implementation message is that screening without capacity for assessment, evidence-based intervention, and diabetes-aware collaboration is incomplete.

Serious mental illness, cognition, and sleep (S115–S116)

People with serious mental illness require enhanced monitoring and self-management support, including metabolic surveillance when second-generation antipsychotics are used. Cognitive capacity should be monitored across the lifespan because executive function and other cognitive changes can directly affect dosing, monitoring, symptom recognition, and decision-making.

Sleep health is elevated to a routine screening domain. The chapter reports high rates of poor sleep, obstructive sleep apnea, insomnia, and restless legs syndrome in diabetes, links sleep disruption to self-management and metabolic outcomes, and describes evidence for CPAP, CBT/CBT-I, selected insomnia therapies, sleep extension, and sleep-hygiene interventions. Referral to sleep or behavioral-health specialists is recommended when indicated.

The chapter’s recommendation wording has been normalized for concise reference while preserving population, action, conditions, and evidence grade. Split-grade recommendations are shown as separate independently reviewable clauses.

TABLE 5.3 — Religious-fasting risk calculation

Page: S101–S102

TABLE 5.4 — Medication changes during fasting

Page: S103
Key content: Medication-specific timing/dose considerations are organized around fasting-related hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, dehydration, and ketoacidosis risk. Metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, acarbose, and pioglitazone generally require no total-dose change; older sulfonylurea such as glyburide should be replaced with a newer agent or reduced by 50%; basal insulin may require a 25–35% reduction when not well managed; and prandial or mixed insulin for a meal followed by fasting is reduced 35–50% in the table.
Interpretation / limit: Do not infer a one-size-fits-all dose change; apply the table in the context of prefasting risk assessment.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance
10/10

Directly practice-relevant behavioral, nutrition, activity, psychosocial, and sleep recommendations for diabetes care.

Evidence basis: Full 2026 ADA Standards chapter with 57 numbered recommendations.
Limiting factor: Relevance is high, but other Standards sections are needed for complete pharmacologic and complication management.
What would change it: Would fall if the user’s scope excluded behavioral/psychosocial care.

Novelty
7/10

The chapter is an annual synthesis rather than a single novel experiment, but it integrates newer fasting, cannabis, cognition, digital DSMES, and sleep evidence.

Evidence basis: Updated 2026 recommendation set and contemporary evidence citations.
Limiting factor: Many core principles are longstanding.
What would change it: Would rise with clearly identified new-for-2026 recommendation changes and evidence mapping to prior editions.

Method strength
8/10

Formal ADA Standards process and graded recommendations provide a strong guideline framework.

Evidence basis: Recommendation grades A/B/C/E and extensive cited evidence base.
Limiting factor: The full guideline-development methodology is external to this supplied section.
What would change it: Would rise with the companion methodology document and explicit evidence-to-recommendation trace for each recommendation.

Evidence strength
8/10

Many core recommendations are supported by systematic reviews, RCTs, and mature consensus evidence, while several implementation recommendations rely on lower-grade or expert evidence.

Evidence basis: Mixed grades across 57 recommendations; quantitative evidence is summarized throughout.
Limiting factor: Evidence strength varies materially by topic and recommendation.
What would change it: Would rise only with primary-source verification of the highest-impact claims and recommendation-specific certainty assessment.

External validity
9/10

Recommendations explicitly emphasize development, culture, social context, access, individual preferences, and diverse care settings.

Evidence basis: Person-centered DSMES/MNT, SDOH, fasting, age-specific activity, behavioral health, and sleep sections.
Limiting factor: Some supporting studies and risk tools may be setting- or population-specific.
What would change it: Would fall where local resources, culture, technology access, or referral capacity differ substantially.

Implementation value
10/10

The chapter provides actionable timing, targets, referral triggers, risk assessment, and behavioral recommendations.

Evidence basis: Tables 5.1–5.7, figures 5.1–5.2, activity targets, sodium/fiber/weight targets, screening intervals and referral guidance.
Limiting factor: Implementation depends on DSMES, RDN, behavioral-health, sleep-medicine, and other resource availability.
What would change it: Would fall in settings without referral infrastructure or means to address access barriers.

Main strengths: comprehensive behavioral-health scope; explicit graded recommendations; concrete implementation targets; attention to culture, social determinants, access, developmental stage, and referral pathways; strong integration of nutrition, movement, mental health, cognition, and sleep.
Main limitations: the source is a secondary guideline synthesis, not a primary study; evidence strength is heterogeneous across recommendations; full ADA methodology is outside the supplied chapter; some interventions depend on specialized resources that may not be locally available; several quantitative claims require direct primary-source verification before reuse as primary evidence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build recurring triggersDSMES, psychosocial, distress, depression, anxiety, cognition, and sleep screening should be linked to diagnosis, annual review, clinical changes, and transitions rather than left to ad hoc recall.
Make referrals executableMap RDN, DSMES, behavioral-health, sleep-medicine, tobacco-cessation, and social-resource pathways so screening produces action.
Preserve quantitative targetsExamples include ≥5–7% weight loss, sodium <2,300 mg/day when appropriate, ≥14 g fiber/1,000 kcal, ≥150 min/week adult aerobic activity, 2–3 resistance sessions/week, sitting interruption at least every 30 min, and ≥60 min/day activity for youth.
Flag high-risk contextsSGLT-inhibitor DKA risk with ketogenic intake, religious fasting, insulin/secretagogue use around exercise or alcohol, cannabis in DKA-prone diabetes, serious mental illness/antipsychotics, cognitive impairment, and sleep disorders warrant explicit safeguards.

Do not use for: replacing individualized medical assessment, reproducing a cited trial as if this chapter were the primary source, or assuming every Grade E/C recommendation carries the same evidence strength as Grade A recommendations.
