One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Clinician-versus-AI performance depends strongly on context and clinician expertise: AI generally won when both parties saw only structured first-24-hour data, but experienced ICU physicians performed better in real-world bedside prediction. Aggregating clinicians and combining clinician with AI predictions often improved performance beyond either source alone.

The study deliberately contrasts two settings. Retrospectively, seven ICU clinicians reviewed de-identified first-24-hour data for 990 ABeICU admissions, while AI models trained on 46,631 ICU admissions used the same data domain. AI generally outperformed one- and two-clinician predictions, but aggregation across seven clinicians usually surpassed AI for classification outcomes other than delirium. For length-of-stay prediction, both humans and AI had weak R² values; no simple seven-clinician advantage emerged.

Prospectively, clinicians made bedside predictions 16-24 hours after admission while caring for patients, and the same AI models predicted from extracted first-24-hour data. After linkage, 238 ICU admissions from 215 patients remained. Subspecialized physicians generally outperformed AI for classification outcomes except delirium; trainees and nurses performed less well and AI often surpassed them for AKI and delirium. Hybrid predictions tended to produce the best operating characteristics, with AND favoring specificity/PPV and OR favoring sensitivity/NPV.

The paper is most useful as evidence that human expertise, contextual information, aggregation, and AI provide partially complementary prognostic signals. It is not evidence that AI deployment improves ICU outcomes. External validity is limited by one Canadian province, only seven retrospective clinicians, COVID-era prospective data, substantial prospective linkage loss, outcome-specific exclusions, and a clinician/AI information-window mismatch.

Section-by-section synthesis

Abstract

The authors compare clinician and AI outcome predictions in both retrospective and prospective ICU settings. AI was trained on 46,631 admissions; 990 admissions were used for retrospective comparison and 238 admissions for prospective comparison. Outcomes included in-hospital and 30-day mortality, AKI, delirium, and ICU/hospital length of stay.

The abstract reports the central interaction: AI generally outperformed clinicians retrospectively, aggregated predictions from seven clinicians outperformed AI, subspecialized physicians generally outperformed AI prospectively, and clinician-AI combinations frequently produced the strongest performance.

Introduction

The rationale is that ICU clinicians face high-dimensional, rapidly changing data while conventional severity scores have limited individual-level discrimination. AI can process large data volumes, but most prior clinician-versus-AI evaluations have been retrospective.

The study therefore targets a more clinically relevant question than benchmark superiority alone: whether the relative performance of human and machine prediction changes when clinicians are actually caring for the patient and can use contextual/tacit information unavailable to the model.

Results — cohorts and AI models

The prospective cohort was described as sicker than the training and retrospective cohorts, with higher in-hospital mortality and delirium and longer hospital stay. Patients included versus excluded from AKI and delirium modeling differed materially, foreshadowing selection-bias concerns.

XGBoost was selected as best for every outcome under median cross-validated AUROC/RMSE. Classification Brier scores ranged from 0.1145 to 0.1926. Optimized thresholds were 0.45, 0.48, 0.52, and 0.50 for in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality, AKI, and delirium. Decision-curve results were broadly favorable except prospective AKI, with some optimized-threshold exceptions.

Results — retrospective comparison

AI generally beat one- and two-clinician classification predictions, but exceptions demonstrate that superiority was not uniform: two-clinician specificity for in-hospital mortality was 0.925 versus AI 0.896, and one-clinician AKI sensitivity was 0.652 versus AI 0.551 (both p<0.001). Aggregation of seven clinician predictions usually reversed the comparison in favor of clinicians, except for delirium.

AI generally performed better on LOS prediction, with two-clinician MAE exceptions for ICU LOS (4.436 vs 4.534 days) and hospital LOS (16.73 vs 17.14 days). Both clinicians and AI had poor R² values for LOS, and the seven-clinician aggregation did not create the same advantage seen for classification.

Results — prospective comparison

Subspecialized physicians generally outperformed AI on classification tasks other than delirium. AI had higher in-hospital mortality sensitivity in one cited comparison (0.700 vs 0.625), while delirium performance did not differ significantly between subspecialized physicians and AI. Trainees and nurses were weaker than senior physicians; they still often beat AI for 30-day mortality but were generally outperformed by AI for AKI and delirium.

LOS comparisons depended on metric. Clinician groups generally had lower MAE, while AI generally had better RMSE and R². The cited hospital-LOS MAE for subspecialists favored AI (37.798 vs 42.646 days), while ICU-LOS RMSE and R² among trainees favored the trainees. This metric instability reinforces the authors’ statement that LOS prediction was poor for both humans and AI.

Results — hybrid predictions, agreement, sensitivity analysis

Hybrid classification predictions usually delivered the strongest operating characteristics. AND combinations favored specificity/PPV; OR combinations favored sensitivity/NPV. Seven-clinician retrospective aggregation was a notable exception, performing as well as or better than hybrids. Regression hybrids also tended to be best, but no single combination rule dominated.

Inter-rater agreement was generally poor among clinicians and mostly poor to fair between clinicians and AI, suggesting that the different predictors often disagreed at the individual-patient level even when aggregate performance was similar. Broadening the positive Likert threshold improved sensitivity/NPV at the cost of specificity/PPV; the authors preferred the primary threshold.

Discussion

The authors interpret the prospective advantage of experienced clinicians as evidence that bedside context and tacit information matter. Conversely, AI may have an advantage when the task is restricted to complex high-dimensional data, as in the retrospective setting. Their practical interpretation is complementarity rather than replacement.

They recommend senior intensivist prediction when available, aggregation when relying on junior clinicians/nurses, and clinician-AI combinations when both are available. This is a performance-based inference, not a demonstrated clinical-outcome benefit.

Methods — design, outcomes, and model development

The ABeICU source database contains 55,689 ICU admissions from 48,672 adults across 15 Alberta ICUs from 2012-2019. Eligibility required adulthood and ICU stay over 24 hours. Models used first-24-hour data to predict outcomes at 24 hours. AKI followed KDIGO creatinine criteria and delirium used ICDSC ≥4.

Five candidate models were evaluated per task; hyperparameters used Optuna Bayesian optimization. Classification selection optimized median AUROC and regression selection RMSE in 5-fold cross-validation; thresholds optimized F1. Calibration, SHAP feature importance, and decision-curve analyses were also performed.

Methods — clinician prediction and statistics

Retrospectively, three ICU physicians and four nurses predicted de-identified cases in REDCap. Fifty cases were predicted by all seven clinicians, while the remaining cases were allocated so patients received one or two clinician predictions. Prospectively, bedside predictions were collected in a 28-bed tertiary ICU from September 2020 through December 2022; 465 predictions for 287 patients were collected before linkage losses.

Performance comparisons used 1,000 bootstrap samples and two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Classification Likert responses were dichotomized; agreement used Cohen κ or ICC. Hybrid classification used AND/OR, and hybrid regression used average/minimum/maximum combinations.

Availability, funding, and competing interests

ABeICU data require permission from Alberta Health Services and Alberta Health; clinician predictions may be shared on reasonable request without patient data. The authors report public source code for preprocessing, training, and analyses. Funding came from University of Calgary entities and NSERC, with no reported funder role.

Joon Lee disclosed being a co-founder and major shareholder of Symbiotic AI, Inc.; the other authors reported no competing interest.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Strongly relevant evidence for evaluating clinical AI against human experts and for designing human-AI prognostic workflows. It should not be interpreted as evidence that deploying these AI predictions improves ICU care or outcomes.
Relevance
9/10

Direct comparison of ICU clinicians, AI, clinician aggregation, and human-AI hybrid prediction under both retrospective and real-world prospective conditions.

Evidence basis: six ICU prediction targets, multiple clinician experience levels, and two evaluation settings.

Limiting factor: prognostic accuracy rather than therapeutic decision-making.

Would raise/lower: workflow-impact or patient-outcome evaluation would raise utility further.

Novelty
8/10

The paired retrospective/prospective design directly tests whether human-versus-AI ranking changes when clinicians have bedside context.

Evidence basis: same AI models evaluated against clinician predictions in both settings plus hybrid combinations.

Limiting factor: individual prediction models and metrics are conventional.

Would raise/lower: multicenter prospective replication would strengthen the novelty claim.

Method strength
7/10

Clear cohort definitions, standardized modeling pipeline, cross-validation, calibration, decision curves, bootstrap comparison, agreement analysis, and sensitivity analysis support a serious comparative evaluation.

Evidence basis: explicit first-24-hour feature window, prespecified performance metrics, and prospective bedside collection.

Limiting factor: observational clinician sampling, extensive supplement dependence, information asymmetry, and inferential testing on bootstrap metric distributions.

Would raise/lower: prespecified multicenter protocol, matched information windows, hierarchical dependence-aware inference, and full supplement access would strengthen confidence.

Evidence strength
6/10

The study demonstrates comparative prediction performance, including prospective real-world data, but not clinical benefit.

Evidence basis: consistent setting-dependent patterns and hybrid complementarity across multiple outcomes.

Limiting factor: no intervention based on predictions, no downstream decision or patient-centered outcome evaluation, and selected numeric reporting in the main text.

Would raise/lower: randomized or prospective impact evaluation linking predictions to actions and outcomes would raise this score.

External validity
4/10

Training data span 15 Alberta ICUs, but clinician comparisons and prospective collection were centered at one FMC ICU in a single Canadian province.

Evidence basis: authors explicitly call for external validation.

Limiting factor: seven retrospective clinicians, COVID-era prospective cohort, and local staffing/workflow context.

Would raise/lower: independent health-system and international prospective replication would raise this score.

Implementation value
7/10

The paper provides actionable design lessons: clinician expertise matters, aggregation can help, hybrid thresholds encode sensitivity/specificity tradeoffs, and drift monitoring is important.

Evidence basis: experience-stratified comparisons, hybrid AND/OR results, and explicit discussion of drift.

Limiting factor: no validated production workflow, alert policy, calibration-monitoring threshold, or clinical utility endpoint.

Would raise/lower: prospective workflow implementation with monitoring and outcome impact would raise the score.

Main strengths

  • Directly contrasts retrospective data-only and prospective bedside clinician evaluation.
  • Uses the same AI models across both comparison settings, isolating some of the contextual change.
  • Stratifies prospective clinicians by experience/type and evaluates clinician aggregation.
  • Includes hybrid human-AI prediction rules rather than framing the task only as replacement.
  • Reports calibration, decision-curve analysis, agreement, and threshold sensitivity in addition to discrimination/error metrics.

Main limitations

  • Approximately 35% of prospective clinician predictions failed linkage due to manual identifier errors.
  • Prospective collection occurred during COVID-19 while AI training data were pre-COVID, creating likely temporal/domain shift.
  • Only seven clinicians contributed retrospective predictions.
  • Prospective clinician-type categories may inadequately represent expertise differences.
  • Clinicians predicted at 16-24 hours; AI could use the full first 24 hours.
  • AKI/delirium exclusions reduced sample sizes by about 40% and produced demonstrable cohort differences.
  • No external validation outside the province and no evidence that acting on predictions improves patient outcomes.
  • The main manuscript delegates many complete metric tables and ancillary analyses to unavailable supplementary material.
Appraisal inference. The manuscript uses rank-sum tests across bootstrap-derived metric samples and states independence is justified because clinicians and AI predicted independently. Because those metric samples arise from the same underlying evaluation cohorts, the appropriateness of treating the resulting metric distributions as independent is not fully established in the main text. This affects confidence in very small p-values more than the broad direction of the reported performance patterns.

Confidence in appraisal: Moderate-high for main-text design and directional findings; moderate for complete performance characterization because the supplementary tables/figures were not supplied.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use nowUse the study to design clinical-AI validation: compare against experienced clinicians, separate retrospective data-only testing from real-world prospective testing, assess aggregation, and report calibration/agreement as well as discrimination.
Do not use nowDo not infer that the AI should replace intensivists, that a model with better retrospective metrics has superior bedside utility, or that hybrid prediction improves patient outcomes without an impact study.
Build/evaluateFor a CDS pipeline, preserve clinician expertise as a comparator/feature, predefine sensitivity-versus-specificity objectives, test hybrid rules, monitor temporal drift, and log information available to human and model at prediction time.
Watch nextIndependent multicenter validation; prospective decision-impact trials; calibration drift over time; human factors and trust; workflow-specific utility; performance by clinician expertise and patient subgroup.
Bottom line. The most reusable result is not that “AI beats clinicians” or the reverse. Performance reverses with setting and expertise, and disagreement between humans and AI creates room for aggregation/hybrid strategies. The next evidence step is to test whether those strategies change decisions or outcomes safely.
