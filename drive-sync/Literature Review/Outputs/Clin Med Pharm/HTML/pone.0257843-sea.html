One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The review found statistically significant improvements in several sleep outcomes with Ashwagandha extract versus placebo. Five RCTs with 400 unique participants were included; all were conducted in India. Four trials used KSM-66 root extract and one used Shoden root-and-leaf extract. Doses ranged from 120 to 600 mg/day and treatment lasted 6–12 weeks.

Primary results. The reported overall-sleep effect was SMD -0.59 (95% CI -0.75 to -0.42; I²=62%; p<0.001), rated moderate certainty by the authors. Sleep Quality Scale, sleep-onset latency, total sleep time, wake after sleep onset, and sleep efficiency favored Ashwagandha. PSQI and total time in bed were not significant. Mental alertness and anxiety improved; quality-of-life domains did not.

Bottom line: The signal is potentially useful for hypothesis generation, but the meta-analysis should not be used alone to support routine clinical use. The main analytic concern is that the headline “overall sleep” model appears to pool multiple correlated outcomes from the same participants as independent entries. The paper reports 400 unique participants yet 1,764 pooled entries, and the forest plots repeatedly include the same trial participants for different sleep measures. That dependence can overweight multi-outcome trials and make confidence intervals look more precise than they are.

Section condensation

Introduction

The article frames poor sleep and insomnia as common health problems with functional and economic consequences and notes increasing use of herbal approaches because of limitations of standard insomnia pharmacotherapy. Ashwagandha is described as a traditional medicinal plant with proposed GABAergic or other sleep-promoting mechanisms, but human sleep evidence was considered limited.

The stated review objective was to determine the effect and safety of Ashwagandha extract on sleep in adults.

Methods

The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021229064) and reported according to PRISMA. CENTRAL, MEDLINE, SCOPUS, Google Scholar, WHO trial registries, ClinicalTrials.gov, CTRI, Google, and AYUSH resources were searched through February 2021; references were hand-searched and manufacturers contacted. Eligible studies were human RCTs in adults over 18 years, with or without insomnia, using Ashwagandha extract and measuring sleep. English-language restriction was applied.

Two reviewers screened and extracted data. Cochrane risk-of-bias domains were assessed. Random-effects meta-analysis used MD or SMD with 95% CIs and I² for heterogeneity. GRADE was applied. The protocol prespecified subgroup analyses; the final review added subgrouping by sleep-measure type, changed the planned older-age threshold from 65 to 60, and could not perform the age subgroup because of insufficient trials.

Results

Five placebo-controlled RCTs with 400 unique participants were included, all from India. Study populations included healthy/stressed adults, adults with DSM-IV insomnia, and one elderly cohort. KSM-66 was used in four trials and Shoden in one; doses were 120–600 mg/day and durations 6–12 weeks. No serious adverse effects were reported; one trial reported mild events including viral fever, headache, reflux, and allergic dermatitis.

The paper reports overall sleep SMD -0.59 (95% CI -0.75 to -0.42; I²=62%). Individual sleep outcomes were mixed: SQS, sleep-onset latency, total sleep time, WASO, and sleep efficiency favored Ashwagandha, whereas PSQI and total time in bed were not significant. Insomnia and ≥600 mg/day strata had numerically larger effects, but the subgroup interaction tests were not significant for insomnia status (p=0.22) or dose (p=0.09). Duration ≥8 weeks showed a larger effect and a significant subgroup interaction (p=0.01).

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the results as a beneficial effect on subjective and objective sleep, mental alertness, and anxiety, with no quality-of-life benefit. They acknowledge the small evidence base, limited elderly data, English-only inclusion, inability to assess publication bias with funnel plots, heterogeneous sleep instruments, and substantial heterogeneity/imprecision for multiple outcomes. Actigraphy is noted to estimate sleep from movement rather than directly measure sleep.

Independent appraisal adds a larger analytic concern: the repeated forest-plot rows and inflated pooled denominators indicate that multiple correlated sleep outcomes from the same trial participants were pooled as separate effect estimates. This likely violates independence assumptions of the conventional random-effects model and undermines the precision of the headline overall-sleep estimate and several subgroup analyses.

Authors’ conclusion

The authors conclude that Ashwagandha appears beneficial for sleep and suggest that ≥600 mg/day and ≥8 weeks may be more effective, while cautioning that serious-adverse-effect and long-term safety data are limited.

The dose claim is stronger than the subgroup evidence supports because the dose interaction test was not statistically significant (p=0.09). The duration subgroup difference was statistically significant (p=0.01), but it remains subject to the repeated-outcome dependence problem.

Supplemental material

The packet includes a completed PRISMA checklist, an explicit CENTRAL/MEDLINE search strategy, and the PROSPERO protocol. The protocol confirms the review question, RCT eligibility, placebo/no-treatment comparator, planned risk-of-bias and random-effects methods, secondary outcomes, and prespecified subgroup framework.

At registration, only preliminary searches had started; formal screening, extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, and analysis had not. The published article transparently notes changes from the protocol involving sleep-measure subgrouping and the age threshold.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Do not use for practice. Best used as a map of the early Ashwagandha-sleep RCT literature and as a source of individual outcome estimates. Do not use the pooled “overall sleep” estimate or its dose/insomnia subgroup comparisons as definitive evidence of clinical efficacy.

Relevance — 6/10

Directly addresses a commonly used complementary therapy for sleep and provides pooled estimates.

Evidence basis: Five placebo-controlled RCTs and clinically interpretable sleep outcomes.

Principal limit: Evidence is small, geographically narrow, and methodologically fragile.

Would raise/lower: Larger independent trials using standardized products and clinically meaningful endpoints would raise this.

Novelty — 7/10

The authors identify this as the first systematic review/meta-analysis focused specifically on Ashwagandha and human sleep outcomes.

Evidence basis: A dedicated synthesis of five RCTs with subgroup and GRADE analyses.

Principal limit: Novelty does not compensate for limitations in the pooled analysis.

Would raise/lower: Updated evidence with additional trials and corrected dependence handling would increase value.

Method strength — 5/10

Search, duplicate screening/extraction, Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment, random-effects pooling, PROSPERO registration, and GRADE are strengths, but the pooled “overall sleep” analysis appears to treat multiple outcomes from the same participants as independent effect estimates.

Evidence basis: The paper reports 400 unique participants but 1,764 entries in the overall-sleep pooling; forest plots show repeated rows from the same trials for different sleep outcomes.

Principal limit: Non-independence can overweight multi-outcome trials and produce over-precise confidence intervals; subgroup analyses inherit the same issue.

Would raise/lower: A multilevel/robust-variance model or one prespecified independent sleep endpoint per trial would materially strengthen the synthesis.

Evidence strength — 4/10

Most individual sleep metrics favor Ashwagandha, but certainty is limited by small trials, heterogeneous measures, repeated correlated outcomes in pooled analyses, and incomplete long-term safety data.

Evidence basis: Authors rate several primary outcomes moderate or low certainty; PSQI and total time in bed were not significant.

Principal limit: The headline overall estimate and subgroup denominators are not based on unique independent participants.

Would raise/lower: Larger trials with prespecified primary sleep endpoints and independent meta-analytic handling would raise confidence.

External validity — 4/10

All five RCTs were conducted in India, only one enrolled older adults, products and doses varied, and treatment lasted 6–12 weeks.

Evidence basis: Included populations covered healthy, stressed, elderly, and DSM-IV insomnia groups.

Principal limit: Generalizability to other populations, formulations, and long-term use is uncertain.

Would raise/lower: Multinational pragmatic trials and longer follow-up would raise this.

Implementation value — 4/10

The review suggests a possible sleep benefit but does not provide evidence strong enough to justify routine clinical adoption or a specific standardized product/dose.

Evidence basis: KSM-66 and Shoden were studied; some effects were observed at 120–600 mg/day over 6–12 weeks.

Principal limit: Dose subgroup interaction was not significant (p=0.09), serious adverse-effect data were limited, and formulations were not standardized across the evidence base.

Would raise/lower: Consistent product specifications, interaction-tested dose finding, and long-term safety data would raise implementation value.

Systematic-review validity

Strengths
  • Prospective PROSPERO registration.
  • Broad database/registry/grey-literature search.
  • Duplicate screening and extraction.
  • Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment.
  • Random-effects models and GRADE reporting.
  • Protocol changes disclosed.
Major limitations
  • Only 5 RCTs / 400 unique participants.
  • All trials conducted in India.
  • Short duration (6–12 weeks).
  • Heterogeneous sleep measures and products.
  • Insufficient studies for funnel plot.
  • Repeated correlated outcomes appear to be pooled as independent observations.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What the source supports: There is a reproducible signal across several short-term sleep measures that Ashwagandha may improve sleep relative to placebo, and the evidence base includes both subjective and actigraphy-derived outcomes.

What it does not support: A validated routine clinical dose, superiority of ≥600 mg/day over lower doses, long-term safety, generalizability beyond the studied populations/products, or a reliable single pooled “overall sleep” effect estimate.

Clinical use now: No practice change should rest on this review alone. The paper is better treated as background evidence that identifies a candidate intervention and the methodological requirements for better trials: standardized extract, prespecified primary sleep endpoint, objective + validated subjective measures, longer follow-up, and stronger safety ascertainment.
