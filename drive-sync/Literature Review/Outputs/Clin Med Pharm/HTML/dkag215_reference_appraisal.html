One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The review argues that high-dose (flu)cloxacillin/cloxacillin should no longer be treated as the unquestioned default for adult MSSA S. aureus bacteraemia because SNAP and CloCeBa show comparable efficacy for cefazolin with materially lower acute kidney injury and fewer adverse-event-driven discontinuations. For PSSA bacteraemia, SNAP similarly supports benzylpenicillin over (flu)cloxacillin when susceptibility permits.

The most important evidence summarized is the adult MSSA SNAP randomized comparison: 1341 adults were randomized to cefazolin or (flu)cloxacillin, with 90-day death in 97/645 (15%) versus 109/642 (17%), adjusted odds ratio 0.81 with 95% credible interval 0.59-1.12, posterior probability of non-inferiority 99.2%, and posterior probability of superiority 89.8%. The renal safety signal favored cefazolin: AKI by 14 days occurred in 92/660 (13.9%) versus 127/648 (19.6%), adjusted odds ratio 0.67 with 95% credible interval 0.50-0.89 and posterior probability of superiority 99.7%. Serious adverse events and adverse-event discontinuations were also lower with cefazolin.

The source is clinically useful because it integrates MSSA SNAP, PSSA SNAP, CloCeBa, CAMERA2, prophylaxis nephrotoxicity studies, and mechanistic plausibility into one practice-facing argument. Its main limitation is format: it is not a systematic review or guideline, does not perform a formal meta-analysis, and is written by trial investigators or contributors with relevant domain expertise. Its strongest use is as a high-yield bridge from the primary RCTs to stewardship policy review; its weakest use is as a sole authority for complex endocarditis, dialysis, pediatric, severe beta-lactam allergy, or local formulary decisions.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract states that existing MSSA SAB guidance has historically favored IV (flu)cloxacillin as first-line therapy, but the recently published SNAP trial found high AKI rates with (flu)cloxacillin in the largest randomized SAB trial to date. The authors frame this as evidence that common clinical practice needs periodic re-testing rather than automatic continuation.

The central claim is that cefazolin provides a safer and equally efficacious option for SAB, with the renal toxicity of high-dose (flu)cloxacillin likely under-recognized. The abstract is assertive and clinically directional; because this is a review, the strength of the claim depends on the underlying trial results rather than new analyses in this article.

Introduction

The introduction emphasizes the severity of SAB: mortality is described as 15%-30% despite effective antibiotics, and a large proportion of deaths are estimated to be infection-related rather than solely due to baseline frailty. The authors contrast this high-stakes syndrome with guideline recommendations that still favor antistaphylococcal penicillins, often based on older standards of care or lower-grade evidence.

The section introduces SNAP and CloCeBa as disruptive evidence. It notes that SNAP showed cefazolin non-inferior to (flu)cloxacillin for MSSA bacteraemia with higher AKI rates in the (flu)cloxacillin group, and that PSSA SNAP similarly found more AKI with (flu)cloxacillin than benzylpenicillin. The review also highlights baseline renal vulnerability in SAB populations, making kidney-sparing antibiotic choice clinically consequential.

SNAP trial findings

PSSA SNAP randomized participants with penicillin-susceptible S. aureus bacteraemia to benzylpenicillin or (flu)cloxacillin. AKI occurred in 17/153 (11.1%) adults treated with penicillin versus 27/124 (21.8%) treated with (flu)cloxacillin, adjusted odds ratio 0.50, 95% credible interval 0.26-0.94, posterior probability of superiority 0.984. Baseline chronic kidney disease and diabetes were broadly similar between arms.

MSSA SNAP randomized 1341 adults with methicillin-susceptible, penicillin-resistant S. aureus bacteraemia: 671 to cefazolin and 670 to (flu)cloxacillin, with 1287 in the intention-to-treat analysis after 4% withdrawal/loss to follow-up. Death at 90 days occurred in 97/645 (15%) with cefazolin and 109/642 (17%) with (flu)cloxacillin, adjusted odds ratio 0.81, 95% credible interval 0.59-1.12, posterior probability of non-inferiority 99.2%, and posterior probability of superiority 89.8%. AKI within 14 days was lower with cefazolin: 92/660 (13.9%) versus 127/648 (19.6%), adjusted odds ratio 0.67, 95% credible interval 0.50-0.89, posterior probability of superiority 99.7%.

CloCeBa trial findings

CloCeBa is presented as an independent smaller trial from France that supports the MSSA SNAP conclusion. In 315 patients with MSSA bacteraemia, cefazolin was non-inferior to cloxacillin for treatment success: 109/146 (75%) versus 108/146 (74%), treatment difference -1%, 95% confidence interval -11 to 9, P=0.012.

The review notes that 30% of CloCeBa cases lacked blaZ and were therefore penicillin-susceptible. AKI ascertainment was less routine than in SNAP, but severe AKI-related adverse events still favored cefazolin: day 7 events were 1/134 (1%) with cefazolin versus 15/128 (12%) with cloxacillin, treatment difference -11% (-18 to -6), P=0.0002. By end of study treatment, rates were 4/111 (4%) versus 17/99 (17%), treatment difference -13% (-23 to -6), P=0.0008.

Cefazolin and infective endocarditis with SAB

The review directly addresses the major clinical hesitation around cefazolin: the theoretical cefazolin inoculum effect, especially for blaZ-positive MSSA endocarditis. It notes that further CloCeBa strain analysis is planned to evaluate this issue.

In MSSA SNAP, 104 evaluable patients had infective endocarditis. Mortality was 8/51 (15.7%) for cefazolin versus 13/53 (24.5%) for (flu)cloxacillin, adjusted odds ratio 0.54, 95% credible interval 0.20-1.43, probability of non-inferiority 94.4%, and probability of superiority 88.9%. This is directionally reassuring but remains underpowered for definitive endocarditis-specific certainty.

Use of cefazolin in practice

The authors state that many services using (flu)cloxacillin are reviewing practice and moving toward cefazolin as first-line therapy for penicillin-resistant MSSA bacteraemia. They suggest the finding likely applies to all MSSA bacteraemia, including cases where penicillin susceptibility is unknown, but this is an extrapolative practice inference rather than a separate randomized result.

Implementation barriers are practical rather than purely evidentiary: cefazolin cost and supply vary by country, with the UK example showing higher indicative drug cost than flucloxacillin but lower administration burden, while Canada is described as having cefazolin at lower cost than cloxacillin. Formulary authorization during SNAP increased site access and may accelerate adoption.

Acute kidney injury and (flu)cloxacillin

This section broadens the renal safety argument beyond MSSA/PSSA SNAP. CAMERA2, a randomized MRSA bacteraemia trial of adjunctive beta-lactam therapy with vancomycin or daptomycin, was stopped for safety concerns: AKI occurred in 27% of those receiving adjunctive (flu)cloxacillin/cloxacillin versus 4% receiving adjunctive cefazolin. The authors describe this as an antecedent signal that made cefazolin the selected adjunctive beta-lactam in an ongoing SNAP MRSA domain.

The review also summarizes surgical prophylaxis literature showing higher AKI rates with flucloxacillin-containing prophylaxis, especially with gentamicin. Mechanistic discussion remains speculative but biologically plausible: reported acute tubulointerstitial nephritis exists, and the authors discuss renal tubular intracellular accumulation, lipophilicity, mitochondrial vulnerability of proximal tubules, and non-allergic tubular dysfunction as possible contributors.

Chronic kidney disease and (flu)cloxacillin

The review emphasizes that chronic kidney disease is common in SAB cohorts. It notes that in MSSA SNAP, cefazolin had a high posterior probability of reducing dialysis initiation during 90-day follow-up compared with (flu)cloxacillin, but there was no difference in ongoing dialysis at 90 days.

The long-term consequence of (flu)cloxacillin-associated AKI is not established in these trials, but the authors cite broader evidence that even mild AKI can contribute to later kidney disease, especially in sepsis. A proposed risk-mitigation approach from a French quasi-experimental study is to use cefazolin from the start in eGFR below 60 mL/min or any AKI risk factor, and to use lower-dose cloxacillin with therapeutic drug monitoring only when AKI risk is absent.

High anion gap metabolic acidosis

The source highlights pyroglutamic acidosis as a rare but potentially fatal high anion gap metabolic acidosis associated with concomitant paracetamol and (flu)cloxacillin. Because this drug combination is common and the syndrome is poorly recognized, management can be delayed.

SNAP had one reported case in the MSSA group, suggesting the adverse reaction could be more frequent than previously appreciated. The authors note that chronic kidney disease increases risk because both drugs and 5-oxoproline handling are affected; they also discuss metformin-associated lactic acidosis as a plausible additional HAGMA concern in haemodynamically compromised SAB patients with renal dysfunction.

Electrolyte disturbance

The review states that (flu)cloxacillin is well recognized to cause hypokalaemia in up to approximately 40% of hospitalized recipients, with lower rates seen with cephalosporins. The proposed mechanism is a larger non-reabsorbable anion and sodium load promoting distal potassium secretion and tubular flow-mediated potassium wasting.

The practical message is that clinicians should not only replace potassium but also consider the antibiotic as the cause. If (flu)cloxacillin is used, electrolyte monitoring and source-medication review are part of safe SAB management; cefazolin may avoid some of this monitoring burden.

Impact on microbiome and antimicrobial resistance

The review acknowledges a common stewardship concern: cefazolin is broader than (flu)cloxacillin and could theoretically exert greater antimicrobial-resistance pressure. The authors counter that direct evidence showing cefazolin causes more resistance than (flu)cloxacillin in MSSA bacteraemia is absent.

Both cefazolin and (flu)cloxacillin can affect the microbiome, and the claim that (flu)cloxacillin is meaningfully more microbiome-sparing remains unproven. No increased Clostridioides difficile infection signal was reported with cefazolin in SNAP, but the source does not provide a dedicated microbiome or resistance outcome analysis.

Allergies

The review notes that about 10% of patients report penicillin allergy, but fewer than 5% have true IgE-mediated hypersensitivity. Cefazolin has a dissimilar side chain to (flu)cloxacillin and is described as having negligible cross-reactivity below 1% in most penicillin-allergy contexts.

The authors cite Dutch guidance that cefazolin can be given safely to most patients except those with severe delayed reactions, who should avoid beta-lactams pending specialist assessment. Having both cefazolin and penicillinase-resistant penicillin options available can reduce unnecessary glycopeptide use for MSSA SAB.

Conclusion, summary box, and patient box

The conclusion states that SNAP substantially raises concern about high-dose (flu)cloxacillin nephrotoxicity. The authors argue that cefazolin is non-inferior to (flu)cloxacillin, probably superior for 90-day mortality, and clearly associated with fewer adverse effects, especially AKI.

The summary and patient boxes translate the evidence into clear messages: cefazolin is non-inferior and probably superior for MSSA SAB mortality, cefazolin and penicillin cause less AKI than (flu)cloxacillin, cefazolin is safe in most penicillin-allergy histories, and cefazolin may reduce electrolyte and metabolic complications. The patient box appropriately simplifies kidney injury frequencies but should not be used as a detailed prescribing guide.

Funding, transparency, declaration of interest

The review states that funders had no role in the review or included trials. SNAP adult domains received public and institutional funding across Australia, Canada, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Singapore, the UK, and South Africa; CloCeBa was supported by French public hospital and Ministry-linked funding.

Several authors report grant support, advisory roles, payments, royalties, stock/options, or leadership positions; others declare no conflicts. The most relevant interpretive issue is that many authors are closely connected to the SNAP/CloCeBa research ecosystem, increasing expertise but also making independent confirmation and primary-trial reading important.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-yield practice-facing synthesis for adult SAB pharmacotherapy and stewardship because it consolidates newly practice-changing randomized evidence and translates the renal safety signal into concrete antibiotic-selection concerns.
Relevance9/10

Directly relevant to inpatient ID pharmacy, SAB stewardship, renal safety monitoring, beta-lactam allergy interpretation, and formulary pathway review.

Evidence basis: Adult MSSA/PSSA SAB RCTs and implementation discussion. Limiter: Does not replace local guideline approval. Raise/lower: Higher if paired with local pathway; lower for pediatrics or non-SAB infections.

Novelty8/10

The cefazolin-versus-(flu)cloxacillin debate is old, but the article is timely because it integrates the largest SAB randomized platform results with CloCeBa and renal safety literature.

Evidence basis: Recent SNAP/CloCeBa results and renal adverse-event synthesis. Limiter: It is a review, not a new primary dataset. Raise/lower: Higher with formal meta-analysis; lower once guidelines fully incorporate these results.

Method strength6/10

The underlying evidence includes large randomized trials, but this article itself is a narrative review without a systematic search, prespecified inclusion criteria, pooled estimates, or independent risk-of-bias assessment.

Evidence basis: Structure and content of the review. Limiter: Narrative selection and author interpretation. Raise/lower: Higher with PRISMA/GRADE methods; lower if key contrary studies were omitted.

Evidence strength8/10

The main clinical claim is strongly supported by randomized data showing non-inferior efficacy and lower AKI with cefazolin, replicated directionally by CloCeBa and consistent with CAMERA2.

Evidence basis: MSSA SNAP mortality and AKI estimates; CloCeBa success and AKI estimates; PSSA SNAP renal signal. Limiter: Endocarditis subgroup and dialysis populations remain less certain. Raise/lower: Higher with subgroup-specific RCT certainty; lower if later trials conflict.

External validity7/10

SNAP's platform structure and multinational context improve generalizability, but practice translation varies by cefazolin access, antistaphylococcal penicillin selection, haemodialysis logistics, and local allergy pathways.

Evidence basis: Multisite trials and the review's access/cost discussion. Limiter: Children excluded; dialysis patients not recruited to SNAP MSSA/PSSA domains. Raise/lower: Higher with real-world implementation cohorts; lower in regions without cefazolin availability.

Implementation value9/10

The article provides enough quantitative signal and practical framing to justify cefazolin-first pathway review, renal/electrolyte monitoring emphasis, and avoidance of uncritical high-dose (flu)cloxacillin use.

Evidence basis: Clear RCT effect estimates, adverse-event framing, allergy and cost/access notes. Limiter: Dosing protocols and local operational details are not fully specified. Raise/lower: Higher with a validated implementation algorithm; lower if local antibiogram/supply constraints dominate.

Main strengths

  • Clinically important question with immediate implications for adult SAB definitive therapy.
  • Preserves key randomized trial effect sizes, denominators, and safety outcomes.
  • Does not focus narrowly on mortality; foregrounds AKI, adverse-event discontinuation, metabolic acidosis, hypokalaemia, allergy, cost, supply, and stewardship tradeoffs.
  • Explicitly recognizes implementation heterogeneity across countries and formularies.

Main limitations

  • Narrative review format creates selection and interpretation risk; no systematic search, meta-analysis, or formal evidence grading is provided.
  • Several authors are embedded in the SNAP/CloCeBa research ecosystem, which increases expertise but reduces independence.
  • Endocarditis data are directionally favorable but subgroup size is small and uncertainty remains wide.
  • SNAP MSSA/PSSA excluded baseline haemodialysis patients; the review argues cefazolin is practical in dialysis, but dialysis-specific efficacy/safety inference is indirect.
  • Long-term renal consequences of (flu)cloxacillin-associated AKI are plausible but not directly proven by the reviewed SAB trials.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Best useUse as a rapid, evidence-rich briefing for adult MSSA/PSSA SAB treatment pathway review, especially cefazolin-first policies and renal safety education.
Do not use forSole-source decision-making in pediatric SAB, dialysis-specific protocols, severe delayed beta-lactam reactions, or endocarditis cases where local ID guidance requires primary-trial review.
Likely practice implicationFor adult MSSA SAB, cefazolin is now strongly supportable as a first-line definitive beta-lactam when clinically appropriate and available.
PSSA implicationFor confirmed PSSA SAB, benzylpenicillin appears to preserve efficacy while reducing AKI compared with (flu)cloxacillin.
Monitoring implicationIf (flu)cloxacillin/cloxacillin remains in use, monitor serum creatinine, potassium, fluid/sodium burden, bicarbonate/anion gap when clinically indicated, and concomitant paracetamol/metformin risk.
Allergy implicationCefazolin is a useful option for many reported penicillin allergies due to low side-chain cross-reactivity; severe delayed reactions still require avoidance/specialist assessment.
Policy caution. This review supports a practice shift, but local implementation should still reconcile primary SNAP/CloCeBa papers, current institutional guidelines, susceptibility reporting, dosing references, renal adjustment policies, allergy algorithms, formulary status, and supply reliability.

Suggested local stewardship actions

  1. Compare current MSSA/PSSA SAB pathway against SNAP/CloCeBa findings and identify where nafcillin/oxacillin/(flu)cloxacillin remains default.
  2. Draft a cefazolin-first adult MSSA SAB review note with explicit exceptions: severe delayed beta-lactam reaction, selected CNS infection concerns, unresolved endocarditis/inoculum-effect policy questions, or organism/drug susceptibility issues.
  3. Add AKI and hypokalaemia monitoring prompts to any pathway that retains high-dose antistaphylococcal penicillins.
  4. Review concomitant acetaminophen/paracetamol use during flucloxacillin therapy if unexplained high anion gap metabolic acidosis appears.
  5. Use the primary RCTs, not this review alone, for committee-level policy documents or guideline revisions.
