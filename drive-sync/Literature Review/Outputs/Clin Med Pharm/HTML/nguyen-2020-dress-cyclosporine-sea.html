One-page synthesis

Verdict: Do not use for practice as comparative proof. Best used as early, hypothesis-generating evidence that short-course cyclosporine may be feasible for selected adults with DRESS, especially when corticosteroids are problematic. The study cannot establish that cyclosporine is equivalent or superior to systemic corticosteroids.

The authors compared 5 adults with definite DRESS treated with cyclosporine against 21 age- and sex-matched patients treated with systemic corticosteroids. Cyclosporine was given intravenously or orally at 3-5 mg/kg divided twice daily for 7 days, then tapered to 1.5-2.5 mg/kg divided twice daily for 7 days; controls received intravenous methylprednisolone 1-2 mg/kg daily followed by a variable prednisone taper. Treatment selection was clinically nonrandom: systemic steroids were contraindicated in 3 cyclosporine patients, and another patient started cyclosporine after mucosal lesions developed.

Across most Table 2 outcomes, reported mean times favored cyclosporine: halted progression 2.0 vs 2.8 days, erythema improvement 3.5 vs 5.5 days, erythroderma resolution 15.7 vs 21.7 days, and hospital stay 8.1 vs 16.2 days. Laboratory normalization also generally occurred earlier with cyclosporine, except kidney injury (6.5 vs 5.3 days). However, the article reports no confidence intervals or p values for between-group effects, only 5 cyclosporine patients were studied, steroid dosing was variable, and confounding by indication is substantial. Two cyclosporine patients discontinued therapy because of reversible adverse effects; 8 of 21 steroid controls had eruption recrudescence during prednisone taper requiring dose escalation.

Section condensation

Background

DRESS is framed as a potentially fatal hypersensitivity syndrome with skin eruption, fever, hematologic abnormalities, and multiorgan involvement. Standard management described by the authors includes withdrawal of the culprit drug, supportive care, and systemic glucocorticoids for severe disease with end-organ dysfunction. Cyclosporine is proposed as an alternative for patients in whom glucocorticoids are problematic, with a mechanistic rationale involving calcineurin inhibition, cytotoxic T-cell effects, and reduced interleukin-5 production.

The evidence gap motivating the study was limited clinical experience with cyclosporine for DRESS.

Methods

Adults admitted to Massachusetts General Hospital or Brigham and Women’s Hospital from January 1, 2008, through May 1, 2019, were eligible when DRESS was diagnosed by dermatology consultation and RegiSCAR score was greater than 5. One investigator reviewed dermatology consultations and progress notes to identify cyclosporine cases. Each case was intended to be compared with 4 age- and sex-matched controls treated with systemic corticosteroids.

Cyclosporine was 3-5 mg/kg divided twice daily for 7 days, then 1.5-2.5 mg/kg divided twice daily for 7 days; controls received IV methylprednisolone 1-2 mg/kg daily with a variable prednisone taper. Primary outcomes included time to halted eruption progression (cessation of worsening), improvement/resolution of erythema, and laboratory normalization; hospitalization duration was secondary. The study was IRB-approved with waiver of consent and reported as following STROBE guidance.

Results

Five cyclosporine-treated and 21 steroid-treated patients were analyzed. Overall, 18/26 (69.2%) were female; mean age was 43 years in the cyclosporine group and 44.5 years in controls. Cyclosporine treatment averaged 12.5 days versus 48.5 days for systemic steroids. Steroids were contraindicated in 3 cyclosporine patients (2 prior steroid-induced psychosis, 1 diabetes), and 1 other patient was switched because mucosal lesions developed.

Mean times favored cyclosporine for halted progression (2.0 vs 2.8 d), erythema improvement (3.5 vs 5.5 d), erythroderma resolution (15.7 vs 21.7 d), hospital stay (8.1 vs 16.2 d), fever normalization (3.0 vs 4.1 d), leukocytosis (8.2 vs 13.2 d), atypical lymphocytes (3.0 vs 6.8 d), eosinophilia (6.0 vs 7.3 d), and AST:ALT elevation (11.7 vs 13.4 d). Kidney injury normalized more slowly in the cyclosporine group (6.5 vs 5.3 d). Eight steroid controls (38.1%) had eruption recrudescence during prednisone taper requiring dose escalation.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpreted the cohort as additional evidence that cyclosporine may be effective and may shorten disease progression and hospitalization compared with steroids. They emphasized the shorter exposure window and stated that no relapse was observed in the cyclosporine cohort. Two cyclosporine patients discontinued treatment because of adverse effects, which were reversible after discontinuation.

Explicit limitations were the small sample, restriction to two academic institutions, variable corticosteroid dosing, and retrospective design. The authors concluded that treatment should account for disease severity and comorbidities and that a randomized clinical trial is needed.

Evaluation and appraisal

7/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses systemic treatment of severe DRESS, a clinically important but uncommon drug reaction.

Evidence basis: Comparative clinical outcomes and dosing are directly relevant to pharmacotherapy decisions.

Limiting factor: The question is narrow and the evidence predates current practice context.

Would raise/lower: Larger contemporary comparative studies or guideline integration would increase relevance for practice.

6/10

Novelty

Rationale: Provides a comparative two-center cohort rather than an isolated case report of cyclosporine for DRESS.

Evidence basis: Five cyclosporine cases were compared with 21 age- and sex-matched steroid-treated controls.

Limiting factor: The dataset is very small and observational.

Would raise/lower: Prospective comparative evidence with prespecified analyses would materially raise novelty and utility.

3/10

Method strength

Rationale: Retrospective case-control design with matching only on age and sex is highly vulnerable to selection and confounding by indication.

Evidence basis: The study used a defined RegiSCAR threshold and two academic centers, but treatment selection differed for clinical reasons.

Limiting factor: Only 5 cyclosporine patients, variable steroid dosing, no randomization, and no reported adjusted comparative model.

Would raise/lower: Randomization or a substantially larger adjusted multicenter cohort would raise this score.

3/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Reported group means consistently favored cyclosporine for many outcomes, but uncertainty and formal comparative statistics were not reported.

Evidence basis: Table 2 supplies denominators and mean/SD values for clinical, laboratory, and length-of-stay outcomes.

Limiting factor: No confidence intervals or p values for between-group effects; small sample and confounding can readily explain differences.

Would raise/lower: Effect estimates with uncertainty from a powered prospective comparison would raise this score.

3/10

External validity

Rationale: Generalizability is limited by the 5-person cyclosporine group and treatment at two tertiary academic hospitals.

Evidence basis: The cohort included adults with definite DRESS across varied culprit drugs.

Limiting factor: Small, selected sample and academic-center setting limit transportability.

Would raise/lower: Larger multicenter samples with broader settings and prespecified subgroup reporting would raise this score.

4/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The cyclosporine regimen is clearly reported, but the evidence is insufficient to establish superiority or a standard treatment pathway.

Evidence basis: Dose, duration, taper, comparator regimen, and several clinically interpretable outcomes are available.

Limiting factor: Safety details are sparse and comparative effectiveness is not causally established.

Would raise/lower: Prospective safety/effectiveness data and current guideline alignment would raise this score.

Validity

The central threat is confounding by indication: several cyclosporine patients received the drug specifically because corticosteroids were contraindicated or clinically problematic. Matching on age and sex does not balance disease severity, organ involvement, culprit drug, timing, comorbidity, or other prognostic factors. Variable steroid dosing further reduces comparator uniformity. The very small cyclosporine group makes estimates unstable and prevents meaningful safety characterization.

The results are descriptive. Absence of reported inferential comparisons means the study does not establish statistical compatibility with equivalence or superiority. The lower mean hospital stay and faster clinical/laboratory normalization are signals for future testing, not causal estimates.

Practice translation

Potential use: The article supports considering cyclosporine as a plausible alternative immunosuppressive strategy in selected DRESS patients when systemic corticosteroids are unsuitable, but only as low-certainty supportive literature.

Do not overclaim: This study does not determine first-line therapy, prove reduced mortality or organ injury, define comparative relapse risk, establish optimal cyclosporine monitoring, or demonstrate superiority over corticosteroids.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What is reusable
The reported cyclosporine schedule, eligibility definition, and outcome set are useful for protocol design and future evidence comparison.
What to monitor conceptually
Clinical progression, erythema, fever, leukocytosis, eosinophilia, hepatic markers, kidney injury, relapse/recrudescence, length of stay, and treatment-limiting adverse effects.
What should not change from this paper alone
Routine preference for cyclosporine over systemic corticosteroids or a universal DRESS treatment pathway.
