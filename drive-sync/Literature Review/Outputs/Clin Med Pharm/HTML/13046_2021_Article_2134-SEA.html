One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. Use this source as a 2021 map of high-dose intravenous vitamin C research, especially combination strategies, mechanisms and molecular profiling. Do not use it alone to choose cancer treatment or a clinical dosing protocol.

The review argues that route and exposure matter. It contrasts oral vitamin C, reported to reach no more than 220 micromol/L in plasma, with intravenous dosing that can produce millimolar concentrations. The authors assemble preclinical monotherapy and combination studies, early clinical studies, mechanistic work and omics data. The clinical literature in the review is heterogeneous and mostly early phase. Monotherapy studies showed tolerability and pharmacokinetic feasibility but no objective antitumor responses. Combination studies contain several response, progression and survival signals, but the review itself notes that most studies were small and that no large double-blind randomized or completed phase III trial had established efficacy at the 2021 cutoff.

Mechanistically, the review emphasizes pharmacological vitamin C as a pro-oxidant and also discusses cofactor-dependent effects on HIF regulation, TET enzymes, collagen and extracellular matrix biology, plus immune effects. Proteomic, transcriptomic and metabolomic studies add recurring signals in glycolysis, iron handling, energy metabolism, translation and DNA-related processes. These findings are hypothesis-generating because most mechanistic evidence comes from cells and animals. The authors propose potential biomarker-defined sensitive groups and practical dosing or screening considerations, but those statements remain review-level hypotheses and author synthesis rather than validated treatment-selection rules.

Best use

Section condensation

Abstract and background

The review presents high-dose intravenous vitamin C as a multi-target anticancer approach and states that early clinical studies support tolerability while stronger clinical efficacy evidence is lacking. It identifies the oral-versus-intravenous pharmacokinetic distinction as central. The review reports oral plasma concentrations no higher than 220 micromol/L and intravenous concentrations in the millimolar range, at least 15 mmol/L, which the authors connect to preclinical cytotoxic concentrations.

The stated aim is broad. The authors cover monotherapy, combination therapy, mechanisms, proteomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, research gaps and future clinical work. This breadth increases usefulness as a map but also means the paper is not centered on one reproducible clinical question.

High-dose VitC as a single agent

Preclinical studies summarized by the authors report activity around 1 to 20 mM. In advanced-cancer phase I monotherapy studies, the review reports doses up to 3 g/kg, plasma concentrations above 10 mM for several hours and a maximum reported concentration of 49 mM. Grade 3 or higher toxicities possibly related to intravenous vitamin C were uncommon in the cited small studies. No objective antitumor responses were reported. Stable disease occurred in 3 of 16, 2 of 24 and 1 of 24 patients in three cited studies.

The authors use these studies to emphasize dose and frequency. They cite 1.5 to 1.9 to 2.2 g/kg and at least three administrations per week as the more favorable monotherapy patterns. Palliative-care reports describe symptom and quality-of-life improvement, but much of this evidence is retrospective or observational. The source therefore supports feasibility and hypothesis generation more than efficacy.

High-dose VitC in combination treatments

The preclinical combination section is large. The review maps 71 studies from 1989 through 2021 and 59 anticancer agents or combinations. Table 1 focuses on 44 studies from 2016 through 2021. Most reported interactions favor sensitization or synergy, but the authors include an important counterexample in glioblastoma in which a lower vitamin C dose and nonstandard radiation schedule were associated with faster progression. This supports the authors' argument that dose, timing and model design need standardization.

The clinical section identifies 34 completed studies and 23 ongoing trials at the review cutoff, with 16 medium-to-high-dose studies in each group. Reported signals include an 8.75-month increase in median time to progression in a cited randomized ovarian study, tumor shrinkage in 8 of 9 patients in an early pancreatic combination study, an objective response rate of 58.3 percent in 24 evaluated advanced colorectal or gastric cancer patients, and longer median PFS and OS in a randomized phase II NSCLC study using vitamin C with modulated electrohyperthermia. These results come from heterogeneous, often small studies. The review does not establish a common effect size across cancers or regimens.

Anti-cancer mechanisms

The review makes pro-oxidant activity the central mechanistic explanation. Pharmacological concentrations can increase hydrogen peroxide and oxidative stress, while dehydroascorbic acid uptake and intracellular reduction may consume glutathione and NADPH in susceptible cells. The review also describes vitamin C as an enzymatic cofactor that can affect collagen synthesis, HIF signaling and TET-dependent DNA demethylation.

Additional preclinical work links vitamin C to epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, invasion, immune response and checkpoint blockade. The review notes stronger checkpoint-combination effects in immunocompetent mice in one study. These mechanisms can support biological plausibility, but they do not establish clinical benefit or identify validated patient-selection biomarkers.

Global molecular profiling studies

The omics section catalogs proteomic, transcriptomic and metabolomic studies. Proteomic findings recur in glycolysis, iron metabolism, cell-cycle regulation and translation. Transcriptomic work implicates apoptosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, iron handling, glycolysis, DNA replication and mismatch repair. The authors note that most transcriptomic studies used less than 1 mM vitamin C, which weakens direct relevance to pharmacological intravenous exposure.

The review identifies four metabolomic studies and reports that all showed reduced ATP and NAD after high-dose vitamin C. Upstream glycolytic metabolites accumulated while downstream metabolites fell, which the authors interpret as compatible with GAPDH inhibition and broader disruption of glycolysis and the TCA cycle. Omics data add mechanistic hypotheses but remain concentrated in model systems.

Conclusions and outlook

The authors propose several candidate sensitive groups, including KRAS-mutant tumors, TET2- or IDH1-mutant disease, tumors with altered iron handling, mismatch-repair deficiency and selected checkpoint-therapy contexts. They also discuss renal insufficiency and G6PD deficiency as safety considerations and suggest high-frequency intravenous regimens that reach millimolar plasma concentrations. These are review-derived proposals, not validated current treatment-selection rules.

The paper closes by calling for randomized phase III studies and stronger clinical validation. It also documents a trial-feasibility problem. Accrual can fail when the standard anticancer partner changes during a trial. The central evidence gap is therefore not mechanistic plausibility. It is proof that a defined vitamin C regimen improves patient-centered outcomes when added to a current standard regimen.

What would raise the score. A prespecified synthesis with new pooled or comparative analyses would raise the score.

Method strength

4/10

The authors report targeted PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov searches for selected subsets, but do not present a reproducible systematic-review method for the paper as a whole.

Evidence basis. The clinical-trial and recent preclinical sections disclose search terms and counts.

Principal limiting factor. No PRISMA-style selection process, study-level risk-of-bias assessment, or certainty framework is reported.

What would raise the score. A registered protocol, reproducible searches, dual screening, risk-of-bias assessment and certainty grading would raise the score.

Evidence strength

4/10

The clinical efficacy evidence is dominated by small phase I or II, observational and heterogeneous studies, while mechanistic support is largely preclinical.

Evidence basis. The review itself notes small sample sizes and no completed phase III trial at publication.

Principal limiting factor. Several quantitative efficacy signals come from uncontrolled or early-phase studies and cannot establish treatment benefit.

What would raise the score. Large randomized trials with patient-centered outcomes and transparent uncertainty would raise the score.

External validity

4/10

The literature spans many cancers, but dose, route, treatment partners, disease stage and study design vary substantially.

Evidence basis. Tables 1 through 3 show broad heterogeneity across models and clinical populations.

Principal limiting factor. Most clinical cohorts were advanced, heavily treated, small, and not designed to establish broad treatment effects.

What would raise the score. Replicated randomized evidence across defined tumor types and routine-care populations would raise the score.

Implementation value

4/10

The review offers practical historical dosing and safety observations, but it does not establish a current standard-of-care regimen.

Evidence basis. The paper discusses plasma targets, frequency, renal and G6PD cautions, and trial feasibility.

Principal limiting factor. The dosing suggestions are author synthesis from heterogeneous studies and are not current guideline recommendations.

What would raise the score. Current guideline, regulatory and phase III evidence tied to a validated regimen would raise the score.
