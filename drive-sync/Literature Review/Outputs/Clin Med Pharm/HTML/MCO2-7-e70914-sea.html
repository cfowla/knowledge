Authors: Andrea Papait; Anna Cargnoni; Franco Scaldaferri; Loris Lopetuso; Antonietta Rosa Silini; Ornella Parolini

Scope: Mechanisms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), current immune-targeted therapies, emerging miRNA-124, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) approaches, combination therapy, and biomarker-guided precision strategies.

Scope warning: This is a broad narrative review, not a guideline or systematic review. It synthesizes primary and secondary literature but does not report a reproducible systematic-search, study-selection, risk-of-bias, or certainty-grading method. Use it to understand mechanisms and the therapeutic landscape, not as a sole source for clinical treatment decisions.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The review argues that IBD is sustained by interacting immune, epithelial, genetic, environmental, and microbial mechanisms, while most available therapies act on only one or a few inflammatory pathways. The authors therefore favor precision-guided, multitarget strategies that combine inflammation control with microbiome restoration, immune rebalancing, and tissue repair.

The mechanistic synthesis is strongest as a map of interacting disease domains. Innate mechanisms include neutrophil/NET activity, macrophage polarization and IL-10 signaling, dendritic-cell activation, and innate lymphoid-cell biology. Adaptive mechanisms emphasize Th1/Th17 pathways, Treg heterogeneity, B-cell/IgA–IgG balance, and tissue-resident memory T cells. Genetic and epigenetic findings are integrated with microbial dysbiosis and metabolite changes. The review repeatedly stresses that UC and Crohn’s disease share core pathways but differ in immune polarization, tissue distribution, and depth of injury.

The therapeutic synthesis moves from anti-TNF agents to IL-12/23 and IL-23 inhibitors, anti-integrin therapy, JAK inhibitors, and S1P modulators, then maps these classes onto deeper STRIDE-II targets. It highlights that trial endpoints, populations, prior treatment exposure, and analytical methods remain heterogeneous, limiting direct cross-trial ranking. Emerging approaches include obefazimod/miR-124 modulation, FMT, and MSC/MSC-secretome therapy. The paper gives particular attention to MSC biology and the gap between encouraging preclinical/local-fistula data and inconsistent systemic/luminal results.

High-value clinical point: The review is useful for linking mechanism to therapeutic class and for understanding why “response” is no longer the only meaningful endpoint. Its Table 2 explicitly separates clinical, endoscopic, histologic, and transmural evidence.
Main evidentiary limitation: The paper sometimes combines regulatory approvals, pivotal trials, observational studies, animal studies, mechanistic experiments, and author-proposed precision hypotheses within the same narrative. Because no formal evidence-grading framework is reported, the reader must distinguish established clinical evidence from hypothesis-generating synthesis.

Section condensation

1. Introduction

The review presents IBD as a chronic immune-mediated disorder whose increasing global burden arises from interactions among genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, immune dysregulation, epithelial dysfunction, and the intestinal microbiome. It cites more than 10 million people living with IBD worldwide and emphasizes persistent failure of durable disease control despite expanding biologic and small-molecule options.

The stated objective is to integrate pathogenesis with therapeutic consequences. The authors organize the disease around genome, exposome, microbiome, and immunome domains; contrast UC and Crohn’s disease; evaluate established therapies against progressively deeper treatment targets; and examine miR-124 modulation, FMT, MSCs, and combination therapy as possible ways to move beyond single-pathway suppression.

2. Pathogenic mechanisms in IBD

Immune system. GALT must balance pathogen defense against tolerance to commensal and dietary antigens. In IBD this balance becomes unstable. Neutrophils and NETs can both worsen tissue injury and participate in resolution; ILC3/IL-22 activity is similarly context dependent. Macrophage IL-10 signaling supports tolerance and healing, whereas aberrant macrophage activation can promote fibrosis. Dendritic-cell abnormalities can amplify Th1-skewed inflammation. Adaptive immunity adds B-cell/antibody effects, Th1/Th17 responses, Treg plasticity, and tissue-resident memory T-cell persistence.

Genetics and microbiota. The review summarizes more than 240 susceptibility loci but notes that known variants explain only a minority of heritability. Single-cell and molecular profiling identify candidate anti-TNF resistance signatures such as oncostatin M and the GIMATS module, but the authors caution against expecting single markers to predict therapeutic benefit reliably. Dysbiosis is described across bacteria, fungi, viruses, short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan metabolites. Importantly, the review explicitly states that microbiome alteration has not been proven to be causally sufficient for IBD.

3.1 Current therapies

Anti-TNF therapy remains foundational but is limited by primary nonresponse, secondary loss of response, immunogenicity, and adverse effects. The review then covers ustekinumab; selective IL-23p19 inhibitors including risankizumab and mirikizumab; anti-integrin therapy; oral JAK inhibitors; and S1P modulators. It highlights several class asymmetries: IL-17 blockade can worsen Crohn’s disease; JAK inhibitor evidence is stronger in UC, although upadacitinib extends the class into Crohn’s disease; and gut-selective trafficking blockade has different depth-of-healing evidence between UC and Crohn’s disease.

The review places increasing weight on objective treatment targets. STRIDE-II is used to frame progression from symptoms and biomarkers to endoscopic, histologic, and transmural outcomes. The authors note that the clinical literature is difficult to compare because endpoint definitions, trial design, prior exposure, patient mix, and analysis differ substantially. They therefore favor direct head-to-head trials plus network meta-analysis and real-world comparative effectiveness evidence.

3.2 Emerging therapies

miR-124 / obefazimod. miR-124 dysregulation is linked to STAT3-driven inflammation. Obefazimod is presented as a small molecule that raises miR-124 and reduces inflammatory signaling. The review cites sustained Phase II extension results in moderate-to-severe UC and notes ongoing Phase III development.

FMT and MSCs. FMT has induced remission in multiple UC randomized trials but is constrained by variable protocols, modest/transient efficacy, donor and recipient heterogeneity, and transmission risks. The review does not support routine standard-care use. MSCs receive the longest treatment: animal models suggest immunomodulatory, regenerative, epithelial-barrier, and secretome/EV effects, but dose, route, timing, cell source, manufacturing, and potency remain unsettled. Clinical evidence appears more convincing for local treatment of perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease than for systemic therapy of luminal disease.

3.3 Potential combination therapies

The authors distinguish pharmacokinetic treatment failure from pharmacodynamic pathway failure and use this distinction to justify mechanism-based combinations. Infliximab plus azathioprine is cited as established evidence that combination therapy can improve corticosteroid-free remission and reduce antidrug antibodies. Dual-biologic and biologic–small-molecule regimens are described as promising for refractory disease but still limited by small cohorts, uncertain long-term safety, and cost.

The review proposes biomarker-anchored hypotheses involving HLA-DQA1*05, tissue oncostatin M/GIMATS signatures, IL12B/IL23R risk patterns, and microbiota enterotypes. These are explicitly framed as hypotheses requiring prospective testing. The negative biomarker-stratification result in PROFILE is used to argue that composite multiomic signatures will likely be necessary.

4. Conclusion and prospects

The final section argues that single-target anti-inflammatory strategies cannot fully address IBD heterogeneity. Future development should combine immune modulation with tissue repair, microbiome manipulation, and molecularly defined patient stratification. The review emphasizes miR-124 regulation, FMT, MSC/EV approaches, and rational combinations as platforms for multitarget therapy.

Translation remains constrained by weak standardization, inconsistent definitions of response/remission, manufacturing and potency challenges for cell products, donor and cryopreservation variability, and limited biomarker validation. The authors’ final position is that IBD management should move from isolated pathway blockade toward precision-guided immune re-education and mucosal restoration.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a current mechanistic and therapeutic landscape review, especially for connecting immunology, deeper treatment targets, microbiome concepts, and MSC/FMT development. It should not replace guidelines, drug labeling, or direct reading of pivotal trials for patient-specific decisions.
Relevance
9/10

Directly relevant to clinical pharmacotherapy, IBD biologics/small molecules, treatment failure, and emerging translational strategies.

Limit: breadth comes at the cost of depth for individual drugs.

Would rise: formal comparative decision framework tied to current guidelines.

Novelty
7/10

The genome–exposome–microbiome–immunome integration and multitarget/precision framing add value beyond a routine drug-class review.

Limit: many component mechanisms and therapies are established.

Would rise: prospective validation of the proposed biomarker-matched combinations.

Method strength
4/10

The article is a narrative review with extensive literature coverage but no reported systematic-search, eligibility, risk-of-bias, or certainty-grading method.

Limit: selection and weighting of evidence cannot be reproduced.

Would rise: systematic methods, prespecified questions, and formal evidence appraisal.

Evidence strength
6/10

Claims draw on pivotal trials, observational evidence, mechanistic studies, and preclinical models, with important negative findings included.

Limit: heterogeneous evidence levels are synthesized without formal grading.

Would rise: explicit separation and certainty rating of trial, observational, and preclinical evidence.

External validity
6/10

The clinical evidence spans multiple IBD phenotypes and therapeutic classes, but many emerging claims derive from models, selected cohorts, or early trials.

Limit: proposed precision subgroups are not prospectively validated.

Would rise: multicenter biomarker-stratified prospective validation.

Implementation value
7/10

Useful for class positioning, deep-remission concepts, treatment-failure mechanisms, and identifying investigational directions.

Limit: it is not an implementation guideline and does not provide patient-level sequencing algorithms.

Would rise: validated decision algorithms incorporating biomarkers, phenotype, prior exposure, safety, and cost.

Strengths

  • Broad mechanistic integration rather than a drug-only review.
  • Connects immune biology to differential therapeutic effects in UC and Crohn’s disease.
  • Preserves several clinically meaningful magnitudes and pivotal-trial examples.
  • Explicitly acknowledges negative or conflicting evidence, including IL-17 blockade in Crohn’s disease, mixed systemic MSC data, PROFILE biomarker failure, and ADMIRE-CD II.
  • Table 2 provides a useful depth-of-endpoint view rather than treating all “remission” outcomes as equivalent.

Limitations

  • No reproducible systematic-review methodology or study-level risk-of-bias assessment.
  • Clinical, preclinical, mechanistic, regulatory, and hypothesis-generating evidence are interwoven.
  • Some precision-medicine combinations are author-proposed hypotheses, not validated treatment strategies.
  • Large evidence tables can create a sense of comprehensiveness without resolving cross-study heterogeneity.
  • Regulatory and practice status can change; current guidelines and labels remain necessary for treatment decisions.
Clinical bottom line: Use this paper to understand why IBD treatment is moving toward deeper endpoints, mechanism-aware sequencing, and possibly multitarget approaches. Do not use the review alone to choose or combine advanced therapies, implement FMT, or initiate MSC-based treatment.

Confidence in appraisal: High for the paper’s content, organization, and internal limitations because the full 64-page source was reviewed. Moderate for claims about comparative therapeutic superiority because the article itself is not a systematic comparative-effectiveness review.

Practice and implementation takeaways

1. Separate failure mechanisms

When an advanced therapy fails, the review’s pharmacokinetic-versus-pharmacodynamic framework is clinically useful: inadequate exposure/immunogenicity and pathway mismatch imply different next steps.

2. Track deeper targets

Clinical remission alone is an incomplete endpoint. Endoscopic, histologic, and transmural outcomes should be distinguished when interpreting evidence and monitoring disease control.

3. Avoid class-wide assumptions

Mechanistic plausibility does not guarantee benefit. IL-17 blockade in Crohn’s disease and the mixed MSC trial record illustrate why direct clinical evidence must dominate pathway logic.

4. Treat emerging modalities as investigational

FMT, MSC/EV therapy, and biomarker-matched combinations remain sensitive to product definition, protocol, phenotype, and safety controls.

5. Demand composite validation

Single biomarkers have not reliably solved treatment selection. The paper’s strongest precision-medicine lesson is the need for prospective validation of composite molecular and clinical signatures.

6. Read pivotal sources for decisions

Use the review as a map. For prescribing, sequencing, safety, and regulatory decisions, return to pivotal trials, current guidelines, product labeling, and relevant regulatory communications.
