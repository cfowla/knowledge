One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In obese inpatients treated with IV unfractionated heparin for VTE, initial bolus and infusion doses were commonly lower than the contemporaneous weight-based recommendations, particularly at higher body weights, and lower infusion intensity per kilogram was associated with longer time to PTT >60 seconds.

The cohort contained 84 adults with BMI >=30 kg/m² treated for PE or DVT during 2004-2006. Median weight was 105.5 kg and median BMI 36.6 kg/m². The central process endpoint was time from heparin initiation to PTT >60 seconds, a hospital-specific threshold stated to correspond to factor Xa >0.3 units. Median time to that threshold was 18.5 hours (IQR 8-41); 28.6% were not therapeutic by 24 hours and 14.3% remained below threshold by 48 hours.

Underdosing was common relative to the article's cited 80 units/kg bolus and 18 units/kg/h infusion recommendations. Bolus dosing was below 80 units/kg in 89.3%. Initial infusion averaged 13 units/kg/h, and 76.2% of patients started more than 100 units/h below the weight-based recommendation. Nearly 29% received a fixed 1,000 units/h infusion. Initial dose per kilogram correlated inversely with time to therapeutic anticoagulation (Spearman r=-0.27, p<0.02), but the regression explained little of the variance (r²=0.07). The study therefore supports a prescribing-process problem and a surrogate delay association, not a demonstrated difference in VTE recurrence or bleeding.

Best use: evidence for recognizing weight-related UFH underdosing and fixed/capped-dose behavior as a medication-use-system failure mode in obesity.
Do not use for: establishing current UFH dosing policy, proving that higher initial doses reduce recurrent VTE, or proving that aggressive dosing is safe in obese patients.

Section condensation

Background and objective

The authors frame obesity as a VTE-recurrence risk factor and note that weight-based UFH formulas can produce unusually high initial doses in obese patients. They describe clinician reluctance to prescribe doses two to three times conventional fixed-dose habits because of bleeding concerns.

The study objective was narrowly operational: quantify actual UFH dosing, compare it with contemporaneous recommendations, and characterize underprescribing in obese VTE patients at one academic hospital.

Methods

Adults with BMI >=30 kg/m² and PE or DVT treated between January 2004 and December 2006 were identified from radiology/nuclear-medicine logs. Inclusion required continuous UFH that eventually yielded PTT >60 seconds. Standardized chart abstraction captured demographics, diagnosis, heparin start time, bolus, initial infusion, first PTT >60 seconds, and the final infusion sustaining PTT 60-90 seconds.

Actual boluses were compared with 80 units/kg, and initial infusions with 18 units/kg/h. Analyses used nonparametric correlations and simple linear regression. Time-to-therapeutic data were log-transformed for regression because of skewness. This design is descriptive/associational, not a randomized comparison of dosing strategies.

Results — population and time to therapeutic anticoagulation

Among 84 patients, 57.1% were women; mean age was 57.1 years, median weight 105.5 kg, and median BMI 36.6 kg/m². PE accounted for 80 cases and DVT for 4. Median time to PTT >60 seconds was 18.5 hours (IQR 8-41; range 4-96).

Twenty-four patients (28.6%) were not therapeutic within 24 hours and 12 (14.3%) were still not therapeutic by 48 hours.

Results — bolus

The mean bolus was 6,949 units or 58 units/kg; 75 of 84 patients (89.3%) received less than 80 units/kg. The authors calculated a 37.9% mean gap between 80 and 58 units/kg.

Neither absolute bolus dose nor bolus dose/kg showed a statistically significant correlation with time to PTT >60 seconds (r=-0.13, p=0.23; r=-0.19, p=0.09, respectively).

Results — continuous infusion

Initial infusion averaged 1,450 units/h or 13 units/kg/h. Sixty-four patients (76.2%) started more than 100 units/h below the 18 units/kg/h recommendation, and 24 (28.6%) received exactly 1,000 units/h. The mean recommended rate was 1,990 units/h, 27.1% above the mean prescribed rate. The final therapeutic rate averaged 1,576 units/h, only 8.0% above the initial prescribed average.

Time to therapeutic anticoagulation was not significantly related to initial units/h (r=-0.18, p=0.10) but was inversely related to units/kg/h (r=-0.27, p<0.02). The log-time regression estimated roughly 0.75-1.5 hours of additional delay for each 1 unit/kg/h decrement across the observed 6-22 units/kg/h range, but r² was only 0.07.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the data as showing clinician preference for conservative initial doses, often near the eventual therapeutic infusion rather than the higher guideline starting rate intended to reach target rapidly. Their informal interviews of 8 residents supported bleeding concern and misunderstanding about dose caps as possible explanations, but this was not a formal qualitative study.

The authors acknowledge limited generalizability from one teaching hospital, restriction to obese patients, absence of post-discharge recurrent-VTE follow-up, and no systematic bleeding-outcome assessment. They explicitly state that the study was designed to compare actual with recommended treatment, not to validate or refute the recommendation itself.

Conclusion

The authors emphasize two findings: frequent delays beyond 24 hours to adequate anticoagulation and frequent initial UFH bolus/infusion doses below contemporaneous recommendations. Their clinical warning is directed at overcautious initial dosing in obese VTE patients.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin/obesity dosing deep dive; do not use alone for current practice policy. The paper is a clear medication-use-system study showing how fixed or conservatively capped UFH dosing can systematically underdose heavier patients.

Evidence basis: The clinical question and population align tightly with UFH/VTE dosing review.

Limiting factor: Historical dosing framework and surrogate endpoint only.

External validity

Rationale: Applicability is constrained by one academic teaching hospital and an obese-only cohort from 2004-2006.

Evidence basis: The cohort spans a broad weight range and includes mostly PE patients.

Limiting factor: Local prescribing culture, laboratory PTT calibration, and historical practice may not generalize.

Would raise/lower: Replication across hospitals, eras, and monitoring strategies would improve generalizability.

8/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper provides a concrete failure mode: fixed/capped initial UFH dosing in obesity can create large dose deficits and slower therapeutic anticoagulation.

Evidence basis: Dose-gap figures and weight relationships are operationally clear.

Limiting factor: It should not be treated as a current dosing standard without updated guideline/local-protocol verification.

Would raise/lower: Contemporary validation linked to current UFH protocols would make it directly actionable.

Validity summary

Internal validity: Stronger for descriptive statements (what was prescribed, how far it differed from the chosen recommendation, and when PTT crossed the threshold) than for causal inference. The inverse dose/kg-time relationship is statistically significant but weak in explanatory power, and unmeasured clinical factors could influence time to therapeutic PTT.

Clinical evidence: The study does not measure patient-centered benefit or safety of guideline-concordant dosing. Its most defensible contribution is process evidence: weight-related underdosing was common and associated with slower attainment of a laboratory target.

PICO / clinical question

Population: obese adult inpatients (BMI >=30 kg/m2) with PE or DVT receiving IV UFH.
Exposure: actual clinician-prescribed initial UFH bolus and continuous infusion.
Comparator: contemporaneous weight-based recommendations (80 units/kg bolus; 18 units/kg/h infusion), plus descriptive comparison with final therapeutic infusion.
Outcome: time to first PTT >60 s; dose gaps and prescribing-pattern measures.
Not measured as study outcomes: recurrent VTE, major bleeding, mortality.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Medication-use failure mode

Fixed or capped infusions can create progressively larger underdosing as body weight increases. A protocol implementation should explicitly prevent accidental substitution of ACS-style caps or familiar fixed rates when the intended VTE pathway uses uncapped weight-based dosing.

Monitoring interpretation

The paper's endpoint is a local PTT threshold tied to that laboratory's factor-Xa relationship. Implementations should keep assay/therapeutic-range provenance explicit rather than treating “PTT >60 s” as universally portable.

What to test locally

Useful quality metrics include percentage below protocol bolus, percentage >100 units/h below protocol infusion, fixed-dose/cap frequency, and time to first therapeutic monitoring result stratified by body weight.

Do not overclaim

The paper does not show that more aggressive dosing lowers recurrent VTE or that it is equally safe with respect to major bleeding. Those questions require other evidence.
