Core thesis: Identical once-daily IP dosing does not produce equivalent exposure. Both drugs achieve high concentrations during the 6-hour antibiotic dwell, but cefalothin clears rapidly and is usually absent during subsequent antibiotic-free exchanges; cefazolin remains measurable and generally above the 8 mg/L target throughout 24 hours.

The most load-bearing quantitative contrast is the 24-hour exposure separation. Median plasma concentration at 24 hours was 0.0 mg/L with cefalothin versus 40.6 mg/L with cefazolin, median elimination half-life 3.01 versus 11.43 hours, and median clearance 10.72 versus 0.99 L/h. Correspondingly, median free-drug time above the 8 mg/L MIC was ~13% (plasma) and 25% (IP) for cefalothin versus 100% in both compartments for cefazolin.

Best-supported implication: If a first-generation cephalosporin is administered once daily by this IP regimen, cefazolin has much more favorable 24-hour exposure than cefalothin. Not established: clinical cure superiority, safety over a full treatment course, or the efficacy of a more frequent/continuous cefalothin regimen.

Section condensation

Background and objective

Peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis is commonly treated with intraperitoneal first-generation cephalosporins, but the authors identified limited modern PK data for cefalothin and cefazolin in infected PD patients. The key uncertainty was whether identical once-daily 15 mg/kg regimens produce adequate exposure throughout a 24-hour dosing interval. The study therefore measured both systemic and PD-effluent concentration-time profiles and interpreted free concentrations against an 8 mg/L MIC target.

Subjects and methods

This prospective observational PK study enrolled adults with clinically confirmed PD-peritonitis who had been on CAPD or APD for at least one month. Prior IP antibiotic therapy within two weeks excluded participation. During acute peritonitis, APD patients were switched to CAPD; all patients received four 2-L exchanges daily. Cefalothin or cefazolin was chosen by the physician, dosed at 15 mg/kg using actual body weight, co-administered with gentamicin in a 2-L dialysate bag, and allowed to dwell for 6 hours.

Blood was collected at 1, 3, 6, 7, and 24 hours; effluent at 3 and 6 hours and after the next three exchanges. UHPLC-MS/MS quantified total and free drug. Noncompartmental analysis estimated bioavailability, AUC, elimination/distribution rate constants, half-lives, clearance, and volume of distribution. Concentrations below quantification were set to zero. Continuous variables were reported as median (IQR) and compared with Mann-Whitney tests; categorical comparisons used Fisher exact or chi-square tests.

Results

Nineteen patients were studied: cefalothin n=8 and cefazolin n=11. Baseline differences were not statistically significant. Both drugs were highly absorbed intraperitoneally (median F about 0.92-0.93) and had comparable 6-hour PD effluent concentrations (~38 mg/L). Their systemic disposition then diverged sharply: cefazolin had much lower clearance (0.99 vs 10.72 L/h), smaller Vd (14.41 vs 39.69 L), longer elimination half-life (11.43 vs 3.01 h), and much larger plasma AUC.

After the antibiotic-containing dwell, cefalothin became rarely detectable in PD effluent and usually disappeared from plasma by 24 hours, whereas cefazolin concentrations remained elevated. Against an 8 mg/L target, cefalothin free plasma concentrations were above MIC in 7/8 patients at 1 and 3 hours, 2/8 at 6 hours, and none at 7 or 24 hours; effluent was above MIC in 7/8 at 6 hours and none thereafter. Median time above MIC was ~13% of the interval in plasma and 25% intraperitoneally for cefalothin versus 100% in both compartments for cefazolin.

Discussion and interpretation

The authors interpret the difference as a consequence of cefalothin's larger apparent distribution volume and faster elimination relative to cefazolin. They argue that a single identical once-daily regimen is pharmacologically mismatched across the two agents: cefalothin may leave long antibiotic-free periods below target, while cefazolin can remain substantially elevated at the next dose. Historical studies are discussed as broadly consistent with the observed short cefalothin half-life and prolonged cefazolin half-life in severe renal impairment/PD, although older assay methods complicate comparison.

The paper does not establish clinical superiority. Its endpoint is PK/PD exposure, not cure, relapse, catheter removal, hospitalization, resistance, or adverse effects. The authors therefore frame more frequent cefalothin dosing, cefazolin preference for once-daily regimens, and cephalosporin concentration monitoring as implications requiring further clinical evaluation.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include prospective acquisition, a predetermined clinically used regimen, complete 24-hour sampling, measurement in both plasma and infected PD effluent, and a validated specific chromatographic assay. Limitations include n=19, single-center enrollment, nonrandom physician-selected cephalosporin, terminal elimination estimated from only two samples, inability to robustly analyze transporter status or residual renal function, and no repeated-dose phase.

The supplement improves assay reproducibility detail and highlights the unresolved effect of residual renal function. It also notes an ISPD continuous cefalothin regimen (500 mg/L loading then 125 mg/L each exchange), but explicitly states that target attainment for that regimen was not established by this study.

Conclusion

The source supports a strong pharmacokinetic conclusion: once-daily IP cefazolin maintains exposure far more consistently across 24 hours than once-daily IP cefalothin in this cohort. It does not support a definitive clinical-outcome conclusion or an optimized alternative cefalothin regimen.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High-value PK evidence for understanding why cefazolin and cefalothin should not be assumed interchangeable under an identical once-daily IP regimen. Use as mechanistic/supporting evidence, not as stand-alone proof of clinical superiority.
8/10

Relevance

Directly addresses intraperitoneal beta-lactam PK/PD in PD-peritonitis and a clinically consequential once-daily dosing question.

Evidence basis: Real patients with active PD-peritonitis; plasma and effluent concentrations; MIC-based exposure assessment.

Limiting factor: Narrow drug pair and dialysis setting.

Would change with: Higher if tied to contemporary clinical outcomes or current guideline implementation.

7/10

Novelty

Provides direct contemporary chromatographic PK comparison of cefalothin and cefazolin during actual PD-peritonitis.

Evidence basis: Prospective sampling in the infected state and comparison across antibiotic-free exchanges.

Limiting factor: Small cohort and prior PK literature existed for each agent.

Would change with: Higher with repeated-dose modeling, population PK, or outcome linkage.

6/10

Method strength

Prospective predefined sampling and validated assay support internal measurement quality.

Evidence basis: Serial plasma/effluent sampling, UHPLC-MS/MS, noncompartmental analysis, registered protocol.

Limiting factor: Observational physician-selected drug exposure, n=19, single center, terminal phase based on two samples.

Would change with: Higher with larger multicenter design, prespecified comparative hypotheses, richer terminal sampling, and population PK modeling.

6/10

Evidence strength

The PK separation between drugs is large and internally coherent, but it establishes exposure differences rather than clinical superiority.

Evidence basis: Large differences in AUC, half-life, clearance, 24-h concentrations, and T>MIC with reported p-values.

Limiting factor: No clinical efficacy endpoint; MIC=8 mg/L is a surrogate threshold and repeated-dose behavior was not measured.

Would change with: Higher with treatment-failure/relapse outcomes and repeated-dose exposure-response data.

5/10

External validity

Findings are plausible for similar CAPD-managed PD-peritonitis patients but transportability is constrained.

Evidence basis: Clinically treated adults with varied transporter status and residual renal function.

Limiting factor: Single tertiary center; APD patients switched to CAPD; very small subgroups; old dosing/guideline context.

Would change with: Higher with multicenter enrollment including contemporary APD practice and broader organism/MIC distributions.

7/10

Implementation value

The study gives a concrete mechanistic reason to prefer cefazolin over cefalothin for once-daily IP dosing and to investigate cefalothin redosing/TDM.

Evidence basis: Cefalothin fell below the 8 mg/L target for most of the interval while cefazolin generally remained above it.

Limiting factor: No trial of alternative cefalothin regimen and no proof that PK target differences changed patient outcomes.

Would change with: Higher with validated dosing simulations, clinical outcome confirmation, and current guideline concordance.

Validity and bias

Selection/assignment: exposure was physician-selected, so the two drug groups were not randomized. Baseline measured variables were similar, but unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Measurement: concentration measurement is a strength because the study used a validated UHPLC-MS/MS assay and sampled both plasma and effluent. Analysis: noncompartmental analysis is appropriate for descriptive PK, but terminal estimates relied on only two samples and the cohort is too small for reliable subgroup modeling.

Outcome hierarchy: concentrations and T>MIC are biologically relevant but surrogate outcomes. The paper did not measure comparative cure, relapse, resistance emergence, catheter removal, mortality, or toxicity. The cefazolin accumulation concern over a 14-day course is therefore plausible but unmeasured.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use the study to explain why once-daily IP cefalothin may provide inadequate late-interval exposure compared with cefazolin.

Do not overclaim

Do not treat this as proof that cefazolin improves clinical cure or that cefalothin necessarily fails clinically.

Research next

Repeated-dose cefazolin accumulation, optimized cefalothin interval/continuous dosing, residual renal function effects, and exposure-response relationships.

Monitoring question

The source motivates cephalosporin concentration monitoring but does not validate a routine therapeutic range or a TDM algorithm.
