Scope warning
This study evaluates one institution's clinician-selected LI and SI UFH protocols. It does not establish equivalence or noninferiority, does not validate a universal anti-Xa target for high-bleeding-risk patients, and should not be used alone to set a therapeutic heparin protocol.

2. One-page synthesis

Core thesis
The authors observed similarly low rates of new or worsening thrombosis in patients treated with a low-intensity UFH protocol (anti-Xa target 0.25–0.35 IU/mL) versus a standard-intensity protocol (0.30–0.70 IU/mL), and propose that LI dosing may preserve efficacy in patients judged to be at high bleeding risk. The data are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory because protocol assignment was nonrandom and strongly confounded by clinician selection.

What the data support
  • In this cohort, thrombotic event counts were low (5 LI, 8 SI) and the adjusted comparison was not statistically significant: OR 0.93 (95% CI 0.29–2.98), p=0.899.
  • Bleeding and transfusion were numerically more common in LI patients, who also entered treatment with materially greater bleeding risk.
  • The study documents a workable institution-specific anti-Xa protocol and provides operational detail suitable for benchmarking.
What the data do not establish
  • Noninferiority or equivalence of LI and SI anticoagulation.
  • Causal reduction in bleeding from LI dosing; the LI group actually had higher observed bleeding.
  • That 0.25–0.35 IU/mL is an optimal anti-Xa target for a broadly defined high-risk population.
  • That HAS-BLED is invalid for UFH-treated patients; formal discrimination/calibration testing was not reported.
Central appraisal
The phrase “comparable efficacy” rests mainly on statistical non-significance in a small number of thrombotic events. The 95% CI for the adjusted thrombotic OR spans substantial benefit and substantial harm, and the study was not designed as an equivalence/noninferiority trial. Absence of a detected difference is therefore not evidence that the protocols are clinically equivalent.

4. Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames uncertainty around optimal therapeutic UFH initiation and maintenance and reports the retrospective comparison of LI and SI protocols in 377 patients. It highlights anti-Xa targets of 0.25–0.35 IU/mL for LI and 0.30–0.70 IU/mL for SI, with LI patients characterized by more prior bleeding and lower baseline hemoglobin.

It reports no significant difference in new/worsening thrombosis (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.29–2.98; p=0.899) and no significant adjusted difference in bleeding (OR 1.21, 95% CI 0.51–2.89; p=0.667), then concludes that LI may have comparable efficacy and that 0.25–0.35 IU/mL may be more optimal in high-risk patients. The latter claim is stronger than the observational evidence can establish.

Introduction

The introduction describes long-standing variation in UFH dosing and monitoring and notes the shift toward weight-based nomograms. It cites the 2012 ACCP recommendation of an 80 units/kg bolus followed by 18 units/kg/h, adjusted to therapeutic levels.

The study rationale is the sparse comparative evidence for lower-intensity UFH strategies in patients at high hemorrhagic risk and the need to understand how patient-specific bleeding/thrombotic factors relate to protocol selection and outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Adults treated with either institutional LI or SI UFH protocols were retrospectively identified. The protocols used anti-Xa rather than aPTT for routine titration, with distinct targets and starting doses. Actual body weight was used at ≤125 kg and adjusted body weight at >125 kg. Anti-Xa levels were obtained six hours after initiation and every six hours until two consecutive therapeutic values, then each morning.

Manual EMR review was recorded in REDCap. Primary endpoints were new/worsening thrombus and bleeding; secondary endpoints were transfusion within one month and death within three months. Statistical testing used Fisher exact tests, independent t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, followed by multivariable logistic regression built from variables significant at p<0.05 in univariate analysis using stepwise selection.

Results

LI patients were a clinically higher-risk group: previous bleeding 24.1% vs 12.8%, initial hemoglobin 9.8 vs 11.6 g/dL, and surgery within one week 34.8% vs 11.9%. SI patients more often had VTE as the treatment indication (53.4% vs 38.0%) and more often received an initial bolus (84.0% vs 65.2%). Median time to therapeutic level was longer with LI (5.5 vs 3.3 hours).

New/worsening thrombosis occurred in 5/158 LI patients (3.2%) and 8/219 SI patients (3.7%); bleeding occurred in 18/158 (11.4%) and 16/219 (7.3%). Major bleeding was 5.0% vs 2.7%, transfusion within one month 29.7% vs 16.4%, and death within three months 15.8% vs 11.0%. Adjusted ORs were 0.93 (0.29–2.98) for thrombosis, 1.21 (0.51–2.89) for bleeding, and 0.95 (0.50–1.80) for three-month mortality.

Discussion

The authors interpret the low and statistically similar thrombosis rates as support for possible LI efficacy while attributing the higher bleeding and transfusion rates to confounding by selection of higher-risk patients into LI. They also note that identical median HAS-BLED scores despite meaningful clinical differences suggest the score may not be useful for choosing UFH intensity.

The discussion appropriately acknowledges retrospective, nonrandomized design and higher baseline bleeding risk in LI patients. However, it does not fully address the inferential consequence: the groups cannot reliably determine the causal effect of anticoagulation intensity on either bleeding or thrombosis, and the low event counts leave clinically important differences unresolved.

Conclusions

The authors conclude that LI UFH may have comparable efficacy to SI UFH and call for a prospective randomized trial matched for comorbidities, risk factors, and indication. The call for prospective testing is justified; the efficacy conclusion should be read as a hypothesis rather than demonstrated equivalence.

Additional information / disclosures

The paper reports University of Florida IRB approval (IRB201702116), states that consent was obtained, and reports no financial support, relevant financial relationships, or other relationships that could appear to influence the work.

6. Validity & statistical appraisal

Selection bias / confounding
Major limitation.
Protocol choice was explicitly based on physician assessment of bleeding and thrombotic risk. LI patients had more prior bleeding, lower hemoglobin, much more recent surgery, and a different indication mix.
Precision
Low for primary clinical comparisons.
Only 13 thrombotic events and 34 bleeding events occurred. The adjusted confidence intervals are broad.
Comparator validity
Weak.
LI and SI groups differed not only in anti-Xa target but also in bolus frequency, starting infusion rate, and clinical risk profile.
Outcome quality
Mixed.
Thrombosis and bleeding are clinically meaningful. Transfusion and 3-month mortality are less specific to the UFH strategy without adjudicated attribution.
Modeling strategy
Potential instability.
Variables entered multivariable modeling only after univariate p<0.05 screening, followed by stepwise selection. With few events, this can produce unstable estimates; the final covariate set is not shown in Table 5.
Equivalence claim
Not tested.
No noninferiority/equivalence margin, power calculation, or prespecified clinically acceptable difference is reported.

Additional reporting concerns

  • No duration of UFH exposure, time in therapeutic range, proportion of anti-Xa values in range, or cumulative dose is reported, limiting dose–exposure–outcome interpretation.
  • No prespecified sample-size/power calculation is reported.
  • Indications are heterogeneous, and subgroup-specific efficacy/safety estimates are not reported.
  • The paper does not report how new/worsening thrombosis was systematically sought or adjudicated, raising the possibility that detection intensity varied by clinical context.
  • Bleeding adjudication detail is limited beyond the major/minor definition; source Table 4 provides counts but not timing relative to UFH initiation or dose changes.
  • Transfusion within one month and mortality within three months can be influenced by surgery, malignancy, acute illness, and other post-UFH events.

7. Practice translation & currency check

Useful for practice
The paper is useful as an example of how one center operationalized a lower-intensity anti-Xa UFH pathway, including starting doses, bolus approach, titration increments, monitoring cadence, and a >125 kg adjusted-body-weight rule. It can inform local protocol comparison, safety review, and hypothesis generation.
Not sufficient for practice change
The study should not independently justify adopting 0.25–0.35 IU/mL as a therapeutic target, replacing a standard therapeutic range, or asserting equivalent antithrombotic efficacy. A local change would require synthesis with current institutional outcomes, laboratory assay characteristics, current labeling/guidance, and stronger comparative evidence.

External currency verification — clearly separate from the 2020 paper

A current U.S. FDA heparin sodium label (2025 revision for one heparin sodium product) permits coagulation monitoring with anti-Factor Xa and reports an aPTT target of 60–85 seconds as corresponding to an anti-Factor Xa level of 0.35–0.70. This external source does not validate the study's 0.25–0.35 IU/mL LI range and underscores that the LI target should be treated as an institution-specific investigational strategy rather than a generally established therapeutic target.

9. Implementation / practice takeaways

Preserve
  • Anti-Xa-based titration workflow and monitoring cadence.
  • Explicit distinction between LI and SI bolus/infusion strategies.
  • Recognition that high bleeding risk drives real-world protocol selection and confounds retrospective comparisons.
Do not overclaim
  • “No statistically significant difference” ≠ equivalence.
  • Higher observed bleeding in LI does not prove LI is harmful because patients were selected for higher baseline risk.
  • The study does not establish 0.25–0.35 IU/mL as optimal.
If designing a local protocol study
  • Prespecify bleeding-risk eligibility for lower intensity.
  • Use matched/weighted or randomized assignment if feasible.
  • Track UFH duration, anti-Xa time-in-range, cumulative dose, bolus exposure, and reasons for interruptions.
  • Use adjudicated thrombosis/bleeding outcomes and clinically meaningful time windows.
If validating a risk score
  • Test discrimination and calibration rather than comparing median score alone.
  • Assess prediction of major bleeding specifically during UFH exposure.
  • Consider surgical status, baseline hemoglobin, recent bleeding, concomitant antithrombotics, and indication.
