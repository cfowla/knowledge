Scope warning: This study describes one center's historical anti-Xa-based UFH protocols and an observational comparison. It does not establish equivalence, noninferiority, or a causal safety/efficacy advantage of the low-intensity protocol, and should not be used alone to set a therapeutic UFH target.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In 377 retrospectively selected patients, observed thrombosis rates were similar under low-intensity and standard-intensity UFH protocols, while bleeding and transfusion were numerically more frequent in the low-intensity group. The low-intensity group was also substantially higher risk at baseline, making causal comparison difficult.

The institutional protocols differed materially: LI used an anti-Xa target of 0.25-0.35 IU/mL, optional 60 units/kg bolus, and 12 units/kg/hr initial infusion; SI used 0.30-0.70 IU/mL, suggested 80 units/kg bolus, and 18 units/kg/hr. New/worsening thrombosis occurred in 3.2% vs 3.7% (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.29-2.98; p=0.899). Bleeding occurred in 11.4% vs 7.3% (OR 1.21, 95% CI 0.51-2.89; p=0.667). Major bleeding was 5.0% vs 2.7%, and transfusion within one month was 29.7% vs 16.4%.

The main interpretive constraint is confounding by indication: protocol assignment was clinician-selected, and LI patients had more prior bleeding (24.1% vs 12.8%), lower baseline hemoglobin (9.8 vs 11.6 g/dL), and more recent surgery (34.8% vs 11.9%). The rare thrombosis outcome yields a very wide confidence interval, so the data are compatible with substantial benefit or harm. The paper is most useful as a concrete institutional nomogram and a hypothesis-generating comparison, not as evidence that the LI target is clinically equivalent or superior.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame UFH protocol intensity as unresolved and report a retrospective comparison of LI and SI anti-Xa-based protocols. They state that thrombosis incidence was comparable and that LI patients bled more often, while suggesting that the 0.25-0.35 IU/mL LI target could be useful in high-risk patients.

The abstract's practical claim is stronger than the design can establish: LI patients were clinically selected because they were higher risk, and the study was not designed for noninferiority or equivalence.

Introduction

The introduction reviews variability in UFH dosing and notes evolution from fixed dosing toward weight-based nomograms. It identifies limited evidence comparing adverse events and efficacy between deliberately lower- and standard-intensity therapeutic protocols.

The study aim is to compare hemorrhage and thrombotic outcomes while considering patient-level factors that may influence protocol choice and outcome risk.

Materials and Methods

Adults treated with therapeutic UFH from July 2011 to July 2017 were retrospectively identified. Separate ACS-protocol patients and those receiving thrombolytics were excluded. LI and SI protocols were monitored with anti-Xa; protocol choice was at physician discretion. Total body weight was used through 125 kg and adjusted body weight above 125 kg.

Primary outcomes were new/worsening thrombus and bleeding on UFH; bleeding was graded using ISTH definitions. Secondary outcomes were transfusion within one month and death within three months. Data came from manual EMR review and REDCap. Analyses used univariate testing followed by stepwise multivariable logistic regression.

Results

LI patients comprised 42.0% of the cohort. They had slower median time to therapeutic level (5.5 vs 3.3 h) and were more likely to have prior bleeding, home oral anticoagulant use, lower baseline hemoglobin, and recent surgery. SI patients were more often treated for VTE and more often received the initial bolus.

Thrombosis was 3.2% LI vs 3.7% SI; bleeding 11.4% vs 7.3%; transfusion 29.7% vs 16.4%; 3-month death 15.8% vs 11.0%. Multivariable ORs were 0.93 for thrombosis, 1.21 for bleeding, and 0.95 for death; all confidence intervals crossed 1 and p values were non-significant. Major bleeding was 5.0% vs 2.7%.

Discussion

The authors interpret the low and similar thrombosis rates as support for comparable efficacy, but attribute the numerically higher bleeding and transfusion in LI to selection of higher-risk patients. They also report that HAS-BLED distributions were similar despite these risk differences and argue that HAS-BLED is not useful for UFH bleeding-risk selection.

The authors acknowledge the retrospective, nonrandomized design. The discussion does not eliminate residual confounding, and it cannot demonstrate that lowering the anti-Xa target caused similar thrombosis outcomes or improved safety.

Conclusions

The paper concludes that LI may have comparable efficacy to SI based on similar observed thrombus incidence and proposes future prospective randomized comparison.

A defensible reading is narrower: the study supports feasibility and hypothesis generation for a lower anti-Xa target in selected patients, but the data do not prove equivalence or define an optimal target.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin-protocol deep dive because it directly compares two anti-Xa therapeutic UFH intensity strategies and publishes the operational nomograms. Do not use it alone for practice change.

Validity and bias

  • Confounding by indication: LI was intentionally chosen for perceived high bleeding risk.
  • Baseline imbalance: prior bleeding, hemoglobin, surgery, oral anticoagulants, and VTE indication differ materially.
  • Rare outcomes / imprecision: only 13 thrombotic events; OR CI 0.29-2.98.
  • No equivalence design: non-significance does not establish comparable efficacy.
  • Stepwise regression: the exact final covariate set is not presented in the main text/table, limiting appraisal of residual confounding.
  • Source-data anomalies: duplicated Table 1 row, platelet unit likely typographic, and prose/table mismatch for mortality CI.

Clinical translation

Best use: historical comparator, protocol-design example, and evidence that a lower anti-Xa target has been operationalized in a tertiary center with low observed thrombosis rates in selected patients.

Do not use for: claiming LI is equivalent to SI, claiming LI lowers bleeding, or selecting 0.25-0.35 IU/mL as an optimal target without stronger and more current evidence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Protocol architecture
The paper gives a reproducible anti-Xa nomogram with distinct bolus, starting rate, titration, monitoring, and high-weight rules.
Risk-selection problem
Clinician-selected “low intensity” creates a different-risk population; comparing outcomes without careful adjustment can make safety conclusions misleading.
Evidence interpretation
Similar event percentages plus non-significant p values are not evidence of equivalence. The wide thrombosis CI is the key quantitative caution.
Risk score finding
HAS-BLED did not discriminate the groups' apparent bleeding risk in this cohort; that observation is hypothesis-generating rather than validation of a new score.

Bottom line: useful protocol-level evidence and implementation detail; low-to-moderate certainty for comparative clinical effects. Confidence in this appraisal is high for source extraction and moderate for practice translation because no external/current-practice verification was part of this run.
