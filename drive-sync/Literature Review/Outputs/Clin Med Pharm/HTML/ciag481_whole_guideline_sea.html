Executive synthesis

One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first

Best use: a high-value operational decision framework for modern US management of ESBL-E, AmpC-E, CRE, DTR P. aeruginosa, CRAB, and invasive S. maltophilia. Do not use it as: a uniformly graded evidence hierarchy or a substitute for primary-study appraisal when a recommendation rests on thin or indirect data.

Core thesis. The guidance’s major contribution is not a drug list; it is a layered decision architecture. Treatment choice is repeatedly conditioned on infection site, illness severity, source control, organism identification, actual AST, resistance mechanism/carbapenemase family, PK/PD exposure, toxicity, and stewardship value. This produces clinically important distinctions that antibiogram-only reasoning misses: carbapenem resistance is not synonymous with DTR P. aeruginosa; KPC, NDM, and OXA-48-like CRE require different therapies; apparent susceptibility may not rescue cefepime for ESBL-E or meropenem-vaborbactam for OXA-48-like CRE; and CRAB and S. maltophilia require deliberate infection-versus-colonization assessment before treatment.

Evidence thesis. The document is clinically sophisticated but methodologically heterogeneous. Some major recommendations have strong direct anchors—the randomized ESBL-E bloodstream trial, the randomized sulbactam-durlobactam CRAB trial, registration trials for selected cUTI agents, and negative randomized inhaled-antibiotic trials. Other recommendations are largely reconstructed from observational cohorts, small trial subgroups, surveillance, PK/PD, animal models, resistance mechanisms, and expert risk management. The panel often makes that uncertainty visible, but the absence of GRADE means recommendation wording should not be interpreted as a standardized certainty level.

Most mature clinical anchor

Invasive ESBL-E: carbapenem preference is anchored by randomized bloodstream-infection evidence.

Most important 2026 hierarchy change

CRAB: sulbactam-durlobactam plus a carbapenem is preferred; high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam is a bridge when the preferred regimen is not immediately available.

Most important mechanism rule

CRE must be stratified by carbapenemase family; NDM and OXA-48-like biology can override otherwise familiar β-lactam choices.

Weakest clinical evidence zone

Invasive S. maltophilia: the preferred/alternative hierarchy depends heavily on microbiology, PK/PD, and animal evidence.

Most important negative evidence

Routine definitive combination therapy and routine nebulized antibiotics are repeatedly constrained when clinical benefit is unproven and toxicity or burden is real.

Most important implementation dependency

Reliable, current AST—including repeat AST after therapeutic exposure—is central across CRE and DTR P. aeruginosa and materially affects CRAB and S. maltophilia.

Guideline design

Methodological frame and governance

A panel of six infectious diseases specialists developed 41 pathogen-specific treatment questions. The source describes a comprehensive but not necessarily systematic literature review supplemented by clinical expertise and consensus. It does not use GRADE, does not provide formal evidence-certainty labels, and does not present a reproducible database/search strategy, study-selection flow, or standardized study-level risk-of-bias process. Therefore, each “Suggested approach” must be interpreted through its underlying Rationale rather than assumed to carry a uniform recommendation strength.

The guidance is unusually explicit about practical assumptions: organism identification, in-vitro susceptibility, differentiation of infection from colonization, prior microbiology, recent antibiotic exposure, local epidemiology, source control, patient stability, and feasible oral exposure. Conflict-of-interest governance is described, and disclosures extend across pages 67–68. Multiple panelists disclosed consulting, advisory, research-funding, editorial, or industry relationships; IDSA describes a formal review process. Disclosure itself is not evidence of bias, but it is relevant governance context for a six-member expert panel.

Methodological strength: the Rationale sections often expose uncertainty, conflicting results, treatment-emergent resistance, AST caveats, and negative evidence instead of presenting recommendations as more certain than the supporting data.
Methodological limitation: without systematic search reporting, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or GRADE, a reader cannot reconstruct how evidence was selected or map recommendation wording to a standardized certainty/strength scale.
Across all six sections

Cross-cutting treatment principles

1. Infection site is a treatment variable

uUTI, cUTI, bloodstream infection, pneumonia, and tissue infection are repeatedly separated because urine, serum, epithelial-lining-fluid, and tissue exposures differ.

2. “Susceptible” is not always enough

ESBL cefepime, OXA-48 inhibitor combinations, AmpC-vulnerable β-lactams, polymyxins, and tetracyclines illustrate why mechanism and exposure can override a simple S result.

3. Source control and severity modify drug choice

Critical illness, persistent source, high burden, bacteremia, and hypoalbuminemia can push therapy toward more reliable systemic exposure.

4. Mechanism-directed therapy is central

Carbapenemase family is a major branch point for CRE and a meaningful modifier for P. aeruginosa.

5. Stewardship constrains otherwise-active drugs

Novel CRE-active agents are often deliberately reserved when narrower urinary or traditional β-lactam options remain effective.

6. Resistance emergence is an outcome

The guidance evaluates not only immediate efficacy but the probability that therapy will select resistance, especially with KPC-E and DTR P. aeruginosa.

7. Definitive combination therapy is not default

CRE and DTR P. aeruginosa are generally de-escalated to one active β-lactam; CRAB is the major section where combination therapy is the default architecture.

8. Colonization matters

CRAB and S. maltophilia evidence is especially vulnerable to respiratory/wound colonization misclassification.

9. Oral step-down requires four conditions

Susceptible oral agent, hemodynamic stability, adequate site exposure, and adequate GI absorption—not formulation availability alone.

10. AMR does not automatically mean longer duration

Duration generally follows syndrome, source control, immune status, and response rather than resistance phenotype alone.

Section 1

ESBL-E

Evidence maturity: Moderate–high clinical maturity

The ESBL-E section is the most clinically conventional part of the guidance: site and severity drive treatment. Uncomplicated UTI is deliberately broad-spectrum-sparing; cUTI favors susceptible TMP-SMX or fluoroquinolones when feasible; invasive non-urinary infection remains carbapenem-centered, with meropenem or imipenem favored over ertapenem in critical illness or hypoalbuminemia. The section repeatedly rejects the idea that a susceptible laboratory result is always enough: piperacillin-tazobactam and cefepime are constrained by AST reliability, inoculum/β-lactamase biology, and clinical outcome signals.

Evidence is comparatively strong for the central invasive-disease question. A randomized bloodstream-infection trial included 391 patients and showed 30-day survival of 88% with piperacillin-tazobactam versus 96% with meropenem; reanalysis by broth microdilution attenuated statistical significance but preserved the direction favoring meropenem. cUTI and newer oral/urinary options also have randomized or subgroup evidence, while cephamycins and several special situations remain observational or mechanistically inferred. The result is a relatively mature section, but not a uniformly high-certainty one.

Section 2

AmpC-E

Evidence maturity: Moderate; mechanistically strong, clinically heterogeneous

The AmpC section replaces historical mnemonics with species-specific and mechanism-specific reasoning. E. cloacae complex, K. aerogenes, C. freundii, and probably H. alvei are treated as the main moderate-risk inducible AmpC organisms; lower-risk species can generally follow AST. Cefepime is the central carbapenem-sparing β-lactam because it combines low induction potential with relative stability to AmpC hydrolysis; ceftriaxone-class agents and piperacillin-tazobactam are avoided for invasive disease because derepressed AmpC can compromise them during therapy.

The mechanistic logic is strong, but direct comparative clinical evidence is incomplete. The guidance cites roughly 20% resistance emergence after ceftriaxone exposure in moderate-risk species, a 2025 meta-analysis of 7 studies/1,099 bacteremia patients showing no detected cefepime–carbapenem difference, and a small 72-patient randomized piperacillin-tazobactam versus meropenem trial with discordant component outcomes. No standardized routine AmpC diagnostic method and heterogeneous species pooling weaken certainty. This section is highly usable operationally, but several recommendations are supported by triangulation rather than definitive trials.

Q2.2Mechanism-informed drug selectionJudge both AmpC induction and stability to hydrolysis. Cefepime is a weak inducer and relatively stable; carbapenems are stable; ceftriaxone-class agents and piperacillin-tazobactam remain vulnerable despite weak induction.

Section 3

CRE

Evidence maturity: Moderate overall; highly variable by mechanism

CRE treatment is the clearest example of mechanism-directed precision in the document. The guidance separates urinary from invasive disease, then separates non-carbapenemase CRE from KPC-, NDM-, and OXA-48-like-producing organisms. KPC invasive infection is treated with ceftazidime-avibactam, imipenem-relebactam, or meropenem-vaborbactam (with a slight rationale-level preference for meropenem-vaborbactam); NDM shifts therapy to aztreonam-avibactam or cefiderocol; OXA-48-like infection favors ceftazidime-avibactam. Routine definitive combination therapy is discouraged once an active β-lactam is known.

The conceptual architecture is excellent, but evidence maturity varies sharply. KPC therapy has substantial observational comparative evidence and >95% in-vitro activity for the principal agents, but no randomized head-to-head trial; treatment-emergent resistance is reported around 10% with ceftazidime-avibactam versus <3% with meropenem-vaborbactam or imipenem-relebactam in cited datasets. NDM recommendations have compelling mechanistic/PK-PD rationale but extremely small direct clinical subgroups, and cefiderocol findings are internally variable. OXA-48 evidence is largely observational plus animal/in-vitro support. The section should be trusted as a decision architecture more than as a uniform certainty grade.

Section 4

DTR P. aeruginosa

Evidence maturity: Moderate; strong operational logic, limited head-to-head data

The resistant P. aeruginosa section distinguishes MDR, carbapenem resistance, and DTR rather than treating them as synonyms. Active traditional non-carbapenem β-lactams should be preserved and exposure-optimized when possible. For established DTR infection, the guidance depends on comprehensive AST to cefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, and imipenem-relebactam; non-urinary infection generally favors the latter three, while ceftolozane-tazobactam is specifically preferred for pneumonia. Carbapenemase identity can alter selection, and repeat AST is emphasized because resistance frequently emerges during therapy.

The evidence base is a mixture of randomized subgroups, observational comparisons, surveillance, PK/PD, and resistance-mechanism studies. The pneumonia preference for ceftolozane-tazobactam is supported by observational success signals (63% versus 51% for ceftazidime-avibactam in one study) plus pulmonary exposure, not a randomized head-to-head trial. Treatment-emergent non-susceptibility is around 20–24% in several newer β-lactam datasets. Three randomized inhaled-antibiotic trials failed to show survival benefit, a useful example of the guidance allowing negative clinical evidence to override attractive pulmonary PK.

Q4.2DTR cUTICefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, and imipenem-relebactam are preferred; once-daily amikacin or tobramycin is an alternative.
Q4.3DTR non-urinary infectionCeftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, and imipenem-relebactam are preferred; ceftolozane-tazobactam is preferred for pneumonia. Cefiderocol is an alternative.
Q4.4Carbapenemase-directed therapyFor KPC-producing P. aeruginosa, select cefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, or imipenem-relebactam according to AST. For MBL producers (NDM/VIM/IMP), cefiderocol is preferred; ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is salvage only if cefiderocol cannot be used.
Q4.5Treatment-emergent resistanceExpect resistance emergence with all newer β-lactams; the guidance estimates roughly 20% overall in available datasets. Repeat AST during persistent infection, recurrence, or subsequent re-presentation after exposure.
Q4.6Combination therapyDo not routinely continue combination therapy when an active β-lactam is available; toxicity increases without established outcome benefit. Reserve combination strategies for no-active-β-lactam salvage scenarios.
Q4.7Nebulized antibioticsRoutine adjunctive nebulized therapy for DTR pneumonia is not suggested because randomized trials have not demonstrated survival benefit; selective salvage use may be considered when systemic options are exhausted.
Section 5

CRAB

Evidence maturity: Moderate overall; strongest anchor for sulbactam-durlobactam

The 2026 CRAB section is decisively sulbactam-centered. Sulbactam-durlobactam plus imipenem or meropenem is the preferred regimen. High-dose ampicillin-sulbactam is no longer presented as a coequal definitive option when sulbactam-durlobactam is available; it is a temporary combination bridge. Cefiderocol, minocycline, and polymyxin B are likewise alternatives used in combination, particularly when sulbactam-durlobactam is resistant or unavailable. Routine nebulized therapy is discouraged.

The preferred drug has the section’s strongest direct evidence: in a randomized 125-patient CRAB pneumonia/bloodstream trial, 28-day survival was 81% with sulbactam-durlobactam plus imipenem versus 68% with colistin plus imipenem, and clinical cure was 62% versus 40%. However, the comparator is not a modern preferred regimen, and the incremental clinical value of adding the carbapenem to sulbactam-durlobactam has not been directly proven. Cefiderocol illustrates a major evidence conflict—>90% in-vitro susceptibility and favorable observational summaries versus concerning/inconclusive randomized results. AST reliability, colonization misclassification, polymyxin toxicity, and two source-integrity inconsistencies further constrain certainty.

Q5.2High-dose ampicillin-sulbactamUse high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam providing 9 g/day sulbactam plus another active agent only as a bridge while awaiting sulbactam-durlobactam, assuming it is not immediately available.

Section 6

S. maltophilia

Evidence maturity: Low–moderate clinical maturity; preclinical/PK-PD heavy

The S. maltophilia section appropriately begins with the hardest clinical problem: deciding whether an isolate represents invasive infection rather than colonization. Cefiderocol monotherapy is preferred; aztreonam-avibactam, levofloxacin, minocycline, and TMP-SMX are alternatives generally initiated in combination because each has important PK/PD, resistance, or clinical-evidence limitations. Ceftazidime is rejected on mechanistic grounds and because contemporary breakpoints are absent. Oral availability of levofloxacin, minocycline, or TMP-SMX does not itself justify step-down; improvement and susceptibility conditions still matter.

This is the least clinically mature section. Cefiderocol’s preferred status rests mainly on near-universal in-vitro activity and reproducible animal-model killing: a rabbit pneumonia model reported 88% survival with cefiderocol versus 25% with TMP-SMX, but this is not human comparative efficacy. Human cefiderocol cohorts are uncontrolled and small trial subgroups are single-digit. Aztreonam-avibactam has strong mechanism but no S. maltophilia-specific breakpoint or validated killing target. Levofloxacin has about 20% reported resistance emergence; TMP-SMX remains >90% susceptible but is primarily bacteriostatic in experimental PK/PD. Combination therapy is therefore a risk-management strategy under uncertainty, not a proven superiority claim.

Question-level decision index (6 questions)
QuestionFocusCompressed guidance position
Q6.1CefiderocolCefiderocol monotherapy is the preferred treatment for invasive S. maltophilia infection, based primarily on near-universal in-vitro activity, PK/PD, and reproducible animal-model efficacy rather than proven human comparative superiority.
Q6.2Aztreonam-avibactamAlternative therapy, preferably initially combined with a second active agent. Ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is a reasonable substitute when aztreonam-avibactam is unavailable.
Q6.3LevofloxacinAlternative as a component of combination therapy; consider monotherapy step-down only after clear, sustained improvement and susceptibility confirmation. Treatment-emergent resistance and weak bactericidal target attainment are major constraints.
Q6.4MinocyclineAlternative as a component of combination therapy; it is the preferred tetracycline derivative, with good oral bioavailability but limited clinical comparative evidence and concern for bloodstream exposure.
Q6.5TMP-SMXAlternative as a component of combination therapy; despite >90% susceptibility, PK/PD studies suggest mainly bacteriostatic activity and comparative clinical evidence is weak.
Q6.6CeftazidimeNot suggested because intrinsic L1/L2 β-lactamases undermine activity and contemporary S. maltophilia susceptibility breakpoints are absent.

Q4.2DTR cUTICefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, and imipenem-relebactam are preferred; once-daily amikacin or tobramycin is an alternative.
Q4.3DTR non-urinary infectionCeftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, and imipenem-relebactam are preferred; ceftolozane-tazobactam is preferred for pneumonia. Cefiderocol is an alternative.
Q4.4Carbapenemase-directed therapyFor KPC-producing P. aeruginosa, select cefiderocol, ceftazidime-avibactam, or imipenem-relebactam according to AST. For MBL producers (NDM/VIM/IMP), cefiderocol is preferred; ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is salvage only if cefiderocol cannot be used.
Q4.5Treatment-emergent resistanceExpect resistance emergence with all newer β-lactams; the guidance estimates roughly 20% overall in available datasets. Repeat AST during persistent infection, recurrence, or subsequent re-presentation after exposure.
Q4.6Combination therapyDo not routinely continue combination therapy when an active β-lactam is available; toxicity increases without established outcome benefit. Reserve combination strategies for no-active-β-lactam salvage scenarios.
Q4.7Nebulized antibioticsRoutine adjunctive nebulized therapy for DTR pneumonia is not suggested because randomized trials have not demonstrated survival benefit; selective salvage use may be considered when systemic options are exhausted.
CRAB
Q5.1Sulbactam-durlobactamPreferred regimen: sulbactam-durlobactam plus imipenem or meropenem. Sulbactam-durlobactam has the section’s strongest direct comparative evidence; the added carbapenem is supported mainly by mechanistic/in-vitro rationale.
Q5.2High-dose ampicillin-sulbactamUse high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam providing 9 g/day sulbactam plus another active agent only as a bridge while awaiting sulbactam-durlobactam, assuming it is not immediately available.
Q5.3CefiderocolAlternative only as combination therapy, principally for sulbactam-durlobactam resistance or temporary lack of access; very high in-vitro activity contrasts with mixed/concerning randomized clinical signals.
Q5.4MinocyclineAlternative combination agent, mainly for sulbactam-durlobactam resistance or bridge therapy. Minocycline is preferred over tigecycline when a tetracycline is chosen because it has a CRAB breakpoint, but <50% of isolates are susceptible at the revised cutoff and bactericidal target attainment is limited.
Q5.5Polymyxin BAlternative combination agent with major limitations from nephrotoxicity, variable exposure, and poor pulmonary pharmacology; should not be treated as a routine coequal option.
Q5.6Nebulized antibioticsNot routinely suggested for CRAB pneumonia. Selective adjunctive use may be reasonable with sulbactam-durlobactam resistance or suboptimal systemic response, but clinical outcome benefit is unproven.
S. maltophilia
Q6.1CefiderocolCefiderocol monotherapy is the preferred treatment for invasive S. maltophilia infection, based primarily on near-universal in-vitro activity, PK/PD, and reproducible animal-model efficacy rather than proven human comparative superiority.
Q6.2Aztreonam-avibactamAlternative therapy, preferably initially combined with a second active agent. Ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is a reasonable substitute when aztreonam-avibactam is unavailable.
Q6.3LevofloxacinAlternative as a component of combination therapy; consider monotherapy step-down only after clear, sustained improvement and susceptibility confirmation. Treatment-emergent resistance and weak bactericidal target attainment are major constraints.
Q6.4MinocyclineAlternative as a component of combination therapy; it is the preferred tetracycline derivative, with good oral bioavailability but limited clinical comparative evidence and concern for bloodstream exposure.
Q6.5TMP-SMXAlternative as a component of combination therapy; despite >90% susceptibility, PK/PD studies suggest mainly bacteriostatic activity and comparative clinical evidence is weak.
Q6.6CeftazidimeNot suggested because intrinsic L1/L2 β-lactamases undermine activity and contemporary S. maltophilia susceptibility breakpoints are absent.

Selected load-bearing quantitative anchors

  • ESBL-E invasive disease: randomized bloodstream trial: 30-day survival 88% (164/187) with piperacillin-tazobactam versus 96% (184/191) with meropenem; broth-microdilution reanalysis narrowed statistical certainty but preserved direction.
  • AmpC: ceftriaxone resistance emergence is summarized at roughly 20% in the principal moderate-risk species; cefepime meta-analysis included 7 studies and 1,099 patients with no detected mortality/cure/relapse difference versus carbapenems.
  • KPC CRE: cited in-vitro activity exceeds 95% for ceftazidime-avibactam, imipenem-relebactam, and meropenem-vaborbactam; reported treatment-emergent resistance is about 10% for ceftazidime-avibactam versus <3% for the other two in cited datasets.
  • NDM CRE: aztreonam-avibactam trial subgroups are extremely small (e.g., 6 and 9 treated patients), underscoring the gap between mechanistic confidence and direct clinical precision.
  • DTR P. aeruginosa pneumonia: one observational comparison reported clinical success 63% (110/175) with ceftolozane-tazobactam versus 51% (89/175) with ceftazidime-avibactam; this is not randomized head-to-head evidence.
  • CRAB: sulbactam-durlobactam + imipenem trial: 28-day survival 81% (51/63) versus 68% (42/62) with colistin + imipenem; clinical cure 62% versus 40%.
  • S. maltophilia: rabbit pneumonia model survival 88% (7/8) with cefiderocol versus 25% (2/8) with TMP-SMX; human comparative inference should not be drawn from this preclinical result.

Appraisal synthesis

Method versus evidence. The document deserves a lower method score than its clinical sophistication might initially suggest. Its strength is expert integration of heterogeneous evidence, not systematic-review methodology. Conversely, the evidence score should not be reduced to the weakest section: several high-impact recommendations have randomized clinical anchors. The correct whole-guideline judgment is therefore “moderate evidence strength with wide within-document variance.”

Recommendation proportionality. The panel is often appropriately cautious, particularly when rejecting routine combination or nebulized therapy and when acknowledging limited direct data for newer agents. The most important proportionality concerns are where “preferred” status is driven primarily by preclinical or observational evidence: cefiderocol for invasive S. maltophilia, parts of carbapenemase-directed DTR P. aeruginosa treatment, and some CRE agent rankings. These recommendations may still be clinically reasonable because alternatives are poor; the appraisal issue is certainty, not necessarily direction.

Bottom line for practice: use now — with conditions.

Use the guidance as the default high-level decision framework for covered US AMR gram-negative infections, then apply local AST, formulary/access, renal/hepatic adjustment, source control, and patient-specific toxicity. For unusual mechanisms, treatment failure, sulbactam-durlobactam resistance, dual NDM resistance, or invasive S. maltophilia, the underlying evidence should be treated as lower certainty and the primary studies or specialist consultation deserve greater weight.

Pharmacotherapy / implementation

Practice translation

AST workflow

Do not stop at routine category labels. Mechanism testing and repeat AST are essential in CRE/DTR pathways; CRAB and S. maltophilia AST have additional reliability limitations.

Infusion strategy

Extended/prolonged infusion is a recurring efficacy strategy for cefepime, carbapenems, ceftazidime-avibactam, cefiderocol, ceftolozane-tazobactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, and high-dose sulbactam regimens.

Renal function

Table 1 is not a renal-adjustment table. It assumes normal renal/hepatic function except for selected augmented-clearance instructions; clinical use requires independent adjustment.

Oral transition

Use only when susceptibility, hemodynamic stability, site exposure, and GI absorption are adequate. Do not infer oral step-down simply because minocycline or another agent has an oral formulation.

Combination therapy

Default differs by organism: generally de-escalate CRE/DTR P. aeruginosa to one active β-lactam; use combination architecture for invasive CRAB; treat S. maltophilia combination therapy as uncertainty management rather than proven superiority.

Toxicity limits

Polymyxin nephrotoxicity, aminoglycoside duration-dependent nephrotoxicity, tetracycline GI toxicity/exposure limitations, TMP-SMX toxicity, and aztreonam-associated transaminase elevations influence hierarchy rather than appearing as afterthoughts.

What should change in practice

  • Build organism/mechanism/site-specific pathways rather than one generic “resistant gram-negative” algorithm.
  • For CRE, make carbapenemase identification an explicit therapeutic branch point whenever feasible.
  • For DTR P. aeruginosa, ensure the lab can test all relevant newer β-lactams and create a repeat-AST workflow after exposure or recurrence.
  • For CRAB, distinguish preferred sulbactam-durlobactam therapy from high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam bridging and from cefiderocol/minocycline/polymyxin salvage.
  • For CRAB and S. maltophilia, require an infection-versus-colonization checkpoint before targeted therapy.
  • Do not routinely add nebulized antibiotics to active systemic therapy for DTR P. aeruginosa or CRAB pneumonia.
  • Do not maintain “double coverage” after a reliable active β-lactam is known for CRE or DTR P. aeruginosa solely to prevent resistance; the guidance finds no established benefit.
Validity boundaries

Limitations and unresolved issues

This SEA evaluates the guidance document, not every primary study it cites. Results attributed to clinical trials, observational cohorts, surveillance, PK/PD, or animal models were reconstructed from the guideline and Pass artifacts; the load-bearing primary publications were not independently reappraised study by study.
  • No independent Supplemental Material review. Multiple dosing entries explicitly refer to supplemental information; therefore this artifact is not a complete dosing manual.
  • No external currency verification. The source is current through 1 March 2026; later updates or post-cutoff evidence were not searched because this synthesis was source-bounded.
  • Accepted manuscript state. The evaluated PDF is an accepted manuscript and may contain copyediting or production issues not present in the final version of record.
  • CRAB source-integrity flag 1 (page 55). The manuscript reports a sulbactam MIC threshold as “≤4 µg/L” while adjacent values and breakpoint conventions use µg/mL. This was preserved as an unresolved source inconsistency rather than silently corrected.
  • CRAB source-integrity flag 2 (page 56). The manuscript describes a cefiderocol randomized subgroup as 54 patients while displayed arm denominators are 39 + 17 = 56. This denominator inconsistency should be checked against the primary citation before reuse.
  • CRE citation [313] wording flag. The NDM paragraph reports outcomes for “ceftazidime-avibactam” in a context discussing ceftazidime-avibactam + aztreonam; the regimen wording was not silently normalized.
  • Evidence heterogeneity. A single guideline-level evidence score hides important variation: ESBL-E and the preferred CRAB regimen have stronger direct clinical anchors than S. maltophilia or parts of NDM/OXA-48/DTR therapy.
  • Implementation dependence. Recommendations assume access to AST, mechanism testing, newer agents, prolonged infusion, and monitoring that may not exist at every site.

Confidence in this appraisal: moderate. Main-text coverage is high—Pass 0 plus six deep section passes reconciled all 41 questions and both main-text tables—but confidence is limited by the lack of independent primary-study verification and the absent supplement.
