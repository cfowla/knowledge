One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Clinically significant pediatric DDI coadministration was uncommon in absolute encounter terms but frequent enough to represent a measurable medication-safety burden: 47,414 hospitalizations (2.03%) had at least one studied DDI exposure, corresponding to 34.91 DDI-exposure days per 1000 hospital days. Exposure varied materially by patient complexity, length of stay, service line, DDI pair, and hospital.

The study’s main contribution is not proof that DDIs caused harm. It is a large, multicenter map of prescribing/exposure patterns after restricting surveillance to 53 pediatric clinically significant pairs rather than all vendor-flagged interactions. Risk grade C accounted for the largest encounter prevalence (1.14% of all hospitalizations), while grade X combinations still appeared in 11,734 encounters (0.50%). The strongest adjusted patient-level gradients were age, number of complex chronic conditions, length of stay, and surgical service-line assignment.

Hospital variation was substantial. Unadjusted hospital proportions ranged from 0.65% to 7.33%, and adjusted proportions from 1.05% to 4.92%. Pair-level variation was also heterogeneous: nicardipine/tacrolimus occurred at every hospital and ranged from 0.01% to 3.6% of hospitalizations, whereas carbamazepine/cyclosporine occurred at only one of 52 hospitals. These differences identify plausible targets for local medication-safety review, but they do not establish preventability because PHIS lacks the detailed clinical context needed to judge appropriateness.

Most important interpretive boundary. The endpoint is same-day coadministration, not an observed adverse drug event. No rate or severity of DDI-caused ADEs was measured. The findings therefore support surveillance, prioritization, and hypothesis generation; they should not be read as evidence that every flagged exposure was harmful or avoidable.

Section-by-section condensation

Background / rationale

The paper frames inpatient pediatric medication safety as a setting in which polypharmacy is common but clinically meaningful DDIs are poorly characterized. Prior pediatric estimates often relied on broad adult-oriented interaction databases, whose severity classifications may not align with pediatric clinical relevance. The authors therefore use a 53-pair pediatric expert-consensus list designed to prioritize interactions with meaningful safety implications.

The two stated objectives were to estimate prevalence and patient-level risk factors for clinically significant DDI exposure and to quantify hospital-to-hospital variation in prescribing.

Methods

The cohort included inpatient and observation discharges of patients younger than 26 years from 52 PHIS tertiary children’s hospitals. Normal newborns, NICU patients, and peri/postpartum obstetrical encounters were excluded. DDI exposure required at least one hospital day on which both medications in a specified pair were administered; topical, ophthalmic, and otic routes were excluded. Class/mechanism pairings were expanded to relevant drugs as defined by the source.

DDIs were stratified by Lexicomp risk grade and effect category. The investigators calculated encounter proportions and patient-day exposure rates, fitted adjusted hospital-level models with hospital as a random effect, and used multivariable logistic regression for patient risk factors. Adjustment covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, payer, CCC count, LOS, service line, region, ICU status, and case mix index.

Results

Among 2,331,305 hospitalizations, 47,414 (2.03%) had a studied DDI exposure. Exactly 1, 2, and ≥3 DDI pairs occurred in 42,735 (1.83%), 3,661 (0.16%), and 1,018 (0.04%) hospitalizations, respectively. Grade X, D, and C exposures occurred in 0.50%, 0.55%, and 1.14% of all hospitalizations. The overall exposure rate was 34.91 per 1000 hospital days.

Age, CCC burden, LOS, and surgical service line had the clearest positive adjusted associations. The most common pairing by reported exposure count was nicardipine/tacrolimus; by patient-day rate in Table 3 it was also dominant at 11.84 per 1000 hospital days. Hospital-level and pair-level variation remained substantial after adjustment.

Discussion

The authors interpret the lower prevalence relative to prior broad potential-DDI studies as a consequence of focusing on a curated clinically significant pediatric list. They argue that risk-grade prioritization and pair-specific variation may help distinguish high-value alert targets from low-value vendor alerts. They also suggest that some hospital variation likely reflects unavoidable clinical needs and specialization, while some may represent preventable prescribing.

Proposed implementation directions include pediatric-specific alert prioritization, focusing interventions on patients/providers with frequent exposures, improving communication across care locations, incorporating patient-specific clinical information into alerts, and using pharmacist involvement for high-risk patients. These are implementation hypotheses rather than interventions tested by this study.

Limitations

The study is retrospective and relies on administrative/pharmacy data with limited patient-level clinical context. It is restricted to tertiary children’s hospitals and excludes newborn/NICU and peri/postpartum populations. Only 53 predefined DDI pairs were studied, so clinically significant interactions outside that list were not counted.

Most importantly, the study did not measure whether exposure caused an ADE or how severe any ADE was. The same-day exposure definition also does not establish pharmacokinetic overlap, causal attribution, or prescribing inappropriateness. Residual confounding from hospital specialization and case mix may contribute to observed variation.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that approximately 2% of patients treated at US children’s hospitals were prescribed clinically significant DDI combinations, with higher odds among older patients, those with greater chronic complexity, longer stays, and surgical service-line diagnoses. Wide hospital variation is presented as an opportunity for medication-safety improvement, with further research needed to connect exposures to actual adverse events and preventable causes.

Verdict: Read soon. Strong multicenter descriptive evidence for where pediatric inpatient DDI exposures occur and how they vary; insufficient evidence to infer actual ADE burden, preventability, or causal safety benefit from any proposed intervention.

Validity assessment

  • Selection: broad PHIS hospital cohort but restricted to tertiary children’s hospitals and selected populations.
  • Exposure ascertainment: objective daily medication-use data, but same-day coadministration is an imperfect proxy for clinically meaningful interaction exposure.
  • Outcome: prescribing/exposure prevalence is well aligned with the dataset; ADE causation is explicitly outside the study’s measured outcomes.
  • Confounding: extensive adjustment and hospital random effects improve comparisons, but case specialization and clinical indication remain plausible residual confounders.
  • Precision: sample size is very large and adjusted confidence intervals are narrow; statistical significance should not be mistaken for clinical importance.
  • Multiplicity/heterogeneity: many pair-level and hospital-level comparisons are descriptive; the paper is best used for pattern identification rather than ranking hospitals as intrinsically safer/less safe.

The highest-yield signals include nicardipine/tacrolimus, fentanyl/linezolid, transplant/nephrology settings, complex chronic disease, prolonged LOS, and surgical services.
Do not use for: estimating the probability that an individual patient will experience an ADE from a specific pair, declaring a particular coadministration inappropriate without clinical review, or claiming that reducing alert counts will improve patient outcomes.

Medication-safety implication: the paper supports a tiered review strategy: first validate whether the locally common/high-risk pairs remain clinically meaningful in the current formulary and workflow; then measure alert burden, override patterns, clinical context, and actual harm before implementing restrictive rules. Patient-specific decision support and pharmacist review are plausible directions, but their effectiveness is not established by this cohort.
