One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Prompt recognition, interruption of infusion, assessment, preservation or removal of the catheter according to antidote needs, medication-specific antidote selection, compress/supportive measures, and defined escalation are intended to limit tissue injury after non-chemotherapy extravasation.

The policy is a bedside operational standard rather than a research report. It covers adult and pediatric extravasation, distinguishes infiltration from extravasation, identifies high-risk irritant/vesicant therapies, and gives stepwise management. Its most reusable elements are the immediate-response sequence, antidote preparation instructions for hyaluronidase and phentolamine, topical nitroglycerin dosing, consultation/reporting triggers, and a 41-medication treatment table.

The main evidence limitation is traceability: the policy lists six references but does not map individual drug recommendations to specific evidence, grade certainty, or describe a reproducible literature-review method. Two named attachments are not present in the retrieved Drive folder. The policy is therefore strong as a local implementation artifact but should not be interpreted as primary evidence or a universal standard outside its governed setting.

Section condensation

Purpose, policy statement, and prevention

The source frames extravasation as a time-sensitive event capable of causing major soft-tissue injury and prolonged follow-up. Prevention emphasizes central or PICC administration for selected irritant/vesicant drugs when possible and appropriate, with pharmacy consultation to assess peripheral access suitability.

The prevention list includes acyclovir, concentrated electrolytes, vasoactive agents, and several other venous irritants. The wording is conditional rather than absolute, which matters because the document also addresses extravasations arising from peripheral therapy.

Definitions and resources

Infiltration is defined as nonvesicant leakage, whereas extravasation is vesicant leakage. Vesicants are described as capable of blistering, sloughing, or necrosis. Hyaluronidase is framed as a dispersion/absorption aid for selected extravasations when used promptly; phentolamine is framed as a local vasodilator for selected ischemic extravasations.

The equipment lists are operationally specific, including syringes, needle gauges, diluent, antisepsis materials, and dressings, with smaller-gauge/syringe specifications for infants.

Immediate response and monitoring

The workflow begins with stopping infusion and attempting catheter aspiration. Clinicians assess and mark the affected area, document the medication name/volume/concentration, perform repeated local assessments every 2 hours until signs and symptoms resolve, and complete a neurovascular examination of the affected limb.

The site should be elevated if possible and pressure avoided. Provider notification is required to determine treatment; pharmacist consultation is explicitly recommended. The catheter is retained when needed for antidote delivery, except for the ICN/SCN immediate-removal instruction.

Antidotes and supportive care

Hyaluronidase is administered as five circumferential subcutaneous injections using a 150 units/mL solution, usually 0.2 mL each (0.1 mL in ICN/SCN), and is described as most effective within 1-2 hours. Phentolamine instructions vary by patient group but use a 0.5 mg/mL dilution and a 12-hour treatment window; topical nitroglycerin 2% is applied as a 1-inch strip and may be repeated every 8 hours.

Warm and cold compress schedules are specified, with ICN/SCN instructed not to receive compresses. Contrast-media extravasation follows unit-specific guidance. WOC consultation and reportable-condition triggers are stratified by patient group and wound progression.

Medication-specific recommendations

The treatment table provides 41 medication rows. Warm compress plus hyaluronidase is the dominant pattern; vasopressor-type agents such as dobutamine, dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, phenylephrine, and terlipressin use phentolamine with topical nitroglycerin 2% as an adjunct/alternative. Valproate is the only listed cold-compress row.

Several rows include atypical pairings that should be preserved exactly as local policy content rather than silently normalized: fosphenytoin lists topical nitroglycerin 2% as an adjunct to hyaluronidase; methylene blue lists topical nitroglycerin 2% as treatment with phentolamine as adjunct; vasopressin has the same ordering. The source spelling “Gentamycin” is preserved.

Workflow / algorithm — Extravasation response

Trigger: suspected IV/intraarterial extravasation
1. Stop infusion.
2. Attempt aspiration from the IV catheter hub.
3. Assess and document: affected area/outline, drug + volume + concentration,
   local signs; repeat local site assessment every 2 hours until resolved.
4. Perform neurovascular assessment; notify provider for any alteration.
5. Elevate if possible; do not apply pressure.
6. Notify provider; consult pharmacist for antidote/treatment selection.
   If the drug is not in the table, contact pharmacy.
7. Retain or remove catheter according to antidote needs and unit exception.
8. Administer medication-specific antidote/treatment and supportive care.
9. Apply WOC/contrast/reportable-condition escalation rules as applicable.

Failure modes / governance: delayed recognition, missing medication-specific guidance, failure to account for ICN/SCN exceptions, inaccessible attachments, and use of a printed copy after electronic supersession.

Antidote preparation and administration

Hyaluronidase

150 units/mL. Remove IV access from the site; not for IV administration. Prepare five 0.2 mL injections (0.1 mL in ICN/SCN) and inject subcutaneously around the circumference with a new 25-gauge needle for each injection. Most effective within 1-2 hours. Apply dry gauze.

Phentolamine — ICN/SCN

Maximum 0.1 mg/kg or 2.5 mg total. Dilute 5 mg in 10 mL 0.9% sodium chloride (0.5 mg/mL). After withdrawing residual drug, administer 0.1-0.2 mL subcutaneous divided doses around the site within 12 hours.

Phentolamine — infants/children/adolescents

Maximum 0.1-0.2 mg/kg or 5 mg total. Dilute 5 mg in 10 mL 0.9% sodium chloride. Use phentolamine to clear the catheter, remove it, then give 0.2 mL subcutaneous divided doses around the site within 12 hours.

Phentolamine — adults

Dilute 5 mg in 10 mL 0.9% sodium chloride. Withdraw residual drug, use a small amount to clear the catheter, remove it, and administer 0.2 mL subcutaneous divided doses around the site within 12 hours.

Topical nitroglycerin

Nitroglycerin 2%, 1-inch strip to the ischemic site; may repeat every 8 hours if necessary.

External currency verification

Verified point: the 2023 Pharmacotherapy focused update cited by the policy is indexed in PubMed (PMID 36938775; DOI 10.1002/phar.2794). Its abstract describes a protocolized multidisciplinary approach, phentolamine as the historical preferred treatment for vasopressor extravasation, and hyaluronidase as commonly used for hyperosmolar and pH-related injuries. It also notes emerging evidence supporting selected peripheral vasopressor/hypertonic-sodium administration, so the DUHS prevention language should be read as a local “whenever possible and appropriate” preference rather than a universal ban on peripheral administration.

FDA norepinephrine labeling available from the 2023 label states that phentolamine infiltration should be used promptly and describes conspicuous local hyperemia when the affected area is infiltrated within 12 hours, which is directionally consistent with the policy's 12-hour phentolamine window.

Verification boundary: this limited currency check does not independently validate every medication-specific table row, dose, compress choice, pediatric exception, or attachment-dependent instruction.

External sources: PubMed 36938775; FDA norepinephrine labeling (2023 PDF).

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for DUHS operational use. The policy is highly actionable and locally authoritative within its effective version, but it is not a primary-evidence document and should not be exported as a universal extravasation standard without row-level evidence verification.

Relevance 9/10

Rationale: Directly supports bedside and pharmacy handling of non-chemotherapy extravasation in the named DUHS settings.

Evidence basis: The policy is explicitly designed for adult and pediatric extravasation care and contains drug-specific treatment instructions.

Limiting factor: Relevance drops outside institutions that do not use DUHS policy, formulary, or escalation pathways.

Would raise/lower: Confirmation that the reader practices under the current DUHS policy version would raise confidence; supersession would lower it.

Novelty 4/10

Rationale: The value is operational synthesis rather than novel evidence generation.

Evidence basis: The document consolidates definitions, response steps, antidote preparation, and a 41-drug treatment table.

Limiting factor: Most clinical content is derivative of reviews, procedural references, and established antidote practice.

Would raise/lower: A transparent update method or institution-specific outcomes demonstrating added value would raise this score.

Method strength 3/10

Rationale: The policy is approved and version-controlled, but it does not report a formal literature-search method, evidence grading, or recommendation-development process.

Evidence basis: Version, approval body, review history, and references are documented.

Limiting factor: No search dates, selection criteria, evidence tables, conflict management, or grading framework are provided.

Would raise/lower: A reproducible evidence-review method with recommendation-to-source mapping would materially raise the score.

Evidence strength 5/10

Rationale: The policy cites relevant reviews and guidance, including a 2023 focused Pharmacotherapy review, but individual recommendations are not linked to evidence strength.

Evidence basis: The bibliography includes contemporary noncytotoxic-extravasation reviews and a management guideline.

Limiting factor: No recommendation grades, denominators, comparative data, or row-level evidence citations are provided.

Would raise/lower: Primary-study linkage and explicit evidence certainty for each antidote/compress recommendation would raise the score.

External validity 5/10

Rationale: Core response principles are broadly recognizable, but the document is an institutional policy with setting-specific exceptions and workflow assumptions.

Evidence basis: The policy covers adults and pediatrics and includes common vesicants/irritants.

Limiting factor: DUHS applicability, ICN/SCN exceptions, and local consult/escalation pathways may not transfer directly.

Would raise/lower: Cross-institution validation or alignment with a current national standard would raise the score.

Implementation value 9/10

Rationale: The document is highly actionable because it specifies immediate steps, monitoring, antidote preparation, compress timing, consult triggers, and medication-specific management.

Evidence basis: The response workflow and 41-row treatment table can be translated into bedside or CDS workflows.

Limiting factor: The named attachments were unavailable in the retrieved folder, and some instructions depend on local personnel/unit context.

Would raise/lower: Access to the missing attachments and machine-readable local order/workflow mappings would raise the score.

Main limitations: no reported systematic development method or evidence grading; no recommendation-to-reference mapping; setting-specific workflow; named attachments unavailable in the retrieved folder; source effective date is 2024 and electronic supersession status must be checked before live use.

Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high for describing the retrieved policy and its internal structure; moderate for practice currency because only selected external points were verified rather than every drug-specific recommendation.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Best use

Do not use for

Trigger → medication → compress eligibility → antidote → adjunct/alternative → time window → catheter rule → monitoring/escalation.
Critical exceptions
ICN/SCN no-compress rule, ICN/SCN immediate catheter removal, age-group phentolamine dosing, and WOC/reportable-condition stratification.
Before deployment
