One-page synthesis

The policy gives a bedside workflow for suspected or confirmed extravasation of non-chemotherapy intravenous or intraarterial medications in adults and children at DUH and DRH. The first actions are concrete: stop the infusion, attempt aspiration through the catheter, assess and mark the affected area, document the drug and exposure details, check neurovascular status, elevate when possible, avoid pressure, and involve the provider and pharmacist. Site findings are reassessed every 2 hours until signs and symptoms resolve.

The medication table is the main operational reference. Most listed agents receive a warm compress plus hyaluronidase. Vasoactive agents such as dobutamine, dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, phenylephrine, and terlipressin use phentolamine with topical nitroglycerin 2% as an adjunct or alternative. Valproate is the only listed medication assigned a cold compress. Methylene blue and vasopressin reverse the usual antidote order: topical nitroglycerin 2% appears in the antidote/treatment column and phentolamine appears as the adjunct or alternative.

The document is strong as a local operations tool because it gives doses, dilutions, time windows, consult triggers, and a drug-by-drug table. Its evidence transparency is much weaker. It cites six sources but reports no search method, evidence grading, recommendation strength, or source-to-recommendation mapping. Two named attachments are listed but are not included in this packet.

Verdict: Read first for DUHS bedside implementation. Use it as a local workflow reference, not as a stand-alone evidence review or a universal extravasation guideline.

Section condensation

Purpose, policy statement, and prevention

The policy covers pediatric and adult patients at risk for or with medication or infusion extravasation. It treats extravasation as time-sensitive because tissue injury may begin immediately and may progress to sloughing, necrosis, grafting, or prolonged follow-up.

Prevention centers on vascular access selection. Certain irritants and vesicants should use a CVAD or PICC when possible and appropriate, and staff should ask pharmacy whether a medication is suitable for peripheral venous access.

Definitions and treatment concepts

Infiltration is leakage of a nonvesicant solution. Extravasation is leakage of a vesicant solution. The document defines vesicants by their potential to injure tissue, including blistering, sloughing, and necrosis.

Hyaluronidase is described as a subcutaneous treatment around the site, most useful early. The policy states that it increases fluid absorption threefold to fivefold. Phentolamine is described as a local vasodilator for selected extravasations. The procedure section gives a 12-hour treatment window for phentolamine.

Assessment and escalation

After stopping the infusion and attempting aspiration, staff document affected-area size, drug name, volume, concentration, local tissue findings, and neurovascular status. Local findings are checked every 2 hours until resolution. Neurovascular changes trigger provider notification.

The site is elevated when possible and pressure is avoided. Provider notification determines whether an antidote is needed. The policy explicitly supports pharmacist consultation, especially when the drug is not listed in the treatment table.

Hyaluronidase, phentolamine, and nitroglycerin

Hyaluronidase is used at 150 units/mL. The procedure prepares five injections of 0.2 mL each and infiltrates the affected circumference with a new 25-gauge needle for each injection. ICN/SCN uses 0.1 mL injections. The document says treatment is most effective within 1 to 2 hours.

Phentolamine is diluted as 5 mg in 10 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride, which yields 0.5 mg/mL. ICN/SCN caps the dose at 0.1 mg/kg or 2.5 mg total. Infants, children, and adolescents use a maximum of 0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg or 5 mg total. Adult instructions do not print a separate maximum dose. All listed groups use a 12-hour administration window. Topical nitroglycerin 2% is applied as a 1-inch strip to ischemic tissue and may be repeated every 8 hours when needed.

Consults, reportable conditions, and compresses

ICN/SCN calls WOC for any extravasation. Pediatric patients trigger WOC consultation for non-blanchable erythema and edema. Adult consultation is directed to wounds caused by medications that can produce skin necrosis. Reportable findings include known vesicant or corrosive extravasation, worsening redness or ulceration, increasing drainage, and the stated temperature thresholds.

Warm dry compresses are applied for 15 to 20 minutes four times daily for 24 to 48 hours. Cold compresses are applied for at least 15 to 30 minutes four times daily for 48 to 72 hours. ICN/SCN should not receive a compress.

Clinical response workflow

1. Stop the infusion.
2. Attempt aspiration through the IV catheter hub.
3. Assess and mark the site. Record drug, volume, concentration, tissue findings, and neurovascular status.
4. Reassess local findings every 2 hours until signs and symptoms resolve.
5. Elevate when possible. Do not apply pressure.
6. Notify the provider. Use pharmacist consultation to select antidote or treatment.
7. If the drug is not in the table, contact pharmacy.
8. Preserve IV access if it is still needed for antidote administration. ICN/SCN removes the catheter immediately.
9. Give the indicated antidote and compress regimen.
10. Use WOC consultation and reportable-condition triggers by patient group and clinical findings.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance9/10

Directly relevant to inpatient medication extravasation response and pharmacy consultation.

Evidence basis
The policy defines the response sequence, antidotes, doses, time windows, compresses, and escalation triggers.
Limiting factor
Its stated applicability is limited to DUH and DRH.
What would change the score
Broader applicability evidence or a multisite standard would raise this score outside DUHS.
Novelty3/10

The document mainly operationalizes known extravasation management rather than presenting new evidence.

Evidence basis
It is a local policy with a drug-by-drug implementation table and patient-group instructions.
Limiting factor
No new comparative data, mechanism study, or formal evidence synthesis is reported.
What would change the score
New primary evidence or a documented update that changes standard management would raise the score.
Method strength3/10

The policy is committee-approved but does not report how evidence was searched, selected, graded, or linked to recommendations.

Evidence basis
Revision history, approving committee, and six references are supplied.
Limiting factor
No reproducible development method or formal evidence grading appears in the document.
What would change the score
A documented search strategy, evidence tables, recommendation grading, and conflict process would raise the score.
Evidence strength4/10

The recommendations are clinically specific, but the document does not show the strength or certainty of evidence behind each one.

Evidence basis
Six cited sources include reviews, procedural material, and textbooks.
Limiting factor
No recommendation-to-source mapping, effect estimates, certainty ratings, or comparative outcome data are provided.
What would change the score
Direct evidence summaries with certainty grading and source-linked recommendations would raise the score.
External validity4/10

The workflow is built for two DUHS hospitals and contains unit-specific neonatal and pediatric instructions.

Evidence basis
Applicability boxes select DUH and DRH, and several instructions are specific to ICN/SCN.
Limiting factor
Local staffing, access, formulary, and consult pathways may not transfer to other institutions.
What would change the score
Cross-institutional validation or alignment with a broader guideline would raise the score.
Implementation value9/10

The document is highly usable at the point of care within its intended system.

Evidence basis
It gives immediate actions, monitoring intervals, dose caps, dilutions, treatment windows, consult triggers, and a 41-row medication table.
Limiting factor
Two listed attachments are missing from the supplied packet, and the source has a few apparent typographic errors.
What would change the score
Embedding the missing attachments and correcting source table typography in a controlled revision would raise the score.

Validity and safety boundary

This is a practice policy, not a trial or systematic review. The SEA therefore appraises operational clarity and evidentiary transparency rather than internal validity of an empirical study. The source itself instructs users to rely on electronic versions over printed copies. Current-practice verification outside the supplied packet was not performed.

Source integrity notes

  • The table prints "Gentamycin" rather than gentamicin.
  • The propofol row prints "Hyauronidase" rather than hyaluronidase.
  • The phentolamine definition on page 2 says treatment occurs "within 12 of the extravasation" without a unit, while the procedural instructions on pages 3 and 4 explicitly use 12 hours.
  • Doxycycline and mannitol rows have blank adjunctive or alternative cells. They were not converted to N/A.
