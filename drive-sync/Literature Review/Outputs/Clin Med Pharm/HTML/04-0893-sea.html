One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. This is a short historical case that documents a severe TSS phenotype linked to a TSST-1-gene-positive MRSA wound isolate. It is useful as existence evidence and as a reminder that resistant S. aureus can cause toxin-mediated disease. It is not strong evidence for incidence, comparative treatment efficacy, or a current empiric regimen.

The patient was a 54-year-old postoperative woman with shock, gastrointestinal symptoms, later rash and desquamation, thrombocytopenia, renal dysfunction, and elevated creatine kinase. Blood, stool, and urine cultures were negative. Pus from the operative wound grew MRSA carrying the TSST-1 gene. Molecular typing identified the G10 PFGE clone, SCCmec II, and ST5-SCCmec II lineage. The authors treated with fluid resuscitation, dopamine, and anti-MRSA therapy with clindamycin. The patient recovered from renal and laboratory abnormalities and developed the expected later desquamation.

The paper's strongest claim is narrow: this clinical syndrome occurred in a patient with a locally cultured, molecularly characterized MRSA isolate carrying the TSST-1 gene. Broader claims are much weaker. A single case cannot show that MRSA TSS risk was rising in Europe, and the favorable outcome cannot identify which treatment component produced recovery. The report also has a published correction to one laboratory value, and the letter describes PCR detection of the TSST-1 gene but does not describe a direct toxin-production assay.

Source condensation

Context and case history

The authors frame MRSA-associated TSS as established in Japan, rare in the United States, and not yet reported in Europe. They then report a 54-year-old woman in Brussels with 2 days of myalgia, diarrhea, and vomiting after recent palate surgery and treatment of a local scar infection with amoxicillin-clavulanic acid.

Examination and laboratory findings

The patient was tachypneic, pale, sweating, febrile at 38.2 degrees C, and hypotensive at 70/50 mm Hg. The operative wound was red and swollen. Reported laboratory findings included leukocytes 19,830/mm3 with 97% neutrophils, platelets 90,000/mm3, creatinine 2.1 mg/dL, bicarbonate 13 mEq/L, and creatine kinase 514 U/L. The raw PDF also prints "cyclic AMP receptor protein 43.7 ng/mL." A later published correction changes that item to C-reactive protein 43.7 mg/dL.

Microbiology and molecular typing

Blood, stool, and urine cultures were negative. Wound puncture released 12 mL of pus, and the pus culture grew MRSA. Multiplex PCR detected the TSST-1 gene. The isolate was typed as PFGE clone G10 carrying SCCmec II and assigned to the ST5-SCCmec II lineage, formerly called the New-York/Japan clone.

The authors cite prior surveillance showing the same epidemic clone in 12% of Belgian hospitals in 2001. That number is secondary evidence from reference 6, not a result generated by this case report.

Treatment and clinical course

The case received aggressive IV fluids, dopamine, clindamycin, and an anti-MRSA glycopeptide. The first treatment paragraph prints the drug as "teicoplamin," while the final paragraph prints "teicoplanin." The report says the outcome was favorable. A diffuse macular rash appeared on day 2, renal failure and other laboratory abnormalities resolved, and extensive peeling of both hands appeared by day 5.

Authors' interpretation

The authors state that the patient met TSS criteria and that bacteriology confirmed staphylococcal TSS. They call the case the first European TSS case due to TSST-1-producing MRSA and argue that clinicians should consider MRSA when choosing initial antimicrobial treatment for TSS, especially in a nosocomial setting.

The letter also summarizes historical treatment recommendations from cited sources. Those statements are part of the authors' 2005 context, not current guidance and not primary results from this case.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance7/10

The case is directly relevant to infectious diseases, critical care, and antimicrobial selection in toxic shock syndrome.

Evidence basis. It documents a clinically recognizable TSS syndrome linked to a TSST-1-gene-positive MRSA wound isolate.

Main limit. It is a single historical case from 2005.

Would raise the score. A larger contemporary case series or surveillance study would raise this score.

Novelty7/10

At publication, the authors presented the case as the first European TSS case due to TSST-1-producing MRSA.

Evidence basis. The report combines a clinically severe syndrome with molecular typing of an epidemic MRSA clone.

Main limit. The first-in-Europe claim is a historical priority claim and is not independently proven by the case itself.

Would raise the score. Systematic contemporaneous surveillance would strengthen the novelty claim.

Method strength3/10

The report has specific clinical, culture, PCR, and molecular-typing details, but it is an uncontrolled single case.

Evidence basis. The wound isolate was cultured and typed as G10, SCCmec II, ST5-SCCmec II, with the TSST-1 gene detected by PCR.

Main limit. No comparator, prespecified protocol, alternative-cause analysis, or direct toxin-production assay is described.

Would raise the score. Replicated cases with standardized diagnostic testing and direct toxin assessment would raise this score.

Evidence strength3/10

The letter supports the existence of this case more strongly than it supports broad claims about rising MRSA TSS risk or treatment policy.

Evidence basis. The syndrome, wound isolate, clinical course, and molecular findings are internally coherent.

Main limit. n=1 and treatment response cannot establish incidence, causality, or comparative treatment efficacy.

Would raise the score. Larger observational data or controlled treatment evidence would raise this score.

External validity2/10

Generalization is narrow because the evidence comes from one postoperative 54-year-old woman in Brussels.

Evidence basis. The report identifies a hospital-associated clinical context and a known epidemic MRSA clone.

Main limit. No broader patient sample, geographic sampling, or denominator is available.

Would raise the score. Multicenter case surveillance with denominators would raise this score.

Implementation value4/10

The practical signal is to consider MRSA in nosocomial TSS, but the specific antimicrobial discussion is historical.

Evidence basis. The case shows why empiric coverage assumptions can fail when MRSA causes toxin-mediated disease.

Main limit. A 2005 case letter is not a sufficient basis for current empiric therapy, dosing, or stewardship policy.

Would raise the score. Current guideline, susceptibility, and local epidemiology alignment would raise this score.

Main strengths

  • The syndrome is described with concrete clinical and laboratory findings.
  • The local wound isolate was cultured and molecularly characterized.
  • The report states the treatment course and temporal evolution of rash, renal recovery, and desquamation.

Main limitations

  • One patient, no comparator, no denominator, and no ability to estimate frequency or treatment effect.
  • The etiologic link is plausible but indirect. Blood cultures were negative and the report does not describe a direct TSST-1 production assay.
  • The published laboratory-value correction and the teicoplamin/teicoplanin spelling inconsistency reduce reporting reliability.
  • The treatment discussion reflects 2005 practice and cannot establish a current empiric regimen.

Clinical practice translation

Best use. Historical case evidence that TSST-1-gene-positive MRSA can be associated with a classic postoperative TSS syndrome and that resistant S. aureus belongs in the differential when the context suggests healthcare-associated infection.
Do not use for. Current antimicrobial selection, dosing, duration, toxin-suppression strategy, or estimates of MRSA TSS prevalence. Those decisions need current guidance, susceptibility data, local epidemiology, and patient-specific review.
