One-page synthesis

Core thesis: MBD-Check is an interpretable machine-learning decision support tool intended to help decide which adults with suspected mild bleeding disorders need preoperative haematology referral. In external validation, it achieved high sensitivity with only moderate specificity.

The study addresses a real preoperative workflow gap: routine coagulation tests are poor screening tests for mild bleeding disorders, while full ISTH-BAT-style assessment and specialized testing are cumbersome outside haemostasis clinics. The authors trained 17 supervised models on a Bern cohort, selected a radial-kernel support vector machine, and deployed the final model as an R-Shiny web application with per-prediction Shapley explanations. The selected inputs were feasible pre-anaesthesia variables: aPTT ratio, PFA-EPI, sex, family history, and a simplified bleeding-history structure derived from ISTH-BAT domains.

The central result is clinically useful but bounded: in the Lucerne external validation cohort (n=217), MBD-Check had AUROC 0.85 (95% CI 0.80-0.90), sensitivity 90.2% (83.1-94.9), specificity 54.3% (44.3-64.0), PPV 0.68, and NPV 0.84. That profile suits a referral-support screen in a pre-selected suspected-MBD population. It does not establish that the tool safely rules out bleeding risk in the general surgical population, reduces perioperative bleeding, reduces resource use, or improves patient outcomes.

Best use
Read as a strong clinical-AI example of problem-driven model development, external validation, interpretability, and usability testing in a narrow diagnostic workflow.
Do not use for
Standalone surgical clearance, replacement of haematology consultation, unselected preoperative screening, pediatric patients, diverse-population extrapolation, or treatment decisions.
Main implementation value
A structured referral triage concept: suspected bleeding tendency -> MBD-Check -> probability threshold of 47% -> haematology referral versus proceed with clinical judgment.
Primary caution
Specificity is low and external validation is geographically narrow; local validation and monitoring would be required before practice adoption.

Section condensation

Summary / abstract

The article presents MBD-Check, an explainable ML decision-support tool for preoperative prediction of mild bleeding disorders. The problem is clinically relevant because mild bleeding disorders are common inherited bleeding conditions and can present unexpectedly during surgery, while routine preoperative coagulation tests and lengthy bleeding assessment tools have practical limitations.

In external validation (n=217), the final model identified 90.2% of patients with mild bleeding disorders and correctly classified 54.3% of patients without mild bleeding disorders; AUROC was 0.85. Usability testing in 86 clinicians found median completion time of 72 seconds and SUS 82.5, interpreted by the authors as excellent usability.

Introduction / rationale

The rationale is strong: anaesthesiologists and surgeons often rely on tests such as aPTT, prothrombin time/INR, and clinical history, but these are limited as screening tools. Haemostasis specialists use the ISTH-BAT, but it is complex for routine pre-anaesthesia workflows. The authors frame ML as useful only if it addresses workflow need, external validation, transparency, and practical implementation.

The article explicitly positions early stakeholder involvement as a way to reduce common clinical-AI failure modes: unclear clinical need, bias, poor external validation, opacity, and non-integration into workflow. This framing improves credibility because the model is not presented as generic AI; it is targeted to a specific referral decision.

Methods - study design and participants

The model was developed from a prospective cross-sectional cohort of consecutive adults referred for suspected mild bleeding disorder at Inselspital, Bern University Hospital from Jan 1, 2012, to March 31, 2017. Eligibility required age at least 18 years and referral for suspected bleeding disorder. All patients underwent diagnostic investigations, and final diagnoses were established by an expert panel. The study reports TRIPOD+AI alignment.

External validation used a second prospective cross-sectional cohort at Cantonal Hospital Lucerne, enrolling patients with suspected mild bleeding disorder from Jan 1, 2011, to Sept 30, 2013. The inclusion criteria and diagnostic investigations were described as consistent with the development cohort. The target population is therefore selected and referral-enriched, not the general surgical population.

Methods - model development

The 555-patient Bern cohort was randomly split into a training dataset (n=445) and an internal validation dataset (n=110). Feature engineering simplified ISTH-BAT response patterns into clinically interpretable bleeding-history levels. Candidate variables were first constrained by a focus group of anaesthesiologists, surgeons, haematologists, laboratory specialists, and researchers to variables feasible during pre-anaesthesia evaluation.

Feature selection used Boruta on the training dataset only. Missing values were imputed with a bagged-tree imputer fitted on training predictors without the outcome; preprocessing also used training-only centering/scaling and Yeo-Johnson transformations. Seventeen supervised ML algorithms were screened using five-times repeated ten-fold cross-validation with AUROC as the primary metric. The five best families were tuned; radial-kernel SVM had the highest internal validation AUROC and became the final model. The selected threshold was 0.47, with false negatives assigned 25% greater cost than false positives.

Methods - external validation, interpretability, and usability

External performance was summarized with AUROC, area under the precision-recall curve, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. Confidence intervals used Clopper-Pearson for classification metrics, DeLong for AUROC, and bootstrapping for precision-recall curves. Sensitivity analyses assessed calendar year, exclusion of patients with anticoagulant treatment or systemic diseases, and alternative imputation methods.

Interpretability was addressed using permutation feature importance, partial dependence plots, centered individual conditional expectation plots, accumulated local effects plots, and Shapley values. The final R-Shiny web application included a Shapley-based explanation plot for individual predictions. Usability testing used four case vignettes and the System Usability Scale in clinicians involved in preoperative screening and evaluation.

Results - cohort and feature selection

The training cohort included 555 patients: 371 female (67%), 184 male (33%), median age 43.7 years. Referral reasons were bleeding tendency in 453 (82%), abnormal coagulation tests in 35 (6%), family history in 42 (8%), reassessment in 8 (1%), and unclear in 17 (3%). Mild bleeding disorders were diagnosed in 288 (52%), most commonly platelet function disorders (n=118, 41% of MBD diagnoses) and von Willebrand factor disorders (n=50, 17%).

After focus-group candidate selection, Boruta identified several bleeding-history features plus PFA-EPI, PFA-ADP, aPTT, and family history. PFA-ADP was excluded because of correlation with PFA-EPI; easy bruising and oral cavity bleeding were excluded because of redundancy or recall concerns. Sex was added because menstrual and postpartum bleeding predictors were included.

Results - diagnostic performance

In internal validation, MBD-Check achieved AUROC 0.77 (0.68-0.85), AUPRC 0.80 (0.68-0.88), sensitivity 0.75 (0.62-0.86), specificity 0.66 (0.51-0.78), PPV 0.70 (0.57-0.83), and NPV 0.71 (0.57-0.83). External validation performance was higher: AUROC 0.85 (0.80-0.90), AUPRC 0.89 (0.83-0.93), sensitivity 0.90 (0.83-0.95), specificity 0.54 (0.44-0.64), PPV 0.68 (0.60-0.75), and NPV 0.84 (0.73-0.92).

Compared with other evaluated tools in the external validation cohort, MBD-Check had the highest AUROC. The article reports AUROC 0.49 for prothrombin time/INR, 0.63 for aPTT, 0.73 for PFA-EPI, and 0.79 for ISTH-BAT. Sensitivity analyses were broadly similar, with AUROC values reported as ranging from 0.76 to 1.00 across analyzed conditions.

Results - interpretability and usability

Permutation feature importance identified simplified ISTH-BAT surgery as the most important predictor (importance ratio 1.32; 95% CI 1.30-1.44), followed by PFA-EPI (1.21; 1.19-1.32) and simplified ISTH-BAT epistaxis (1.18; 1.16-1.22). The model is therefore not a black-box-only result; the leading signals are clinically plausible bleeding-history and platelet-function variables.

Usability was tested in 86 respondents: 33 surgeons, 29 anaesthesiologists, and 24 haematologists. Five additional responses were excluded for task times exceeding 5 minutes. Work experience ranged broadly. Median tool completion and interpretation time was 72.0 seconds (IQR 49.0-79.5), and median SUS was 82.5 (IQR 72.5-90.0).

Discussion / limitations / conclusion

The authors argue that MBD-Check can consolidate streamlined bleeding history and relevant laboratory values into a usable preoperative decision aid. They propose using the tool only when a bleeding tendency is suspected: probability below 47% suggests that further evaluation might not be required depending on clinical judgment, while probability at or above 47% supports haematology referral.

The limitations are material. Both cohorts are old; the sample size is modest for ML; the population is referral-enriched rather than unselected surgical patients; the external validation cohort is from the same country and a similar tertiary setting; ethnicity data were not collected; specificity is limited; and mild factor VII deficiency was classified differently than other rare factor deficiencies. The study also validates diagnosis of mild bleeding disorder, not perioperative bleeding outcomes or clinical impact after deployment.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first, with clinical-action caution. The article is worth full reading for diagnostic ML, perioperative referral workflow design, and interpretable clinical decision support. It should not be used to justify unsupervised practice adoption or broad preoperative screening.

Limiting factor: narrower direct relevance to pharmacy practice unless involved in pre-op pathways or AI governance.

Strengths

  • Clinically coherent problem selection: targeted to a defined preoperative referral decision rather than a generic AI use case.
  • Prospective data collection in both development and external validation cohorts.
  • External validation with uncertainty estimates and comparator tools.
  • Leakage-aware preprocessing described in main text, including training-only imputation and transformations.
  • Interpretability implemented at both global and individual-prediction levels.
  • Usability evaluation in target clinicians, not only model-performance reporting.

Limitations and red flags

  • Population boundary: tool is for patients with suspected mild bleeding disorder, not unselected preoperative patients.
  • Specificity: external specificity 54.3% means false-positive referral burden is expected.
  • Outcome gap: the model predicts diagnosis, not perioperative bleeding, transfusion, surgical delay, patient safety, or cost outcomes.
  • Temporal drift: development and validation cohorts are from 2011-2017; diagnostic practice and classifications have evolved.
  • Geographic diversity: both cohorts are Swiss tertiary-care settings; ethnicity data were not available.
  • Reference-standard nuance: expert-panel diagnosis can be affected by available clinical features; the authors note that external diagnoses relied more on clinical aspects, which could favor MBD-Check.
  • Reproducibility boundary: the uploaded file references appendix/code resources, but those were not independently inspected in this appraisal.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate-high. The uploaded main article provides enough detail to appraise the clinical question, cohorts, main methods, headline performance, figures, tables, limitations, and implementation stance. Confidence is not maximal because the supplementary appendix, code, web tool behavior, calibration details, and local deployment context were not independently evaluated.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Practice bottom line: MBD-Check can inform a structured preoperative referral workflow for adults with suspected mild bleeding disorder, but the evidence is insufficient to use it as a standalone clearance or rule-out tool outside the studied population.
Reasonable next step
Use as a paper/workflow model for designing a local validation study or pilot: define suspected-MBD criteria, collect required variables, run silent-mode predictions, compare against local haematology outcomes, and monitor false negatives.
Do not change immediately
Do not reduce haematology referral access, cancel diagnostic workups, or clear high-concern surgical patients solely on MBD-Check probability. Do not apply to patients without suspected MBD.
Operational constraints
Requires availability and standardization of PFA-EPI, consistent aPTT normalization, structured bleeding history capture, and agreement on referral threshold and override rules.
Safety monitoring
Track perioperative bleeding among low-probability patients, haematology referral volume, false-negative diagnoses, false-positive workups, calibration drift, missing data patterns, and clinician override reasons.
Clinical exclusions / caution zones
Not validated for pediatric patients, broad pre-op screening, diverse international populations, acquired coagulopathy, active anticoagulant-management questions, or direct prediction of surgical bleeding risk.
Dosing / renal / hepatic
Not applicable: this is a diagnostic decision-support article, not a pharmacotherapy intervention. Medication-induced bleeding and organ dysfunction require separate clinical evaluation.

What would justify clinical implementation

  • Prospective contemporary validation in the local target population, including calibration and decision-curve/net-benefit analysis.
  • Pre-specified definition of suspected mild bleeding disorder and clear rules for when the model is not eligible.
  • Integration into the pre-anaesthesia workflow with auditable data capture and documented clinician override.
  • Monitoring for false negatives and perioperative bleeding, not only AUROC.
  • Assessment of referral burden, time to surgery, patient outcomes, and haematology workload.

What not to overclaim

  • Do not claim the model predicts perioperative haemorrhage; it predicts mild bleeding disorder diagnosis.
  • Do not claim broad generalizability; validation is limited to a similar Swiss referral setting.
  • Do not claim low-risk patients can always proceed; the authors preserve clinical judgment in the workflow.
  • Do not infer causal importance from feature-importance rankings.
  • Do not treat web-app usability as evidence of clinical benefit.
