One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In adults presenting to the emergency department with early septic shock after at least 1 L of IV fluid, a restricted-fluid strategy with earlier vasopressors produced clear process separation but did not improve days alive and out of the hospital at 90 days compared with a greater-fluid strategy with later vasopressors.

The trial randomized 1000 patients; 963 were included in the intention-to-treat population after 37 patients did not provide consent for use of data. Baseline shock severity was clinically meaningful: median age about 68 to 69 years, median APACHE II score 18, median lactate about 3.2 to 3.3 mmol/L, median systolic blood pressure 85 to 86 mm Hg, and median prerandomization fluid volume 1500 mL. Respiratory and urinary tract sources were the most common suspected infection sites.

The intervention achieved the intended separation. Over 24 hours, the vasopressor group received a median 1140 mL IV fluid versus 2248 mL in the fluids group, a median difference of -1108 mL. Vasopressor infusion was used in 86.5% versus 67.6%, and median time to vasopressor initiation was 0.4 versus 1.4 hours. Despite this, the primary outcome was identical: median days alive and out of the hospital at day 90 were 76 in both groups, median difference 0.0 days (95% CI, -2.7 to 2.7; P=1.00).

Explicit practice warning: Evidence is insufficient to make routine early vasopressor plus restricted fluids the default resuscitation strategy for all ED septic shock patients. The trial supports feasibility and likely acceptability in selected monitored patients, but not superiority for patient-centered outcomes or mortality.

Practice bottom line

Practice stance: No routine protocol-wide switch based on this trial alone. A restricted-fluid/early-vasopressor pathway is reasonable as an available option for selected, closely monitored ED septic shock patients after initial fluid resuscitation, but it should not be represented as outcome-improving.
QuestionAnswer
Should this change default sepsis resuscitation?No, not as a universal default. The primary patient-centered outcome was neutral. The trial did not show that earlier vasopressors and less fluid improved days alive out of hospital or mortality.
Should this support early vasopressors in selected patients?Yes, conditionally. The strategy was feasible and did not show an excess of prespecified complications in the main report. It may be useful when persistent hypotension follows an initial bolus and further fluid is unlikely to help or could plausibly harm.
Should this reduce reflexive additional boluses after 1 to 2 L?Yes, with reassessment. The trial supports moving from fixed repeated boluses toward perfusion-guided reassessment, especially once initial fluid has been given and antimicrobials have started.
Should this be used to claim mortality benefit?No. Mortality estimates crossed no effect and allowed both benefit and harm. Any mortality-based NNT/NNH would be misleading.
Should this be used to claim pulmonary-edema reduction?Cautiously. Pulmonary edema was reported less often with the vasopressor strategy, but it was a safety outcome in an unblinded trial and could be affected by ascertainment/reporting bias.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames a genuine resuscitation uncertainty: larger fluid volumes may restore perfusion but can cause harm from excess fluid, whereas early vasopressors may support pressure with less fluid. Adults with ED septic shock were randomized to restricted fluids plus early vasopressors versus greater fluids plus later vasopressors for 6 to 24 hours.

Among 963 intention-to-treat patients, the early-vasopressor strategy reduced 24-hour IV fluid by a median 1108 mL and increased vasopressor exposure by 18.9 percentage points. The primary outcome was neutral: median days alive and out of hospital at 90 days were 76 in both groups. Pulmonary edema was reported less often with the vasopressor strategy: 0.6% versus 5.0%.

Introduction / background

The introduction places the trial against guideline uncertainty. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign and ESICM guidance have supported early crystalloid resuscitation, including the 30 mL/kg concept, but evidence quality for initial volume is limited and specific timing of vasopressor initiation has historically been less settled.

Prior restrictive-fluid trials and meta-analyses did not establish a mortality benefit for lower fluid volumes. Smaller single-center early vasopressor trials suggested possible benefit, but estimates remained imprecise. ARISE FLUIDS was designed to test whether earlier vasopressors with less fluid improves a patient-centered 90-day outcome.

Methods

This was an investigator-initiated, open-label, multicenter RCT at 51 sites. Eligible adults had suspected infection, persistent hypotension despite at least 1000 mL bolus fluid, lactate >2 mmol/L, and IV antimicrobials started. Key exclusions included more than 2000 mL IV fluid before screening, more than 6 hours since ED presentation, care limitations, immediate surgery, and clinician-determined unsuitability.

Randomization used a secure website, 1:1 allocation, permuted blocks, and site stratification. The primary endpoint was days alive and out of the hospital through day 90; patients who died before day 90 were assigned 0 days. The main analysis used quantile mixed-effects regression with site as a random effect. The trial was powered to detect a 7-day absolute difference in the primary outcome, not small mortality differences.

Results

From 2080 eligible patients, 1000 were randomized; 963 remained in the intention-to-treat population after 37 patients did not consent to use of all data. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar. Median prerandomization fluid volume was 1500 mL in both groups, and vasopressors had already been started in 11.9% versus 8.5%.

Intervention separation was substantial: 0-6 hour fluid volume 500 versus 1500 mL and 0-24 hour fluid volume 1140 versus 2248 mL for vasopressor versus fluids groups, respectively. Primary outcome and major secondary outcomes appeared similar. Reported pulmonary edema was lower with restricted fluids/early vasopressors, while invasive ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and mortality did not show clear differences.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusion

The authors conclude that restricted fluids plus early vasopressors did not increase days alive and out of hospital at day 90 compared with greater fluids and later vasopressors. They interpret time-to-event process differences, such as earlier ICU discharge and vasopressor cessation, as descriptive clinical-management outcomes rather than direct evidence of patient benefit.

Important limitations include open-label treatment, safety-event reporting bias risk, intervention limited to 24 hours, no control over later hospitalization, exclusion of patients with treatment limitations and patients with preexisting fluid-restriction conditions such as cardiac failure, exclusion after more than 2000 mL fluid or more than 6 hours in the ED, and uncertain generalizability to low-resource settings.

FIGURE 1 - Trial Algorithm
Purpose: Define eligibility and the two hemodynamic strategies.
Inputs: Suspected sepsis; SBP <90 mm Hg or MAP <65 mm Hg despite >=1000 mL IV fluid bolus; lactate >2.0 mmol/L; first antimicrobial dose being received.
Fluids group: Up to 1000 mL IV fluid within 1 hour after randomization if MAP remained below target or hypoperfusion persisted; additional 500-mL boluses; vasopressors after fluid level restored or nonresponse; maintenance fluids per usual practice.
Vasopressor group: IV resuscitation fluids ceased; vasopressor therapy commenced immediately; 250-mL fluid boluses allowed only when clinically indicated; maintenance fluids discouraged.
Shared process: Reassess at least hourly for 6 hours, then as indicated; monitor for fluid overload, hypovolemia, or ischemia; continue protocol up to 24 hours while in a critical care area or until ward transfer.
Critical exception: In either group with critical hypotension, fluids and vasopressors could be started concurrently.
Interpretation: The algorithm tests strategy, not a specific drug, target MAP, pressor dose, line type, or fluid responsiveness method.

Guideline context checked for practice alignment

Current sepsis guidance remains compatible with individualized reassessment rather than reflexive repeated fluids. The 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign suggests at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid in the first 3 hours for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock with low certainty; recommends crystalloids as first-line fluid; suggests balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline; suggests initial bolus resuscitation followed by vasopressors if hypotension persists; suggests starting vasopressors peripherally rather than delaying until central access; and recommends norepinephrine as first-line vasopressor for septic shock.

Insufficient evidence for clinical action: This paper does not justify ignoring local sepsis-bundle requirements, removing initial fluid resuscitation, delaying antibiotics/source control, replacing dynamic bedside reassessment with a fixed pressor-first rule, or applying restricted fluids broadly to excluded high-risk groups.

Not a dosing trial: ARISE FLUIDS does not establish a vasopressor dose, concentration, access site, line size, maximum peripheral duration, fluid type, dynamic fluid-responsiveness method, or MAP target. Those must come from guidelines, drug references, institutional policy, and bedside assessment.

Vasopressor choiceTrial allowed clinician preference. Reported vasopressors included norepinephrine, epinephrine, vasopressin, metaraminol, dopamine, and phenylephrine. For practice, norepinephrine remains the usual first-line agent unless patient-specific factors justify another agent.
MonitoringMAP/SBP, heart rate/rhythm, mental status, skin perfusion/capillary refill, urine output, lactate trend, oxygenation, work of breathing, fluid balance, pulmonary edema, peripheral line site, extravasation, limb/digital ischemia, and need for central access or ICU escalation.
Contraindications / cautionsCritical hypotension may require concurrent fluids and vasopressors rather than strict adherence to either arm. Caution with severe arrhythmia, uncontrolled ischemia, peripheral vascular disease, limb ischemia risk, uncontrolled extravasation risk, and inability to monitor. Trial exclusions should function as caution flags for extrapolation.
Drug interactionsNo formal interaction analysis. Adrenergic vasopressor response can be affected by agents that alter sympathetic tone or vascular responsiveness, but this trial does not guide interaction management. Apply drug-specific references and local critical-care policy.
Renal considerationsNo renal dose adjustment issue for crystalloid volume strategy or vasopressor selection was tested. AKI/RRT outcomes were not meaningfully different. Renal dysfunction should affect fluid tolerance, perfusion targets, urine-output interpretation, and antimicrobial/TDM decisions, not automatic adoption of the restricted strategy.
Hepatic considerationsNo hepatic subgroup or dosing conclusion. Hepatic dysfunction may affect lactate interpretation and hemodynamic tolerance, but ARISE FLUIDS does not provide a hepatic-specific practice rule.
Safety responseHave an extravasation protocol, vasopressor rescue/escalation criteria, pulmonary edema monitoring, and a clear threshold for central access and ICU transfer if pressor dose escalates or duration is prolonged.

Implementation constraints and operational takeaways

ED workflow
Order sets must separate initial bolus, reassessment, pressor initiation, and bolus escape criteria. A simple “fluid-restricted” checkbox is inadequate.
Nursing workload
Peripheral vasopressors require line checks, titration monitoring, extravasation readiness, and clear escalation paths.
Pharmacy role
Standardize peripheral norepinephrine concentrations, compatibility guidance, maximum local dose/duration, extravasation treatment, and pump library limits.
ICU/bed capacity
The vasopressor group had more ICU admission in the main report (76.5% vs 67.8%). A pressor-forward pathway can shift resource demand even without improving patient-centered outcomes.
Documentation
Record why additional fluids were limited, what perfusion markers were followed, and when pressors were chosen after initial resuscitation.
Quality metrics
Do not use this trial alone to override sepsis bundle reporting requirements. Align local documentation with current policy and regulatory expectations.

What should change in practice

  • Move away from automatic repeated boluses without reassessment after the initial resuscitation phase.
  • Maintain a safe early vasopressor option when persistent hypotension follows initial fluid and monitoring infrastructure exists.
  • Use dynamic or serial perfusion assessment where feasible rather than fixed fluid volume alone.
  • Track pulmonary edema and fluid balance as safety outcomes in sepsis quality review.

What should not change in practice

  • Do not frame early vasopressors/restricted fluids as mortality-improving based on ARISE FLUIDS.
  • Do not abandon antibiotics, source control, lactate/perfusion reassessment, or guideline-concordant initial resuscitation.
  • Do not extrapolate to unmonitored wards or settings without peripheral vasopressor governance.
  • Do not use subgroup findings to create special protocols; subgroup analyses did not show reliable treatment-effect heterogeneity.

Main strengths

  • Large, pragmatic, multicenter RCT with clinically meaningful early septic shock population.
  • Meaningful separation in both fluid volume and vasopressor timing.
  • Patient-centered primary outcome rather than only lactate clearance, MAP achievement, or process measures.
  • Explicit safety monitoring and main-text reporting of pulmonary edema and organ support outcomes.

Main limitations

  • Open-label treatment; pulmonary edema and some safety outcomes may be subject to reporting bias.
  • Not a noninferiority trial and not powered for small mortality differences.
  • Excluded patients with more than 2 L fluid before screening, delayed ED time, immediate surgery, treatment limitations, clinician unsuitability, and some preexisting fluid-restriction conditions.
  • Does not standardize pressor agent, dose, MAP target, route, peripheral-line characteristics, or fluid-responsiveness assessment.
  • Supplementary appendix was not included in the uploaded source, limiting deeper appraisal of safety definitions, detailed eligibility, and protocol deviations.
