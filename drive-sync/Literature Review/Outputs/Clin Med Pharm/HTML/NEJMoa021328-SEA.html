One-page synthesis

Core finding: In older/high-risk patients with atrial fibrillation, a rhythm-control strategy based mainly on antiarrhythmic drugs and cardioversion did not improve survival over rate control. Rhythm control produced more hospitalizations and more treatment-related adverse effects. Stroke rates were low and similar, but most ischemic strokes occurred after warfarin had been stopped or when anticoagulation was subtherapeutic.

AFFIRM randomized 4060 patients (mean age 69.7 years) to rate-control or rhythm-control strategies. The trial was pragmatic at the strategy level: physicians could choose among protocol-approved drugs, combinations, cardioversion, and selected nonpharmacologic options. Rate control targeted ≤80 beats/min at rest and ≤110 during a six-minute walk. Warfarin INR 2.0-3.0 was mandatory in rate control but could be stopped in rhythm control after sustained apparent sinus rhythm.

The primary all-cause mortality comparison numerically favored rate control but did not reach conventional statistical significance: 356 versus 310 deaths; five-year mortality 23.8% versus 21.3%; HR 1.15 (95% CI 0.99-1.34), P=0.08. Adjustment gave HR 1.18 (95% CI 0.99-1.41), P=0.07. Hospitalization was higher with rhythm control (80.1% vs 73.0%, P<0.001). Torsade, bradycardic arrest, pulmonary and gastrointestinal events, bradycardia, and QT prolongation were more frequent with rhythm-control treatment. The rhythm-control arm also had substantial crossover to rate control (37.5% at five years).

Methods

Population and design

Patients were eligible if they were at least 65 years old or had other stroke/death risk factors and if AF was judged likely to recur, clinically important, and suitable for long-term treatment. Anticoagulation could not be contraindicated, and participants had to be eligible for at least two drug trials in each strategy. The protocol was approved across 213 clinical sites and satellites; participants provided written informed consent.

Randomization was stratified by clinical site. The primary analysis was intention-to-treat time to death from any cause. Time-to-event outcomes used Kaplan-Meier estimates and log-rank tests, with adjustment for 10 interim analyses. Prespecified subgroup and multivariable Cox analyses used baseline covariates including age, sex, rhythm at randomization, AF episode type/duration, CAD, hypertension, CHF, and LVEF.

Treatment strategies

Rhythm control allowed physician-selected antiarrhythmic drugs (including amiodarone, sotalol, class I agents, and later dofetilide), combinations, and cardioversion. Rate control used beta-blockers, verapamil/diltiazem, digoxin, or combinations with explicit resting/exercise rate targets.

After failure of at least two pharmacologic trials, selected nonpharmacologic therapies could be used. Warfarin target INR was 2.0-3.0. Continuous anticoagulation was mandated in rate control; in rhythm control it was encouraged but could be discontinued after at least 4, preferably 12, weeks of apparently maintained sinus rhythm.

Results

Of 7401 eligible patients offered enrollment, 4060 enrolled. Mean age was 69.7 years; 39.3% were women; 70.8% had hypertension and 38.2% coronary artery disease. Echocardiograms were available in 3311 patients; 64.7% had left atrial enlargement and 26.0% depressed LV function.

Rhythm maintenance was imperfect: sinus rhythm in the rhythm-control arm fell from 82.4% at one year to 62.6% at five years; crossover to rate control reached 37.5% by five years. In the rate-control group, 34.6% were in sinus rhythm at five years and crossover to rhythm control reached 14.9%. Warfarin use remained >85% at rate-control assessments but about 70% in rhythm control after the first four months; only 62.3% of measured INRs were in range.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Landmark strategy trial with strong internal relevance to AF management and anticoagulation decisions, but its rhythm-control package is historically specific and cannot be treated as a direct test of contemporary early ablation-based rhythm control.
Relevance — 10/10
Rationale: directly addresses a foundational AF management question. Evidence basis: 4060-patient randomized strategy trial with mortality, hospitalization, stroke, and safety outcomes.
Principal limit: historical treatment context. Score would be lower for a question focused specifically on modern ablation; it could not meaningfully rise above 10.
Novelty — 9/10
Rationale: at publication, the trial challenged the presumed superiority of restoring sinus rhythm. Evidence basis: a large direct randomized comparison of competing AF management strategies.
Principal limit: novelty is historical. Score would be lower when judged only against today's evidence base; a similarly practice-changing result in a previously untested contemporary strategy would raise it.
Method strength — 8.5/10
Rationale: large multicenter randomized design with ITT analysis and a hard primary endpoint. Evidence basis: prespecified survival analysis, interim monitoring, subgroup plan, and multivariable adjustment.
Principal limit: substantial strategy crossover and heterogeneous drug choices. Score would rise with stronger treatment separation and fall with greater loss to follow-up or post hoc endpoint selection.
Evidence strength — 8.5/10
Rationale: mortality, hospitalization, stroke, and safety findings are directly supported by event counts and time-to-event analyses. Evidence basis: printed hazard ratios, confidence intervals, Kaplan-Meier estimates, and adverse-event tables.
Principal limit: the primary mortality difference did not meet conventional significance and received treatment diverged from assignment. Score would rise with clearer treatment separation and a definitive primary estimate; it would fall if findings depended mainly on post hoc analyses.
External validity — 7/10
Rationale: strong applicability to older/high-risk AF patients resembling the enrolled cohort. Evidence basis: broad multicenter enrollment with common hypertension, CAD, and structural heart disease.
Principal limit: younger/lone AF and highly symptomatic patients were underrepresented, and current rhythm-control options differ. Score would rise with broader age/risk representation and contemporary therapy; it would fall when extrapolated to populations unlike AFFIRM.
Implementation value — 8/10
Rationale: the trial remains highly useful for medication-safety, hospitalization, and anticoagulation lessons. Evidence basis: higher rhythm-control adverse events/hospitalization and strokes clustered after warfarin discontinuation or subtherapeutic INR.
Principal limit: current rhythm-control choices have evolved substantially. Score would rise for a current strategy trial with modern ablation/anticoagulation and fall if used as a stand-alone contemporary treatment algorithm.

Major strengths

  • Large randomized multicenter sample with all-cause mortality as the primary endpoint.
  • Strategy-level pragmatism reflects real prescribing choices of the period.
  • Detailed safety and hospitalization outcomes expose tradeoffs beyond rhythm status.
  • Warfarin/stroke data directly test a clinically consequential assumption about anticoagulation discontinuation.

Major limitations

  • Rhythm control was predominantly pharmacologic; only small numbers received ablation or other nonpharmacologic rhythm therapies.
  • Five-year crossover was 37.5% from rhythm to rate versus 14.9% from rate to rhythm, reducing treatment separation.
  • Patients with frequent/severe symptoms could have been selected away from rate control; younger low-risk/lone AF patients were not well represented.
  • Some rate-control patients were in sinus rhythm, and some rhythm-control patients had recurrent AF; assigned strategy and achieved rhythm are not equivalent.
  • The trial predates contemporary ablation technology, DOAC-based anticoagulation, and evidence favoring selected early rhythm-control strategies.

Practice translation and contemporary verification

Do not use AFFIRM as a blanket argument against rhythm control today. The source establishes that the specific antiarrhythmic-heavy rhythm-control strategy used in this older/high-risk population did not improve survival and caused more utilization/toxicity. It does not invalidate modern early rhythm control or catheter ablation in appropriately selected patients.

External verification (not source-derived): As checked on August 12, 2026, the ACC guideline hub still lists the 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS atrial fibrillation guideline as the current comprehensive U.S. AF guideline. That guideline emphasizes early rhythm control and gives catheter ablation a first-line Class 1 role in selected patients. This contemporary context explains why AFFIRM remains historically foundational but is not a stand-alone current-practice rule.

Current guideline source: Joglar JA, Chung MK, Armbruster AL, et al. 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Atrial Fibrillation. Circulation. 2024;149:e1-e156. doi:10.1161/CIR.0000000000001193. ACC Atrial Fibrillation Guideline Hub verified 2026-08-12.
