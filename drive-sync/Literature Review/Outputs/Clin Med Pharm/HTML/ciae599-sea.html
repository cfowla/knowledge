One-page synthesis

Core finding. Among 25,391 adults with suspected sepsis receiving both agents within 24 hours, 84.4% received β-lactams first. In the primary IPW analysis, β-lactam-first sequencing was associated with lower in-hospital mortality (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80–0.99; P=.046). The estimate weakened when time-to-β-lactam replaced time-to-first antibiotic (aOR 0.93, 95% CI 0.82–1.05) and with propensity-score matching (aOR 0.94, 95% CI 0.82–1.07).

The clinically important operational signal is the treatment delay attached to sequence. Median time to the first β-lactam was 133 minutes in the β-lactam-first group versus 339 minutes in the vancomycin-first group, a source-reported difference of 206 minutes (about 3.5 hours). The vancomycin-first group was also less severely ill on multiple measured dimensions and had a very different infection-source distribution, underscoring that sequence was not randomly assigned.

The study therefore provides plausible, highly actionable but moderate-certainty evidence: when both empiric agents are intended, allowing vancomycin to materially delay β-lactam delivery may be undesirable. The strongest causal claim remains uncertain because the effect was modest, the primary CI barely excluded 1.0, most subgroup estimates were nonsignificant, the shock interaction pattern was counterintuitive, and residual/time-varying confounding cannot be excluded.

Section condensation

Abstract / Introduction

The paper asks whether sequence matters when vancomycin and a broad-spectrum β-lactam are both prescribed for suspected sepsis. The rationale is operational: vancomycin often takes at least an hour to infuse and may delay β-lactam administration when IV access is constrained; prior single-system bloodstream-infection data suggested a much larger mortality association favoring β-lactam first.

The authors broadened the question to a multihospital suspected-sepsis population, where confirmed MRSA is uncommon and only a minority have positive blood cultures. Their stated aim was to estimate the association between β-lactam-first versus vancomycin-first strategies and mortality.

Methods

Adults ≥18 years admitted from the ED at five MGB hospitals from June 2015 through August 2022 were eligible if they had suspected infection within 24 hours plus acute organ dysfunction within 12 hours and received both vancomycin and a qualifying β-lactam within 24 hours. Exclusions included outside-hospital transfer, comfort measures/death within 6 hours, psychiatric/obstetric admission, pre-arrival antibiotics, incomplete key physiologic data, and simultaneous first-dose starts within 1 minute. The exposure was sequence; the primary outcome was in-hospital mortality.

The primary causal-inference approach was IPW using propensity scores, with covariate balance assessed by standardized mean differences and mortality modeled with a weighted logistic GLM and robust sandwich variance. Covariates covered demographics, hospital/encounter factors, comorbidity, granular severity markers, infection site, and time-to-first antibiotic. Analyses also substituted time-to-first β-lactam, evaluated clinically defined subgroups and tighter time windows, and used 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching as a sensitivity method.

Results

The cohort included 25,391 patients: 21,449 β-lactam first and 3,942 vancomycin first. Vancomycin-first patients were generally less severely ill and more likely to have skin/musculoskeletal and MRSA infections; β-lactam-first patients more often had septicemia, urinary, and intra-abdominal diagnoses. Initial antibiotic delivery was also later in the vancomycin-first group. IPW achieved measured covariate balance with all post-weighting SMDs below 10%.

Crude mortality was nearly identical (13.4% vs 13.6%), but the primary weighted estimate favored β-lactam first (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80–0.99). The estimate was not significant after substituting time-to-first β-lactam (0.93, 0.82–1.05) or under PSM (0.94, 0.82–1.07). Most subgroups were directionally favorable but nonsignificant; the no-shock subgroup showed a significant association whereas the shock subgroup did not.

Discussion / Conclusion

The authors interpret the attenuation after adjustment for time-to-β-lactam as consistent with earlier β-lactam delivery lying on the causal pathway from sequencing to mortality. They emphasize that gram-negative pathogens remain common, MRSA represented a small fraction of the cohort, and β-lactams cover many common gram-positive organisms. They also contrast their more modest 11% odds reduction with a prior report of a 52% reduction in short-term mortality.

The main limitations are a single healthcare system, imperfect bacterial specificity of the suspected-sepsis cohort, unmeasured concurrent sepsis treatments, uncertainty about why sequence-related delays occurred, and residual confounding. The authors conclude that β-lactam-first sequencing is associated with modestly lower mortality but requires stronger confirmation, ideally randomized or via more rigorous multi-system target-trial emulation.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. High relevance and unusually direct implementation value for ED/inpatient sepsis antibiotic workflow, but use it as practice-supporting observational evidence rather than a causal mandate.
Relevance
9/10

Basis: large ED suspected-sepsis cohort and clinically actionable exposure. Limit: not a dosing/selection trial. Higher with direct patient-centered randomized evidence.
Novelty
7/10

7/10

External validity
6/10

Limit: local MRSA prevalence, IV access workflow, order sets, and pharmacy/nursing processes may differ. Higher with diverse multi-system validation.
Implementation value
8/10
The intervention concept is simple: avoid sequencing that creates hours of delay in β-lactam delivery when both agents are intended.
Limit: the paper does not test an implementation protocol or measure balancing harms. Higher with prospective workflow implementation outcomes.

Strengths

  • Large cohort with granular clinical variables and direct medication administration timing.
  • Explicit balance diagnostics show IPW successfully balanced measured covariates.
  • Multiple subgroup, timing-window, mediator-oriented, and alternative propensity-method analyses.
  • Focus on the broader suspected-sepsis population makes the operational question more relevant than bloodstream-infection-only cohorts.

Principal limitations

  • Observational sequence assignment is plainly confounded by infection source, severity, calendar year, hospital characteristics, and timing.
  • The primary effect is small and only narrowly statistically significant; the PSM and time-to-β-lactam models do not exclude the null.
  • The single-system setting constrains transferability of both pathogen prevalence and medication-administration workflow.
  • The study cannot determine whether sequence itself, delayed effective therapy, provider intent, IV access, or other unmeasured care processes generated the association.
Independent appraisal point. Replacing time-to-first antibiotic with time-to-first β-lactam changes the estimand because time-to-β-lactam is plausibly a mediator of sequencing. Its attenuation is consistent with the authors’ mechanism, but it should not be interpreted as a clean falsification or confirmation of causality.

Practice translation

Best useSupport ED/pharmacy/nursing workflow design that gets the broad-spectrum β-lactam infused promptly when both vancomycin and β-lactam are ordered and sequence would otherwise create a long delay.
Do not use forClaiming an 11% causal mortality reduction for every patient, delaying clinically urgent MRSA-active therapy, or overriding local sepsis policy without contextual review.
Operational signalThe observed median β-lactam delay associated with vancomycin-first sequencing was ~3.5 hours relative to ED arrival—far larger than the difference in time to the first antibiotic alone.
What to monitor if implementedTime-to-β-lactam, time-to-vancomycin, total time-to-both agents, IV access limitations, infusion compatibility/availability, and balancing delays in MRSA-directed therapy.

Bottom line: For a clinical pharmacy workflow, this paper is most useful as evidence against a process that lets vancomycin monopolize limited IV access and substantially postpone β-lactam delivery. The data favor β-lactam-first prioritization in many empiric sepsis scenarios, but the evidence remains observational and is not sufficient by itself to define a universal rule.
