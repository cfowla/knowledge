Scope warning. This is a 2016 comparative efficacy synthesis of short (about 24–26 week) glycaemic trials. It did not compare safety and predates modern cardiovascular, heart-failure and kidney outcome evidence. It should not be used alone to determine current inpatient formulary equivalence or contemporary SGLT-2 treatment selection.

One-page synthesis

The review used Bayesian network meta-analysis to compare licensed SGLT-2 inhibitor doses in two settings: monotherapy for type 2 diabetes inadequately controlled with diet/exercise and add-on therapy for patients inadequately controlled with metformin. The synthesis focused on HbA1c target attainment, mean HbA1c change, weight and systolic blood pressure. Across the network, all studied agents generally outperformed placebo. In monotherapy, canagliflozin 300 mg produced the largest HbA1c reduction (1.23 percentage points versus placebo), while canagliflozin 100 mg produced the greatest weight loss. In dual therapy, canagliflozin 300 mg again ranked highest for HbA1c reduction (0.77 percentage points) and weight loss (2.5 kg versus placebo), but the authors judged many between-drug differences small and not clinically significant.

The load-bearing limitation is that treatment rankings come mainly from indirect comparisons in sparse networks, not head-to-head trials. Several studies did not report all outcomes; fixed-effect modeling was used; and sensitivity analyses materially altered some results, especially systolic blood pressure. The article itself contains an unresolved count discrepancy: it states that 13 trials were included while its drug-specific trial counts sum to 14. The review did not compare safety, and follow-up was short. The source is therefore useful for historical comparative glycaemic context and for understanding early SGLT-2 network-meta-analysis evidence, not as a standalone current practice document.

Verdict: Read soon. Best use: historical comparative efficacy and methodological context for SGLT-2 formulary reasoning. Do not use for current safety, cardiorenal efficacy, inpatient substitution, or guideline recommendations without newer evidence.

Methods

Search and eligibility

MEDLINE, MEDLINE In-Process and EMBASE were searched from January 2005 through September 2014 and updated in January 2015. Eligible evidence consisted of randomized controlled trials of licensed SGLT-2 doses lasting at least 24 weeks in adults with type 2 diabetes inadequately controlled either with diet/exercise or with metformin. Studies had to report HbA1c target attainment and/or mean HbA1c change; weight and systolic blood pressure were also sought. The supplement documents the Ovid MEDLINE Boolean search and its English-language, human and year filters.

Analysis

The Bayesian NMA was run in WinBUGS 1.4.3, preserved randomization, and accounted for multi-arm trial correlations. Convergence was assessed using trace plots and Brooks–Gelman–Rubin statistics with two chains; the first 20,000 samples were discarded and the reported estimates were based on a further 70,000. The authors selected a fixed-effect model using deviance information criterion and the small number of studies. Direct and indirect evidence were checked for inconsistency where possible. Sensitivity analyses added atypical Bolinder, Henry and Kashiwagi trials.

Results

Evidence base

The screening process began with 535 abstracts, removed 73 duplicates, screened 462 records, excluded 417 at title/abstract stage, and assessed 45 full texts. The narrative reports 13 included trials; eight contributed monotherapy comparisons and six contributed dual-therapy comparisons, with incomplete outcome availability across trials. Most trials were judged low risk of bias, with some unclear domains. All included trials were pharmaceutical-company funded.

Monotherapy

All SGLT-2 inhibitors significantly improved HbA1c target attainment, mean HbA1c and weight versus placebo. Canagliflozin 300 mg produced the largest HbA1c reduction (1.23 percentage points vs placebo). Relative to canagliflozin 300 mg, HbA1c reduction was smaller by 0.20 points with canagliflozin 100 mg, 0.37 with empagliflozin 25 mg, 0.49 with empagliflozin 10 mg, and 0.64 with dapagliflozin 10 mg; credible intervals excluded no difference for those comparisons. Canagliflozin 100 mg produced more weight loss than several alternatives, including 1.37 kg more than dapagliflozin 10 mg. SBP reduction ranged from 6.1 mm Hg with canagliflozin 300 mg to 2.6 mm Hg with empagliflozin 10 mg, but some placebo comparisons were imprecise.

Dual therapy with metformin

All assessed SGLT-2 inhibitors improved the four outcomes versus placebo in the base case. Canagliflozin 300 mg gave the largest HbA1c reduction (0.77 percentage points) and weight loss (2.5 kg). Canagliflozin 100 mg had 0.15 points less HbA1c reduction than canagliflozin 300 mg; dapagliflozin 10 mg had 0.23 points less, but its 95% CrI crossed no difference. Canagliflozin 300 mg produced 0.88 kg more weight loss than empagliflozin 10 mg. The base case found no significant between-drug SBP differences. Sensitivity analysis was consequential: adding Henry and Kashiwagi materially changed SBP rankings, and adding Bolinder worsened dapagliflozin's HbA1c ranking.

Interpretation and limitations

The authors concluded that there were few clinically significant differences. Their interpretation was that canagliflozin's apparent monotherapy advantage did not persist in dual therapy, arguing against a large clinically important SGLT-1 contribution. Major limitations were no head-to-head trials, sparse networks, incomplete outcome reporting, fixed-effect modeling, short follow-up, differences in baseline BMI and glycaemia, use of directly randomized canagliflozin 300 mg despite real-world titration from 100 mg, and no comparative safety analysis.

Bayesian NMA workflow

Inputs: trial arm results for licensed agents and placebo. Process: multi-arm Bayesian network model → convergence assessment → fixed-effect selection → direct/indirect inconsistency check → sensitivity analyses. Outputs: relative effects with 95% credible intervals. Failure modes: sparse edges, incomplete outcome data, trial-effect modifiers, and influential atypical trials.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 8/10

Directly relevant to comparative SGLT-2 efficacy and historical formulary reasoning.

Evidence basis: Systematic class comparison of HbA1c, weight and SBP.

Limiting factor: No cardiorenal outcomes or inpatient evidence.

Would raise/lower: A contemporary comparative effectiveness dataset would raise this score.

Novelty: 7/10

At publication, the network synthesized multiple SGLT-2 agents without requiring head-to-head trials.

Evidence basis: Bayesian NMA and explicit canagliflozin potency question.

Limiting factor: Indirect comparison was already a known method and the evidence base was sparse.

Would raise/lower: Direct comparative RCTs or richer class-wide outcomes would increase novelty.

Method strength: 6/10

Search, duplicate checking, risk-of-bias assessment and Bayesian NMA were methodologically coherent.

Evidence basis: Multiple databases, Cochrane bias tool, multi-arm correlation, convergence checks and sensitivity analyses.

Limiting factor: Fixed-effect modeling, sparse networks, incomplete outcomes and no head-to-head trials constrain robustness.

Would raise/lower: More trials, random-effects robustness and transparent trial-level data would raise the score.

Evidence strength: 5/10

The efficacy signal versus placebo is consistent, but between-drug differences depend on indirect evidence and some sensitivity analyses materially change rankings.

Evidence basis: RCT network with credible intervals and inconsistency checks.

Limiting factor: Small study counts, source trial-count discrepancy, short follow-up, and unstable SBP sensitivity findings.

Would raise/lower: Direct comparative trials and stable results across modeling assumptions would raise the score.

External validity: 5/10

The evidence spans several regions but trial populations and baseline BMI differed substantially.

Evidence basis: Multiple international RCTs.

Limiting factor: 24–26-week duration, selected trial populations, varying baseline HbA1c/BMI and small participants per center in some trials.

Would raise/lower: Longer, pragmatic and more representative studies would raise the score.

Implementation value: 4/10

Useful for historical glycaemic class context but insufficient for current formulary interchangeability or inpatient practice.

Evidence basis: Provides relative HbA1c/weight/SBP estimates and methodological caveats.

Limiting factor: No safety comparison, no cardiorenal outcomes, no current guideline alignment.

Would raise/lower: Current comparative outcomes, safety and practice guidance would raise the score.

Main strengths. Systematic multi-database retrieval, duplicate checking of extraction/quality assessment, explicit Bayesian modeling, multi-arm handling, credible intervals, inconsistency checks, and sensitivity analyses.
Main weaknesses. No head-to-head comparisons, sparse evidence per pair, short follow-up, incomplete reporting, fixed effects, outcome instability in sensitivity analysis, industry funding of all component trials, and no comparative safety assessment.

Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high for what this 2016 source supports. Confidence is lower for any current-practice inference because contemporary evidence and guidance were deliberately not imported into this source-focused artifact.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use it for

Historical class-efficacy context; early comparative HbA1c/weight/SBP estimates; identifying modeling assumptions and trial heterogeneity that can distort indirect comparisons.

Do not use it for

Current SGLT-2 safety ranking, cardiovascular/renal/heart-failure equivalence, inpatient substitution policy, or contemporary guideline recommendations.

Read next

Direct comparative effectiveness evidence, modern outcome trials and current guideline/consensus documents should be layered on top of this historical efficacy network before formulary decisions.
