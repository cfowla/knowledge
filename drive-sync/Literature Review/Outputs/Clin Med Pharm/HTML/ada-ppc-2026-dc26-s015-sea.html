One-page synthesis

This chapter organizes diabetes-in-pregnancy management across preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum care. The highest-load-bearing recommendations are early pregnancy planning with effective contraception until glucose is optimized, preconception A1C below 6.5% when safely achievable, pregnancy glucose monitoring with fasting below 95 mg/dL and postmeal targets, CGM for type 1 diabetes pregnancy, insulin as the required or preferred pharmacotherapy for type 1 diabetes, GDM, and type 2 diabetes in pregnancy, pregnancy-targeted AID for type 1 diabetes, aspirin prophylaxis for preeclampsia risk, chronic-hypertension treatment below 140/90 mmHg, avoidance of fetotoxic medications, postpartum OGTT after GDM, and lifelong diabetes surveillance.

The chapter repeatedly separates strong recommendations from areas of uncertainty. Early treatment of hyperglycemia below overt diabetes is not universally beneficial. CGM evidence outside type 1 diabetes remains limited. Metformin and glyburide cross the placenta and are not first line, with incomplete long-term offspring safety. Aspirin is recommended despite limited diabetes-specific efficacy data. AID systems without pregnancy-specific targets require careful selection and expert assistive techniques. The source is directly actionable, but its development methods and evidence-grading definitions are delegated to the separate Introduction and Methodology, limiting chapter-only appraisal of guideline methodology.

Section condensation

Diabetes in pregnancy and preconception counseling

The chapter frames pregnancy risk as driven largely by hyperglycemia but modified by chronic diabetes complications and comorbidities. It lists spontaneous abortion, fetal anomalies, preeclampsia, fetal demise, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, hyperbilirubinemia, and respiratory distress among major risks. Preconception counseling is therefore routine care from puberty onward, with family planning and effective contraception maintained until treatment and A1C are optimized. A1C below 6.5% is the preferred preconception target when safely achievable.

Preconception care extends beyond glucose. It includes interprofessional care when available, nutrition and physical activity, diabetes self-management education, medication review, retinal assessment, screening for nephropathy and other comorbidities, and standard obstetric prepregnancy care. In a cited randomized trial, at least 5% prepregnancy weight loss among people with overweight or obesity and prior GDM was associated with lower GDM recurrence, OR 0.18 (95% CI 0.04–0.88). GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists should be stopped before pregnancy, with enough time to transition to pregnancy-compatible therapy and reach preconception glycemic goals.

Glycemic goals, A1C, and CGM

Pregnancy glucose goals are tighter than nonpregnancy goals. Recommendation 15.8 sets fasting below 95 mg/dL, 1-hour postmeal below 140 mg/dL, or 2-hour postmeal below 120 mg/dL. Table 15.2 adds lower target bounds for type 1/type 2 diabetes and insulin-treated GDM. A1C falls physiologically in pregnancy and misses postmeal excursions, so it is secondary to direct glucose monitoring. The ideal A1C is below 6% when this can be achieved without significant hypoglycemia; below 7% is acceptable when needed for safety.

CGM is recommended for type 1 diabetes pregnancy. CONCEPTT found better TIR/TAR and modestly better A1C without more hypoglycemia, along with fewer LGA births, shorter infant hospital stays, and less severe neonatal hypoglycemia. Pregnancy type 1 CGM targets are 63–140 mg/dL for more than 70% of time, below 63 mg/dL for under 4%, below 54 mg/dL for under 1%, and above 140 mg/dL for under 25%. The chapter does not extend the same strength of evidence to all type 2 diabetes or GDM pregnancies. Early abnormal glucose metabolism below overt diabetes remains a major uncertainty. TOBOGM showed a modest composite neonatal benefit, 24.9% versus 30.5%, RR 0.82 (95% CI 0.68–0.98), but subgroup heterogeneity and possible harm at lower glycemic levels argue against universal early treatment.

Nutrition, lifestyle, insulin, AID, and noninsulin therapy

Nutrition counseling favors whole foods and balanced macronutrients. The cited pregnancy dietary reference intake includes at least 175 g carbohydrate, 71 g protein, and 28 g fiber daily. Lifestyle therapy is foundational in GDM; cited studies suggest 70–85% of people diagnosed using Carpenter-Coustan criteria may manage GDM with lifestyle alone. Regular exercise has been reported to reduce odds of GDM by 38%, gestational hypertension by 39%, and preeclampsia by 41%. A meta-analysis of 32 randomized trials supports telehealth combined with in-person GDM care across several maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Insulin is required for type 1 diabetes pregnancy and is the preferred agent for GDM and type 2 diabetes pregnancy. Insulin needs often fall early, then rise about 5% per week from roughly week 16 through week 36, commonly doubling relative to prepregnancy. Both MDI and pump therapy are acceptable. Pregnancy-targeted AID now has strong support. In AiDAPT, AID improved TIR by 10.5 percentage points, reduced TAR by 10.2 points, and lowered A1C by 0.31 points. Systems without pregnancy-specific targets may still help selected patients but require expert techniques. Metformin and glyburide are not first line because they cross the placenta and may fail glycemic control; offspring safety remains incompletely resolved.

Preeclampsia, chronic hypertension, and other medications

The chapter recommends aspirin 100–150 mg/day beginning at 12–16 weeks for pregnant people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes; 162 mg/day may be acceptable. It simultaneously states that diabetes-specific evidence for reduction of preeclampsia remains insufficient. For diabetes with chronic hypertension, treatment should be initiated or titrated below 140/90 mmHg and deintensified below 90/60 mmHg.

A CHAP secondary analysis in 434 people with diabetes and chronic hypertension found that attained pressure below 130/80 mmHg was associated with a lower composite adverse perinatal outcome, 19.3% versus 46.5%, adjusted RR 0.43 (95% CI 0.30–0.61), plus lower severe preeclampsia, indicated preterm birth, and NICU admission. This analysis was not randomized to the lower goal, so residual confounding remains. ACE inhibitors and ARBs should be stopped before pregnancy because later gestational exposure can cause fetopathy. Most lipid-lowering therapy is stopped, but selected very-high-risk patients may be considered for continuation after shared risk-benefit review.

Postpartum care

Insulin sensitivity rises abruptly after placental delivery. One cited study found immediate postpartum insulin requirements about 34% below prepregnancy requirements, with return toward baseline over 1–2 weeks. The chapter recommends active insulin reassessment, breastfeeding support, contraception, psychosocial support, and attention to hypoglycemia during lactation and disrupted sleep.

After GDM, the preferred 4–12-week test is a 75-g OGTT rather than A1C because postpartum A1C can be distorted and the OGTT is more sensitive. Lifelong screening every 1–3 years is recommended. The chapter estimates lifetime maternal diabetes risk after GDM at 50–60% and cites roughly 10-fold higher type 2 diabetes risk. For people with prediabetes and prior GDM, intensive lifestyle intervention and metformin have durable prevention value. The cited Diabetes Prevention Program follow-up reports 35% and 40% reductions in progression to diabetes over 10 years for lifestyle intervention and metformin, respectively, versus placebo.

Limiting factor: local formulary, device access, and maternal-fetal medicine resources still shape implementation. Local pathways can translate the guidance directly.

Main limitations

  • The chapter is a secondary guideline source, not the primary evidence behind its cited trial results.
  • The full ADA guideline-development and evidence-grading method is outside this PDF.
  • Evidence remains limited or heterogeneous for early abnormal glucose treatment, CGM in all type 2 diabetes/GDM pregnancies, non-pregnancy-targeted AID, aspirin efficacy specifically in preexisting diabetes, and long-term offspring outcomes after noninsulin pharmacotherapy.
  • Several device and medication comments depend on current U.S. availability and labeling.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Preconception
Build pregnancy intention, contraception, A1C, medication safety, renal and retinal screening, and nutrition into routine diabetes visits before conception.
Monitoring
Use pregnancy-specific fasting/postmeal targets. In type 1 diabetes, pair CGM with direct pre/postmeal goals rather than substituting GMI for A1C.
Pharmacotherapy
Insulin remains the core pregnancy agent. Avoid first-line metformin/glyburide for GDM and review ACE inhibitor, ARB, MRA, lipid-lowering, and GLP-1/GIP-GLP-1 therapies before conception.
Technology
Pregnancy-targeted AID has the clearest support. Non-pregnancy-targeted systems require individual selection, assistive techniques, and expert teams.
Hypertension
Use aspirin prophylaxis per the chapter and treat chronic hypertension below 140/90 mmHg while recognizing the evidence boundary around lower attained pressures.
Postpartum
Reduce insulin promptly as needed, support breastfeeding and contraception, use 75-g OGTT at 4–12 weeks after GDM, and continue screening every 1–3 years.
