One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. The paper is worth keeping as a clinical-AI benchmark design example. Its current-value claim is about evaluation method and failure modes, not about what a 2026 model can do.

In trial 1, ChatGPT-3.5 answered 9 of 30 questions accurately, 30%, and ChatGPT-4 answered 12 of 30, 40%. The difference was not significant, p=0.51. Neither version answered all three questions correctly in any category. Both scored 0 of 3 in administration/preparation. The supplement shows the most serious example: ChatGPT-4 confused Arexvy with ibalizumab-uiyk and supplied an IV HIV regimen instead of vaccine administration instructions.

Repeatability was also weak. On the 10 repeated questions, ChatGPT-3.5 accuracy moved from 30% to 20% to 10% across days 0, 1, and 7. ChatGPT-4 moved from 60% to 40% to 50%. Pairwise kappa values ranged from 0.21 to 0.62 for ChatGPT-3.5 and 0.23 to 0.80 for ChatGPT-4. Word burden was much higher than Lexicomp. Mean word counts were 153.6, 201.1, and 30.2 for ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4, and Lexicomp. The two assessors both said they would not recommend ChatGPT to a health care provider.

Main interpretive limit. The study asks a useful safety question, but its evidence is frozen to old model snapshots and one prompting style. It shows that a clinical LLM can fail badly and inconsistently. It does not show the present-day accuracy of ChatGPT as a product family.

Section condensation

Introduction

The paper frames AI chatbots as a potentially accessible alternative to subscription drug-information resources, while focusing on the clinical risk of inaccurate or misleading responses. The stated gap is the limited evaluation of broad, practical drug-information questions and the limited head-to-head comparison of ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4.

Methods

The investigators built 30 pharmacist-oriented questions across 10 categories. They searched Lexicomp first to create the comparison answers. Each question was sent in a fresh chat with history cleared and no follow-up prompting. Two clinical pharmacists rated completeness and accuracy on a four-point scale. A third pharmacist adjudicated disagreements. Scores 3 and 4 counted as accurate. Ten questions were randomly selected, one per category, and repeated on days 1 and 7.

McNemar testing compared model accuracy. Cohen kappa measured pairwise agreement, Cochran Q tested accuracy over time, chi-square tested category and cardiology subgroup differences, and ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests compared word counts.

Results

Overall accuracy was low, 30% for ChatGPT-3.5 and 40% for ChatGPT-4. The category results were uneven, and administration/preparation was 0 of 3 for both models. The repeatability analysis showed changing classifications across days for several questions. The calcium gluconate plus ceftriaxone example changed from inaccurate to accurate and back to inaccurate for ChatGPT-3.5.

The models were also verbose. Mean word count was 153.6 for ChatGPT-3.5 and 201.1 for ChatGPT-4 versus 30.2 for Lexicomp, p<0.001. Median counts were 139.5, 215, and 23, p=0.035. Cardiology questions were numerically more accurate for both models, but subgroup differences were not significant.

Discussion and limitations

The authors attribute possible errors to hallucinations, single-turn prompting, and stale training data. Their core concern is practical: an answer can be wrong on one day and correct on another, which is a bad property for a clinical reference tool.

The paper names four main design limits. The question sample was small and may have been underpowered for category comparisons. Accuracy scoring required subjective judgment. The usability survey allowed neutral responses. The study used one reference source even though drug-information practice often uses more than one source.

Conclusion

The authors advise caution when using ChatGPT for drug information because the tested versions had poor accuracy and inconsistent responses. They call for larger studies, broader question sets, newer AI systems, and further usability work.

Evaluation and appraisal

9/10

Relevance

Direct fit for clinical pharmacy and clinical-AI evaluation.

Evidence basis. Real-world pharmacist questions, drug-information accuracy, reproducibility, and usability.

Main limit. The tested models and workflow are dated.

Would improve with. Replication with current models and contemporary drug-information workflows.

6/10

Novelty

The study combines broad drug-information categories with repeat testing and usability rather than testing one narrow question type.

Evidence basis. Thirty questions across 10 categories, three time points for a repeated subset, and assessor usability data.

Main limit. Small question set and prior literature already documented variable ChatGPT accuracy.

Would improve with. A larger external question bank, prespecified taxonomy, and broader model comparison.

5/10

Method strength

The protocol is transparent and uses independent pharmacist rating with adjudication, but the sample is small and the question set is author-constructed.

Evidence basis. Two raters, third-rater adjudication, calibration, repeated trials, prespecified tests, supplement with full question list.

Main limit. No external sampling frame, no multiple-reference gold standard, and limited power.

Would improve with. Larger prospectively sampled questions, blinded adjudication, multiple reference sources, and confidence intervals.

5/10

Evidence strength

The observed errors and repeatability failures are real within the tested conditions, but the study supports a narrow claim about two 2024 model versions.

Evidence basis. 30-question primary trial, repeated 10-question subset, direct examples in the supplement.

Main limit. Subjective accuracy scale, one reference source, sparse category-level denominators, no current-model validation.

Would improve with. Independent replication with current systems and a stronger clinical reference standard.

3/10

External validity

The result does not generalize well to later models, iterative prompting, retrieval-enabled workflows, or other clinical settings.

Evidence basis. Single-turn prompts, March 2024 model snapshots, one institution-derived question set.

Main limit. Rapid model change and restricted prompt protocol.

Would improve with. Multisite question sampling and repeated evaluation across model versions, prompts, and tool-enabled conditions.

7/10

Implementation value

The paper gives a useful evaluation pattern for clinical AI: real questions, explicit reference answers, repeated runs, error examples, and word-burden measurement.

Evidence basis. Full question inventory and reproducibility example make the design easy to reproduce.

Main limit. It is more useful as a benchmark-design template than as a current capability estimate.

Would improve with. A reusable benchmark package with question provenance, gold-answer adjudication, and automated longitudinal reruns.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the paper's internal findings, moderate for broader interpretation. The main article and all four supporting tables were available. The main limitation is temporal. The study evaluated March 2024 systems.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Do not treat one correct run as validation

Repeat the same clinical question at separated time points and report item-level switching, not only mean accuracy.

Keep the gold answer outside the model

Build the reference answer before testing the model. Record the source and adjudication process so the benchmark can be reviewed later.

Measure answer burden

Clinical usefulness is not only correctness. Track response length and whether reviewers can find the relevant sentence quickly.

Preserve failure examples

The Arexvy error is more informative than the overall 40% number because it shows a confident drug-identity substitution with a fully invented regimen.

Use a stronger modern replication design

For a new benchmark, sample questions prospectively, use more than one authoritative source when needed, blind adjudicators to model identity, and test single-turn plus clinically realistic follow-up or tool-enabled workflows.

Do not use this as a 2026 capability estimate

Use it as evidence that clinical LLM evaluation needs versioned, repeated testing. The measured accuracy values belong to the specific models and protocol tested in March 2024.
