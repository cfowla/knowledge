One-page synthesis

Verdict: Do not use for practice. The topic is clinically important, but the supplied article has multiple internal conflicts that break the evidence chain from included studies to pooled estimates.

The article claims that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce all-cause mortality by 14% and heart-failure hospitalization by 26% across a broad heart-failure population. It reports pooled HR 0.86 for mortality and 0.74 for HF hospitalization, along with improvements in NT-proBNP, LVEF, and diuretic efficiency. The authors describe consistent effects across ejection-fraction groups, diabetes status, individual SGLT2 inhibitors, and follow-up duration.

Those claims are not reliable from this source. Table 1 contains 21,808 participants when its 15 sample-size rows are summed, not the reported 28,484. Figures 2 and 3 use each total study sample size as the denominator for both treatment and control columns. Supplementary Table S1 says many of the studies in those forest plots did not contribute mortality or hospitalization. The main text, figures, tables, supplement, and bibliography also disagree on trial names, endpoint counts, risk-of-bias counts, follow-up range, and the mortality confidence interval. Two included-study citations are explicitly a protocol and a design paper, yet the forest plots assign them outcome events.

Practical implication. Do not use this meta-analysis to support inpatient SGLT2 interchangeability, class-effect claims, formulary decisions, or patient-level treatment choices. Retrieve and appraise the primary trials separately.

Section condensation

Abstract and objective

The stated objective is to evaluate SGLT2 inhibitors for mortality, HF hospitalization, NT-proBNP, LV systolic function, and diuretic efficiency regardless of ejection fraction or diabetes. The abstract reports 15 RCTs and 28,484 participants, with mortality HR 0.86, HF hospitalization HR 0.74, NT-proBNP MD -168.4 pg/mL, LVEF +3.8 percentage points, and diuretic efficiency +480 mL/day.

The abstract presents these findings as consistent across HFrEF, HFpEF, diabetes status, and individual SGLT2 inhibitors. The source-integrity problems described below mean those estimates should be treated as article claims, not validated effects.

Introduction

The introduction describes the global burden of HF and reviews SGLT2 inhibitor mechanisms and trial evidence. It frames the gap as the need for a contemporary synthesis across HF phenotypes, diabetes status, mortality, hospitalization, biomarkers, LV function, and diuretic response.

The background section does not determine the validity of the pooled results. Some background citations also appear poorly matched to the claims they support, which adds to the broader citation-lineage concern.

Methods

The authors report PRISMA 2020 methods, searches of five major databases, dual-reviewer screening, prespecified eligibility criteria, dual data extraction, RoB 2, GRADE, fixed and random effects, I-squared, Cochran Q, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, funnel plots, Egger and Begg tests, trim-and-fill, and fail-safe N.

The design description is conventional, but the resulting study map is not reproducible from the article packet. The supplement and main report disagree on study identities and which endpoints each study contributed. That disconnect is more important than the stated methods.

Study selection and characteristics

Figure 1 reports 1,847 records, 428 duplicates, 1,419 screened records, 1,374 screening exclusions, 45 full-text assessments, 30 full-text exclusions, and 15 included studies. The Results text says the final set contains 28,484 participants and reports study sizes of 23 to 6,263 participants.

The 15 sample-size values in Table 1 sum to 21,808. The prose also says follow-up ranged from 6 to 36 months, while Table 1 ranges from 3 to 28 months. Supplementary Table S1 changes two study names relative to Table 1.

Risk of bias and certainty

The authors report RoB 2 and GRADE. The prose says 10 studies were low risk and five fell into moderate-risk descriptions. It assigns high certainty to mortality and HF hospitalization, moderate certainty to NT-proBNP and LV function, and low-to-moderate certainty to diuretic effectiveness.

Table 2 prints 13 overall Low ratings and two Some concerns ratings. This count does not match the prose. The article's certainty ratings therefore do not resolve the more basic problem of whether the correct studies and outcomes were pooled.

Primary outcomes

The Results report mortality HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.92, I-squared 18%, and HF hospitalization HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.81, I-squared 39%. Figure 3 matches the hospitalization estimate.

Figure 2 instead prints mortality HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.89. Both primary forest plots include all 15 studies even though Supplementary Table S1 maps only seven to mortality and eight to HF hospitalization. The row denominators duplicate each study's total sample size in both trial arms.

Secondary outcomes

The article reports NT-proBNP MD -168.4 pg/mL, LVEF +3.8 percentage points, and diuretic efficiency +480 mL/day. It states that 11 RCTs contributed NT-proBNP and 10 contributed LV systolic-function data.

Figure 4 shows 10 NT-proBNP rows, while the supplement maps nine. Figure 5 shows 10 LV-function rows, while the supplement maps five to related LV endpoints. The safety statement that renal adverse events did not increase is not accompanied by a pooled safety result in the supplied article.

Subgroups, sensitivity, and publication bias

The paper reports favorable subgroup estimates by HF phenotype, diabetes, agent, and follow-up. It also reports stable fixed-effect, leave-one-out, small-study exclusion, and placebo-only analyses. Egger and Begg tests are non-significant.

These analyses cannot repair incorrect or inconsistent study inputs. The trim-and-fill reporting is also unclear because it repeats the original HR and then supplies what appears to be the original confidence interval rather than a clearly labeled adjusted estimate.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the results as broad class-wide benefit and describe SGLT2 inhibitors as a cornerstone therapy across HF phenotypes and diabetes status. They acknowledge secondary-endpoint heterogeneity, small mechanistic studies, missing participant-level granularity, and unanswered questions about therapy sequence and acute HF.

The article's stated limitations do not address the main internal data-lineage problems identified in this appraisal. The conclusion is therefore stronger than the supplied evidence packet can support.

Source-integrity review

Gate result: failed. The supplied packet does not provide a coherent mapping from included publications to trial populations, endpoint contributions, event counts, and pooled estimates.
  1. CRITICAL. The article says the 15 included RCTs contain 28,484 participants, but the 15 sample sizes printed in Table 1 sum to 21,808, a difference of 6,676.
  2. CRITICAL. Figures 2 and 3 use each Table 1 study sample size as the denominator in both the treatment and control columns. Across the 15 rows, the printed per-arm denominators sum to 43,616, exactly twice the 21,808 Table 1 total.
  3. CRITICAL. The abstract and Results report pooled all-cause mortality HR 0.86 with 95% CI 0.79 to 0.92. Figure 2 prints HR 0.86 with 95% CI 0.83 to 0.89.
  4. CRITICAL. Supplementary Table S1 says only seven listed studies contributed all-cause mortality and eight contributed HF hospitalization, while Figures 2 and 3 include all 15 studies for both outcomes.
  5. MAJOR. Supplementary Table S1 uses Ganguly 2024 for the DELIVER-like row and Dougherty 2023 for the post-MI row. Main Table 1 uses Verma 2020 and Kotit 2023 in those positions.
  6. CRITICAL. The included-study list cites Mordi 2017 as a trial protocol and Ferreira 2025 as a design paper, yet Table 1 and the primary forest plots assign them completed-trial characteristics and mortality or hospitalization event data.
  7. MAJOR. The NT-proBNP Results text says 11 RCTs contributed data, Figure 4 plots 10 study rows, and Supplementary Table S1 maps nine rows to NT-proBNP.
  8. MAJOR. The LVEF Results text and Figure 5 use 10 studies, while Supplementary Table S1 maps only five rows to LVEF, LV systolic function, reverse remodeling, or an LV-function surrogate.
  9. MAJOR. The Results text describes follow-up as 6 to 36 months. Table 1 contains a 3-month minimum and a 28-month maximum.
  10. MAJOR. The risk-of-bias prose reports 10 low-risk trials and five trials described as moderate-risk categories. Table 2 prints 13 overall Low ratings and two Some concerns ratings.
  11. MODERATE. The study-selection prose says 30 articles were included from 45 potentially relevant records and then says 30 were excluded. Figure 1 instead shows 30 full-text exclusions and 15 included studies.
  12. MODERATE. The Results discuss SOGALDI-PEF and DAPA-ICG, but neither name appears in Table 1 or Supplementary Table S1.
  13. MODERATE. The trim-and-fill paragraph says the method altered a pooled HR of 0.86 without major changes and then gives 0.79 to 0.92, which is formatted like the original confidence interval rather than a clearly reported adjusted estimate.

These findings are based on comparisons within the supplied PDF and Supplementary Table S1. No discrepancy was silently corrected. No external bibliographic correction was performed because the run did not activate external verification.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 8/10

Directly addresses SGLT2 inhibitors in heart failure and class-wide clinical outcomes.

Evidence basis. The topic fits heart-failure and formulary evidence review.

Limiting factor. Internal source integrity prevents practical use.

What changes the score. A corrected meta-analysis with verified trial lineage would retain high relevance.

Novelty 4/10

The claimed update through 2025 is recent, but the central clinical question is established.

Evidence basis. The paper adds recent trials and secondary functional outcomes in its stated scope.

Limiting factor. The source does not demonstrate a reliable new synthesis because trial mapping is inconsistent.

What changes the score. A transparent, reproducible search and correct trial set could support a higher score.

Method strength 1/10

The reported PRISMA, RoB 2, GRADE, subgroup, sensitivity, and bias methods are undermined by incompatible source tables and figures.

Evidence basis. The paper describes standard meta-analysis methods.

Limiting factor. Sample totals, endpoint mapping, trial identities, denominators, risk-of-bias counts, and primary confidence intervals conflict within the supplied packet.

What changes the score. A reproducible data table linking each included trial to verified outcomes and effect estimates would be required.

Evidence strength 1/10

The pooled estimates cannot be trusted from this source because the underlying event and study mapping is internally inconsistent.

Evidence basis. The paper prints precise pooled estimates and sensitivity analyses.

Limiting factor. Figures use duplicated total-study denominators as both treatment and control denominators, and some included citations are protocols or design papers while the forest plots assign them outcome events.

What changes the score. Independent reconstruction from the cited primary trials would be required.

External validity 2/10

The paper claims broad applicability across ejection fraction, diabetes status, drugs, and follow-up, but the evidence base cannot be validated from the supplied source.

Evidence basis. The stated population is broad.

Limiting factor. The trial set and subgroup lineage are unstable across the main table, supplement, and figures.

What changes the score. A verified patient and trial composition with prespecified subgroup definitions would be required.

Implementation value 1/10

This source should not guide formulary or clinical decisions.

Evidence basis. The topic is operationally relevant.

Limiting factor. Internal inconsistencies are severe enough that the pooled findings should not be carried into practice.

What changes the score. Use verified primary trials, major guidelines, and a trustworthy meta-analysis instead.

Final verdict: Do not use for practice.
Best use. A source-quality case study and a pointer list for independently retrieving the underlying trials.
