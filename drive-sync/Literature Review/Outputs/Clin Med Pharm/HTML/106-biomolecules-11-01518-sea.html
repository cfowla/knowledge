One-page synthesis

The review links hyaluronic acid (HA) biology to therapeutic design. It contrasts high- and low-molecular-weight HA, covers synthesis, enzymatic and oxidative degradation, hydration, extracellular-matrix functions, and receptor signaling through CD44, RHAMM, ICAM-1, and related binding partners. The therapeutic premise is that HA can provide lubrication and matrix support while also acting as a ligand for inflammatory-cell targeting, especially through CD44.

The application survey is widest in osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis but extends to tendinopathy, intervertebral-disc injury, inflammatory skin disease, wound healing, inflammatory bowel disease, lung inflammation, oral inflammation, bladder inflammation, acute kidney injury, and myocardial infarction. The source repeatedly highlights promising preclinical delivery systems and selected clinical observations, but it does not report a systematic search strategy, study-selection flow, risk-of-bias assessment, or quantitative evidence synthesis. The main practical value is therefore mechanistic and formulation-oriented evidence mapping, not practice-changing comparative effectiveness.

Section condensation

Introduction; biosynthesis, degradation, hydration

HA is presented as a hydrophilic extracellular-matrix glycosaminoglycan built from repeating D-glucuronic-acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units. Native high-molecular-weight HA is associated with intact tissue and anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive functions, whereas smaller fragments can signal tissue distress and promote inflammation. HA is synthesized at the inner plasma membrane by HAS-1, HAS-2, and HAS-3.

Degradation occurs through hyaluronidases and reactive oxygen species. The review emphasizes rapid turnover and compartment-dependent half-life, plus exceptional water-binding and dynamic matrix behavior. These physicochemical properties underlie hydration, viscoelasticity, lubrication, tissue permeability, and delivery-system behavior.

Biological functions and mechanisms

HA contributes to lubrication, hydration, matrix organization, cell migration, repair, and tissue remodeling. The source separates passive structural functions from signaling functions. Molecular weight, receptor expression, signaling pathways, and cellular state can alter whether HA-associated signaling is pro- or anti-inflammatory.

CD44 receives the greatest attention as a broadly expressed HA receptor and therapeutic targeting handle. RHAMM/CD168, ICAM-1, and other HA-binding receptors extend the mechanistic map to motility, growth-factor responses, leukocyte adhesion, and tissue repair.

Inflammatory joint disease

Osteoarthritis occupies the largest formulation table. The review surveys intra-articular HA, drug conjugates, liposomes, micelles, hydrogels, particles, lubricated polymers, and combination strategies aimed at pain control, cartilage preservation, chondrogenesis, and inflammation reduction. Most tabled systems are preclinical or mixed in-vitro/in-vivo programs rather than direct clinical comparators.

For rheumatoid arthritis, activated macrophage CD44 expression is used as the main targeting rationale. The reviewed systems include corticosteroid, methotrexate, curcumin, photosensitizer, and anti-inflammatory payloads in HA-functionalized carriers. Table 2 again mixes in-vitro and animal evidence with diverse routes and endpoints.

Tendinopathy, disc injury, and skin inflammation

Tendinopathy is framed around HA's anti-inflammatory, lubricating, viscoelastic, and tissue-compatible properties. The disc-injury section considers HA-containing hydrogels and extracellular-matrix approaches for hydration and regeneration. The skin section discusses HA in matrix maintenance and selected cell-model repair experiments.

These sections are heterogeneous and not organized as comparative evidence reviews. The value is in mapping candidate mechanisms and delivery concepts rather than estimating a treatment effect.

Wound healing and inflammatory bowel disease

During wound healing, HA is described as accumulating in the wound bed and coordinating inflammatory-cell and fibroblast migration, cytokine signaling, and phagocytosis. Table 3 summarizes five topical or local HA systems with reported secondary outcomes involving healing time, cytokines, growth factors, tissue repair, and wound area.

For inflammatory bowel disease, high CD44 expression in inflamed tissue motivates targeted delivery. The review describes HA/5-ASA, budesonide-loaded HA nanoparticles, KPV systems, HMWHA, and other constructs. Table 4 reports mucosal healing, inflammation relief, regeneration, symptom improvement, and epithelial-barrier restoration across heterogeneous experimental models.

Lung, oral, bladder, renal, and cardiac inflammation

The lung section reports a crosslinked HA/sodium-ascorbyl-phosphate system that reduced IL-6 and ROS in a Calu-3 model. Oral applications center on tissue lubrication and inflammatory periodontal conditions. In bladder inflammation, HA and chondroitin sulfate are discussed as GAG-layer and anti-inflammatory strategies.

The renal section describes an HA-curcumin prodrug with improved solubility, renal accumulation, and epithelial-cell uptake in ischemia-reperfusion work. The cardiac section discusses HA in myocardial-infarction repair and reports beneficial secondary findings for HA oligosaccharides in a rat model.

Conclusions and future outlook

The authors conclude that HA has wide biomedical potential because of its mechanical, swelling, lubrication, hydration, regenerative, and receptor-binding properties. They emphasize targeted delivery through receptors such as CD44 and note sustained interest in OA and RA as alternatives or adjuncts to conventional treatment.

The article itself identifies important delivery barriers: short half-life in biological fluids, potential added toxicity from crosslinking, and viscosity that may hinder administration or effectiveness. It calls for longer-circulating or longer-resident systems, better understanding of degradation, simpler administration routes, and more study outside articular disease.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best use is mechanistic orientation, formulation ideation, and citation discovery. Do not use it alone to choose an HA product, route, dose, or treatment strategy.

Relevance 7/10

Rationale. Broadly relevant to inflammation, drug delivery, biomaterials, and clinical pharmacy.

Evidence basis. Multiple inflammatory organ systems and HA carrier strategies are covered.

Limiting factor. Breadth reduces disease-specific depth.

What would change it. A narrower clinical question with current evidence would raise practical relevance.

Novelty 5/10

Rationale. Useful synthesis of 2017–2021 work, but no new experiment or formal synthesis method.

Evidence basis. Mechanisms and applications are compiled from prior literature.

Limiting factor. Narrative review format.

What would change it. A systematic comparative framework or new quantitative synthesis would raise novelty.

Method strength 3/10

Rationale. The article gives a scope aim but no reproducible systematic-search or appraisal method.

Evidence basis. No database/search strategy, screening flow, risk-of-bias framework, or certainty grading is reported.

Limiting factor. Selection and emphasis may be subjective.

What would change it. Prespecified systematic methods and study-level quality assessment would materially raise the score.

Evidence strength 4/10

Rationale. Many promising results are secondary reports from heterogeneous in-vitro, animal, and selected clinical work.

Evidence basis. Tables explicitly mix in-vitro and in-vivo programs and summarize endpoint directions rather than pooled estimates.

Limiting factor. No quantitative synthesis or certainty assessment.

What would change it. Direct primary-study appraisal and controlled clinical evidence would raise the score.

External validity 4/10

Rationale. Generalizability varies sharply across cell models, animal models, formulations, routes, and diseases.

Evidence basis. The review spans many experimental platforms and indication contexts.

Limiting factor. Translational distance is often substantial.

What would change it. Replicated human comparative studies in defined populations would raise external validity.

Implementation value 4/10

Rationale. Strong for research ideation and formulation mapping, weak for direct clinical implementation.

Evidence basis. Mechanisms, carriers, and routes are described but standardized dosing, patient-selection, monitoring, and comparative benefit-risk are not established.

Limiting factor. It is not a clinical guideline.

What would change it. Current guideline alignment and actionable human evidence would raise implementation value.

Clinical translation boundary. The source does not support a stand-alone recommendation to use HA for any listed inflammatory condition. Current indication-specific decisions require separate primary evidence, guideline, label, and patient-level review.

Implementation and research takeaways

Use as a map
Good source for identifying HA mechanisms, receptor targets, carrier designs, and disease areas worth deeper primary-literature review.
Preserve molecular weight
HMWHA and smaller fragments can have different biological effects; evidence extraction and formulation design should not collapse them into one exposure.
Track delivery liabilities
Residence time, degradation, viscosity, and crosslinking toxicity are core constraints.
Do not inherit trial claims
Read cited primary studies before treating tabled outcomes as primary evidence.
