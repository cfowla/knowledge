Scope warning. This is a very small uncontrolled, unblinded study. It provides a prospective signal of short-term motor change after prednisolone but does not establish comparative efficacy, optimal dosing, or long-term safety.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In nine children with FCMD whose motor function had begun to regress, alternate-day oral prednisolone was followed by a small but statistically significant improvement in GMFM total score over the study period. The signal is clinically interesting because FCMD motor function is expected to decline after its peak, but the design cannot separate treatment effect from measurement variability, regression to the mean, co-interventions, or other time-varying factors.

The cohort included one mild, seven typical, and one severe FCMD case. Prednisolone began at 0.5 mg/kg every other day and could be escalated monthly to 1.0 mg/kg every other day if response was inadequate. The mean age at initiation was 8.10 years (SD 2.14). Figure 1 reports a mean GMFM total-score change of +1.23 percentage points (SD 1.10), with eight of nine patients increasing and one decreasing; the Wilcoxon signed-rank test yielded P=0.015. Individual total-score changes ranged from -0.30 to +2.80 percentage points.

Short-term adverse events were limited in this small cohort: Table 4 shows agrypnia in 3/9, irritability in 1/9, increased appetite in 1/9, and moon face in 1/9, with no discontinuation or suspension attributed to adverse events. The article also reports non-motor changes such as motivation, salivation, voice volume, and vocabulary, but two internal count discrepancies reduce confidence in those secondary observations: Table 6 shows decreased salivation in three patients while the discussion says two, and Table 6 shows increased vocabulary in one patient while the discussion says two.

Section condensation

Abstract / research question

The authors frame FCMD as a disease without established disease-modifying treatment and note prior case reports suggesting steroid responsiveness. Their stated objective was to evaluate oral steroid therapy for restoring or maintaining motor function in FCMD patients with documented motor decline.

The abstract reports alternate-day prednisolone starting at 0.5 mg/kg, escalation to 1.0 mg/kg if response was inadequate, GMFM as the motor outcome, Wilcoxon signed-rank testing, mean age 8.10 years, mean GMFM change +1.23 (SD 1.10), and P=0.015.

Background

FCMD is linked to FKTN mutations and abnormal alpha-dystroglycan glycosylation. The article describes typical motor development followed by regression and draws a mechanistic/therapeutic analogy to Duchenne muscular dystrophy, where corticosteroids are established to slow functional decline.

The authors cite case reports of FCMD steroid use, including short-term treatment around viral illness, as the rationale for a prospective intervention study. These prior reports are background support, not evidence generated by this cohort.

Methods

The study was open-label and single-arm. Eligible patients had genetically diagnosed FCMD, were 3-10 years old, had clinically confirmed motor decline, and consented to steroid treatment. Prednisolone started at 0.5 mg/kg on alternate days and could be increased by 0.2-0.25 mg/kg on alternate days each month after an inadequate 1-month response, to a maximum of 1.0 mg/kg alternate-day.

Motor function was assessed with the 88-item GMFM across five dimensions; total score is the average of the dimension percent scores. Post-treatment evaluation occurred 6 months after medication compliance was confirmed. Before-after comparisons used a Wilcoxon signed-rank test with P<=0.05.

Results

Nine patients were treated: four boys and five girls; eight had homozygous 3-kb FKTN insertion mutations and one had a composite heterozygous mutation. Clinical forms were one mild, seven typical, and one severe. Observed alternate-day prednisolone doses at evaluation were 0.5 mg/kg in two patients, 0.7 mg/kg in one, and 1.0 mg/kg in six; mean treatment duration was 8.86 months.

Mean GMFM total-score change was +1.23 percentage points (SD 1.10), with 8/9 patients increasing; P=0.015. Dimension A increased in 8/9; dimension B increased in 5/9, was unchanged in 2/9, and decreased in 2/9; among evaluable patients, dimension C increased in 1/2 and dimension D in 1/1. Short-term adverse events did not lead to treatment discontinuation.

Discussion / interpretation

The authors interpret the observed functional improvements as evidence that steroid therapy may improve or maintain motor function even after FCMD regression has started and speculate that earlier initiation might improve peak motor performance. They also discuss possible swallowing, pulmonary, motivational, and language effects, while acknowledging uncertainty about dose-response and optimal timing.

The principal limitations named by the authors are the small sample size, heterogeneous steroid doses, and delays related to confirming drug compliance. The discussion calls for larger studies, a more robust dosing schedule, and long-term safety/effectiveness assessment, especially when starting therapy early.

Data availability / disclosures

Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. Funding came from a National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry psychological/neurological disease research grant (2-4) and the SHISEIKAI Scientific Award. The authors declared no competing interests.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Do not use for practice. Best use is as preliminary prospective evidence supporting further controlled study of corticosteroids in FCMD. It is not strong enough to establish routine treatment, optimal dose, duration, starting age, or long-term risk-benefit.
Relevance
5/10

Clinically relevant to rare neuromuscular pharmacotherapy, but narrow in population and not directly generalizable to common inpatient practice.

Evidence basis: Prospective steroid intervention in genetically defined FCMD with functional outcome measurement.

Main limiting factor: Very rare disease and highly specialized context.

Would change with: Higher score if the clinical question specifically concerns FCMD management.

Novelty
7/10

The article presents an intervention cohort beyond prior case reports and is framed as the first independent intervention study in FCMD.

Evidence basis: Prospective single-arm treatment of nine patients after prior evidence was largely case-based.

Main limiting factor: Novelty rests partly on the authors’ literature framing and the sample is small.

Would change with: Would fall if comparable prospective cohorts predated it; rise with demonstrably first-in-field controlled evidence.

Method strength
3/10

Prospective measurement is useful, but the open-label single-arm design and n=9 substantially limit causal inference.

Evidence basis: Defined eligibility, regimen, GMFM instrument, paired statistical test, trial registration.

Main limiting factor: No control group or blinding; heterogeneous dose/duration; compliance-related timing variability.

Would change with: Randomized or matched control design, blinded outcome assessment, prespecified endpoint hierarchy, and larger sample.

Evidence strength
3/10

The GMFM signal is internally consistent for most patients, but the magnitude is small and unsupported by a contemporaneous comparator.

Evidence basis: Mean change +1.23 pp, 8/9 increases, P=0.015, patient-level data visible in Figure 1.

Main limiting factor: Small sample, uncontrolled natural-history comparison, no confidence interval, and internal inconsistencies in secondary outcomes.

Would change with: Replicated controlled evidence with effect estimates, confidence intervals, clinically meaningful thresholds, and long-term follow-up.

External validity
2/10

Generalizability is very limited.

Evidence basis: Single Japanese center; 8/9 patients had the homozygous 3-kb founder insertion; only one mild and one severe case.

Main limiting factor: Tiny, genetically and geographically concentrated cohort.

Would change with: Multicenter cohorts with broader genotypes, clinical forms, ages, and standardized dosing.

Implementation value
4/10

The dosing escalation and GMFM follow-up are described clearly enough to inform research protocols, not routine care.

Evidence basis: Starting dose, escalation increments, maximum dose, evaluation timing, and observed short-term adverse events are reported.

Main limiting factor: Long-term safety, optimal start/stop criteria, dose-response, vaccine implications, and comparative benefit remain unresolved.

Would change with: Controlled dose-ranging and long-term safety data with explicit monitoring and stopping rules.

Key threats to inference: absence of a control group; unblinded treatment and outcome context; tiny sample; heterogeneity in dose and treatment duration; uncertain clinical significance of a 1.23-point mean change; no reported confidence interval; secondary-outcome count inconsistencies; and incomplete supplement availability in the retrieved source set.
What the study does well: clinically defined regressing FCMD population, explicit steroid regimen, validated motor-function instrument, patient-level Figure 1 data, paired within-patient analysis, trial registration, and transparent acknowledgement of major limitations.

Practice / implementation takeaways

What this supports

A hypothesis that alternate-day prednisolone may produce short-term motor-function improvement or stabilization in some children with FCMD after decline has begun.

What this does not support

Routine steroid treatment, a preferred dose, a preferred age to start, duration of therapy, long-term safety, vaccine policy, or a quantified causal effect versus no treatment.

Research-useful details

0.5 mg/kg alternate-day starting dose; escalation by 0.2-0.25 mg/kg alternate-day monthly after inadequate response; maximum 1.0 mg/kg alternate-day; GMFM follow-up after compliance confirmation.

Next evidence needed

Multicenter controlled cohorts or randomized designs, blinded GMFM assessment, standardized dose and timing, clinically meaningful change thresholds, longer safety follow-up, and reconciliation of non-motor outcome definitions.
