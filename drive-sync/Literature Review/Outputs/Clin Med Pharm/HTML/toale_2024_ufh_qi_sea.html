One-page synthesis

Core thesis. A multidisciplinary bundle of policy clarification, EHR/order-set redesign, nursing communication tools, medication availability changes, and nurse education was associated with improved adherence to an institutional nurse-driven UFH protocol. Overall compliance rose from 79.4% to 85.2%, while median time to first therapeutic aPTT decreased modestly from 831.5 to 808 minutes.

Why the paper matters: its strongest contribution is operational rather than causal. It makes visible the many small failure points in UFH management - timing, duplicate lab orders, manual aPTT scheduling, undocumented off-protocol changes, hold/restart ambiguity, medication availability, and delayed review of results - and shows how an anticoagulation stewardship program can coordinate changes across nursing, pharmacy, providers, laboratory services, informatics, and patient safety.

Principal limitation: the study uses a descriptive, uncontrolled before/after design. It reports no confidence intervals, formal statistical testing, adjusted analysis, or patient-centered safety outcomes. The observed process improvements therefore support feasibility and workflow redesign, not a strong causal claim that the bundle improved clinical outcomes.

Clinical / QI question

PopulationAdult patients on the institution's VTE UFH protocol for at least 6 hours. Exclusions included transition from a low-therapeutic-goal protocol, provider-customized protocols, and transfer-in while already receiving a UFH infusion protocol.
InterventionMultidisciplinary QI bundle: policy updates, EHR/order-set changes, nursing communication orders, UFH infusion reporting, Pyxis stocking on high-use units, and nursing education.
ComparatorHistorical baseline period before the intervention bundle, with an intermediate planning/implementation period.
OutcomesCompliance with eight predefined standards of care and time to first therapeutic aPTT; narrative tracking of selected "never events" and safety-event reports.
Population-definition ambiguity. Although the project is framed around the "VTE UFH protocol," Table 1 lists only 83%-85% of infusions as VTE indications; 9%-11% were atrial fibrillation and 6% other. The paper does not fully explain why non-VTE indications were managed under this protocol designation.

Methods

Protocol and monitoring framework

The institutional VTE protocol used an initial UFH bolus of 80 units/kg followed by 18 units/kg/hour. aPTT was checked at baseline, 6 hours after infusion initiation, and 6 hours after dose changes. After two consecutive therapeutic aPTT values, monitoring was reduced to daily. The institution's therapeutic aPTT range for VTE was 60-100 seconds, explicitly described as institution-specific.

Data acquisition and eligibility

Clinical pharmacists used an EHR-derived report containing UFH order/start times, pharmacy verification/preparation/delivery information, aPTT collection and processing times, and medication administration record actions. Data collection stopped when the infusion was discontinued or held for more than 2 hours. Pump interoperability became available in October 2020 and could be used to clarify MAR discrepancies during later phases.

QI design and standards

A multidisciplinary workgroup was formed in June 2020. After baseline analysis, the group created a project charter, prioritized failure modes using a process flowchart, Pareto chart, and prioritization matrix, and began interventions in December 2020. The stated aim was a 10% relative increase in overall compliance, from 79.4% to 87.3%, by December 2022.

#Standard of care
1Baseline aPTT within 48 hours before UFH initiation.
2Initiate UFH within 60 minutes of the ordered start time.
3Obtain aPTT within 5-7 hours of infusion initiation or dose change.
4Obtain, process, and record the aPTT result within 2 hours.
5Follow the UFH protocol when an aPTT-directed dose change is indicated.
6Adjust the infusion, when needed, within 30 minutes of the aPTT result.
7Move to once-daily aPTT after two consecutive therapeutic values.
8Obtain the appropriate aPTT while the patient is receiving UFH.

Standards 2, 3, and 6 were designated focus areas because baseline adherence was low and the team identified multiple improvement opportunities. Data were reviewed every 1-3 months and entered into REDCap. Early aPTTs that were not acted upon were excluded from compliance impact; tests drawn less than 5 hours after initiation or dose change and acted upon were considered noncompliant.

Analytic limitation. The report is primarily descriptive. It does not report hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, adjusted modeling, or a contemporaneous control group. It also does not provide the opportunity-level denominator for each standard, making precision and weighting of the reported compliance percentages difficult to assess.

Interventions

Policy clarificationDefined timing for continuous-infusion starts; required documented orders for off-protocol changes; clarified coagulation specimen handling; clarified hold/restart processes.
Baseline aPTT workflowAllowed UFH to start after the specimen was obtained but before the result was available; allowed nurses to discontinue a redundant baseline aPTT order when a result existed within 48 hours.
Medication availabilityPlaced premixed UFH bags in Pyxis machines on high-utilization units to reduce avoidable start delays.
Routine-lab cleanupAdded nursing communication and provider reminders to discontinue routine daily aPTT orders that could conflict with protocol-timed draws.
Information visibilityDisplayed the three most recent aPTTs on the MAR and the most recent aPTT in the ordering screen; created a shared UFH infusion report containing rate, boluses, weight, anticoagulant orders, aPTTs, worklist tasks, standing aPTT orders, and hold orders.
Hold/restart orderCreated a dedicated UFH infusion communication order with explicit stop and restart dates/times for procedures.
Nursing educationPharmacy and nursing education created a module covering UFH basics, protocol details, new EHR functions, and common baseline errors; completion was monitored.

Discussion, limitations, and future improvements

Authors' interpretation

The authors conclude that the QI initiative improved compliance and decreased time to therapeutic aPTT, while acknowledging that the 10% compliance target was not reached. They describe residual delays in starting UFH as often unavoidable - for example, line-access problems, patient absence from the unit, or provider instructions to delay therapy - and emphasize documentation of such exceptions.

Residual workflow barriers

aPTT ordering remained manual: nurses still had to calculate when the next test was due, enter the order, and contact a dispatch number for phlebotomy. Result-driven infusion adjustment also depended on a nurse noticing the result in the open EHR. These residual human-memory and handoff dependencies explain why the project improved but did not fully automate the process.

Never events and safety reports

The paper states that two predefined "never events" - acting on the baseline aPTT as though it were the 6-hour level and starting the infusion at a rate different from the provider order - did not occur postimplementation. It also states that very few safety events and no serious safety events were reported after project completion. Exact event counts and denominators are not provided, so these observations should be treated as supportive narrative rather than quantified outcome evidence.

Future work

The authors planned annual nursing anticoagulation competency training and continued stewardship surveillance. They also describe a future intention to reduce the initial recommended dose for obese patients to prevent early supratherapeutic aPTTs. That proposed dosing change was not tested by this project and should not be interpreted as an evidence-supported result of the study.

Major limitations

  • Retrospective data collection limited the ability to clarify individual events with bedside nurses.
  • No concurrent control group; secular changes and other institutional initiatives may contribute to observed improvements.
  • The intervention was bundled, so the effect of individual components cannot be isolated.
  • Pump interoperability became available during the project, potentially changing data visibility and measurement fidelity across periods.
  • No inferential statistics or uncertainty estimates are reported.
  • No bleeding or thrombotic clinical outcomes were captured.
  • Single-center cancer-center environment limits direct generalization to hospitals with different nurse/phlebotomy/EHR workflows.
  • The institutional aPTT therapeutic range (60-100 s) is reagent- and coagulometer-specific and should not be generalized directly.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first for UFH workflow / protocol redesign; do not use alone to justify patient-outcome claims. The paper is particularly useful as a concrete medication-use-system case study. Its evidence is much stronger for implementation feasibility and failure-mode identification than for clinical efficacy.

Validity appraisal

Internal validity: moderate-to-low for causal effect, moderate for descriptive process surveillance. The baseline, implementation, and postimplementation periods are clearly defined, and data sources are granular, but the intervention overlaps with broad institutional changes and no control series is available.

Outcome validity: strong for operational questions where the outcome is directly protocol adherence; weaker for the implied safety claim because protocol compliance is a surrogate. The authors explicitly state the assumption that higher compliance lowers complication rates rather than measuring that relationship.

Clinical significance: overall compliance improved meaningfully as a process metric, especially for initiation and result-processing. The median time-to-first-therapeutic-aPTT improvement was only 23.5 minutes from a baseline of about 13.9 hours; without statistical uncertainty or clinical outcomes, its clinical importance is uncertain.

What the source supports

  • A structured multidisciplinary audit can identify multiple human-factor and EHR failure points in nurse-driven UFH protocols.
  • A bundled intervention can be associated with improved adherence across several protocol steps.
  • Making relevant aPTT and infusion information visible in the MAR/reporting workflow is a plausible and implementable safety strategy.
  • Manual lab scheduling, phlebotomy handoffs, and result recognition remain residual failure modes even after substantial EHR redesign.

What the source does not support

  • That the intervention reduces major bleeding, recurrent VTE, mortality, or other clinical outcomes.
  • That any single intervention component caused the improvement.
  • That the institution's aPTT target or dosing protocol should be adopted elsewhere.
  • That reducing the initial UFH dose in obesity is beneficial; this is presented only as a future plan.
  • That aPTT is superior or inferior to anti-Xa monitoring.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Audit the entire protocol, not only dose selectionMeasure order-to-start time, lab timing, specimen-to-result time, time-to-action, hold/restart behavior, duplicate laboratory orders, and protocol deviations separately.
2. Surface protocol state at the point of workThe most recent aPTTs, current infusion rate, last rate change, pending draw, boluses, hold orders, and active anticoagulants should be visible without hunting across the chart.
3. Eliminate ambiguous communicationOff-protocol changes and peri-procedural holds should require explicit electronic orders with dates/times rather than undocumented verbal instructions.
4. Remove avoidable start delaysDo not let redundant baseline labs or medication distribution delays prevent timely initiation when local policy permits treatment after specimen collection.
5. Treat phlebotomy as part of the medication systemCorrect ordering is insufficient if specimen collection requires a separate dispatch call or has unpredictable turnaround.
6. Distinguish process success from clinical benefitTrack bleeding, thrombosis, transfusion, recurrence, and other patient-centered outcomes if the goal is to demonstrate safety or effectiveness rather than workflow compliance alone.
Best use: blueprint for local UFH protocol failure-mode review and anticoagulation stewardship redesign.
Do not use for: selecting a universal aPTT target, changing UFH dosing in obesity, or claiming reduced bleeding/thrombosis without additional evidence.
