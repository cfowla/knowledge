One-page synthesis

Core thesis: A fixed aPTT range embedded in a heparin nomogram is not automatically portable across laboratories because aPTT response to heparin depends materially on reagent and analytical system. The letter argues that transferring the McMaster thresholds without calibration could produce substantial over-anticoagulation at a site using a less heparin-sensitive reagent.

Raschke and Hertel use proficiency-testing variability and a concrete cross-site example to show the problem. At McMaster, aPTT 60–85 seconds was associated with heparin concentrations of 0.2–0.4 U/mL. At their institution, using plain Dade Actin, the corresponding aPTT range was 35–50 seconds with a mean control of 25 seconds. They therefore warn that importing an 85-second “therapeutic” threshold could correspond locally to heparin concentrations above 0.7 U/mL.

Levine accepts the generalizability problem but defends the nomogram as a locally standardized tool. His proposed solution is assay cross-calibration: compare the same patient plasma with Actin FS and the receiving laboratory’s thromboplastin, then convert the local aPTT to an Actin-FS-equivalent. He also argues for a standardized aPTT reporting system, drawing an analogy to INR standardization of prothrombin time.

Why this belongs in a heparin deep dive: it is a concise historical demonstration that a dosing algorithm and its laboratory target cannot be separated from the assay used to generate that target.
What it does not establish: this correspondence does not validate a modern therapeutic aPTT target, compare contemporary monitoring strategies, or report bleeding/thrombosis outcomes. Its numeric targets are historical and assay-specific.

Source condensation

Main letter — Raschke & Hertel

The authors focus on analytic heterogeneity in aPTT testing. They note multiple thromboplastin sources and laboratory systems and cite CAP proficiency data showing a single specimen could yield materially different aPTT results by method. They contrast McMaster’s highly sensitive reagent (therapeutic heparin 0.2–0.4 U/mL corresponding to aPTT 60–85 seconds) with their own Dade Actin system (same heparin range corresponding to 35–50 seconds; mean control 25 seconds).

They argue that blindly accepting 85 seconds as therapeutic at their site could imply heparin concentrations >0.7 U/mL and aPTT >3× control. Their conclusion is that the Cruickshank nomogram is not directly generalizable to laboratories using differently responsive aPTT reagents and could produce dangerous over-heparinization if transplanted without calibration.

Reply — Levine

Levine agrees that commercial aPTT reagents differ markedly in heparin responsiveness and that the published nomogram may not directly generalize. He emphasizes that its value at McMaster was local standardization and faster achievement/maintenance of the institution’s therapeutic aPTT range.

For transfer to another assay, he proposes paired testing of patient plasma with Actin FS and the new reagent and conversion to an Actin-FS-equivalent. The reply’s example maps 35 seconds with Dade Actin to 60 seconds with Actin FS. Levine then argues that aPTT monitoring needs a standardization approach analogous in concept to INR for prothrombin time.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High conceptual value for understanding assay-dependent heparin monitoring and nomogram portability; low evidentiary strength for defining contemporary therapeutic targets.
Clinical / topic relevance — 8/10Directly addresses a core heparin-monitoring failure mode: treating an assay-dependent aPTT threshold as universally portable. Lowered because it is historical correspondence rather than a current monitoring standard.
Internal validity / evidence strength — 3/10The argument is plausible and quantitatively illustrated, but the letter does not provide a formal study design, patient sample size, statistical methods, or clinical outcomes. Would rise with paired-sample validation and outcome data.
Quantitative transparency — 5/10Key values are explicit and interpretable, but derivation of the local heparin/aPTT correlations is not reported in enough detail to reproduce them. Would rise with raw sample counts, regression/calibration methods, and uncertainty.
External validity — 4/10The paper’s central point is precisely that fixed thresholds have poor transportability across reagents. The calibration principle is broadly useful; the numeric ranges are site/reagent specific. Would rise with multi-laboratory validation.
Implementation value — 6/10Provides a clear operational rule: validate/cross-calibrate the laboratory target before transplanting a dosing nomogram. Not sufficient as a standalone modern protocol.

Main strengths

  • Uses a concrete proficiency-testing example to make assay variability clinically legible.
  • Connects laboratory-method variation to the risk of excessive heparin exposure rather than treating it as an abstract analytical issue.
  • The reply openly accepts the limitation and supplies a practical cross-calibration concept.
  • The exchange separates the usefulness of standardized dosing logic from the portability of fixed assay thresholds.

Main limitations

  • Letter/reply format; no prospective comparative clinical study.
  • No patient-level sample size, bleeding outcomes, thrombosis outcomes, or formal validation statistics.
  • Several quantitative claims are secondary reports or local correlations whose methods are not fully described here.
  • Historical reagents and therapeutic conventions limit direct contemporary application of the exact numbers.
  • The proposed cross-reagent conversion is conceptually useful but only illustrated with one example in the correspondence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

RetainDo not transplant fixed aPTT thresholds between institutions or assays without local validation/calibration.
Design implicationWhen evaluating a heparin nomogram, separate the dose-adjustment logic from the assay-specific therapeutic range that drives that logic.
Laboratory implicationDocument the reagent/instrument context and the evidence used to establish the local therapeutic monitoring range.
Do not overclaimDo not use the paper’s 35–50 sec, 60–85 sec, or 1.5–2.0× control values as universal contemporary targets.
Practice-change judgment: no direct contemporary practice change should be based on this correspondence alone. Its best use is historical/methodological support for assay-specific validation and monitoring standardization.
