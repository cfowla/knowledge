PICO-like clinical frame

Population: middle-aged and older men with symptoms/signs or end-organ features possibly attributable to androgen deficiency, low or low-normal serum testosterone, and no identifiable hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular pathology. Intervention: testosterone therapy (transdermal gel in TEAAM, T-Trials, and TRAVERSE; long-acting intramuscular testosterone undecanoate in T4DM), generally 1-4 years. Comparator: placebo; T4DM applied the same community lifestyle programme to both groups. Outcomes: vitality/mood, cognition, sexual function, glucose metabolism, body composition, muscle/physical function, bone/fracture, anemia/erythrocytosis, cardiovascular events/VTE, PSA/prostate/LUTS.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: The four landmark trials support modest, domain-specific benefits of testosterone in selected men without classical HPT-axis disease, while short-term MACE and prostate-cancer findings are broadly reassuring. The review simultaneously identifies important counter-signals—erythrocytosis, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism uncertainty, PSA-driven evaluation, urinary retention, and unexpectedly more trauma-pattern fractures—and argues for individualized rather than symptom-agnostic prescribing.

The most reproducible benefit pattern is improvement in libido/sexual activity, correction or prevention of anemia, increased lean mass and strength, and increased bone density/estimated bone strength. Vitality and mood effects are small. Cognition is not convincingly improved. Glycemic results are heterogeneous: T4DM, which combined intramuscular testosterone with a lifestyle programme, reduced diabetes incidence, whereas the TRAVERSE diabetes substudy did not show a clear glycemic benefit. This makes formulation, population, co-intervention, outcome measurement, and disease stage important when comparing trials.

Safety evidence is strongest for short-term major cardiovascular events because TRAVERSE was specifically powered for MACE and met noninferiority, but the study had high discontinuation and did not eliminate uncertainty around arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, or longer-term outcomes. The fracture result is clinically important because testosterone improved bone metrics but did not reduce major osteoporotic fracture and was associated with more overall clinical fractures, mostly ankle/rib patterns attributed to trauma. Prostate cancer incidence was low, but high-risk men were excluded and biopsy ascertainment was not systematic.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction and review selection

The authors distinguish classical hypogonadism with identifiable HPT pathology from the common later-life pattern of low or low-normal testosterone without an identifiable HPT lesion. They explicitly focus on four placebo-controlled trials enrolling at least 300 men, lasting at least 1 year, and assessing clinically important outcomes.

This is not presented as a systematic review. The paper uses the four trials as a clinical decision framework and treats “testosterone therapy” as an umbrella term because in men without a defined HPT deficit the intervention may function as pharmacotherapy rather than physiologic replacement.

Vitality, mood, and cognition

TEAAM and T4DM, which admitted low-normal testosterone concentrations, did not improve global health-related quality of life. T-Trials and TRAVERSE, which used lower testosterone thresholds, showed small improvements in vitality/mood, especially in symptomatic men. TRAVERSE also showed no sleep-quality improvement.

Cognitive effects were negative overall. The T-Trials cognitive substudy did not improve memory/cognition in men with age-associated memory impairment; an exploratory whole-cohort executive-function signal requires confirmation. TEAAM and TRAVERSE found no clinically meaningful cognitive benefit.

Sexual function

TEAAM found no sexual benefit, but the authors regard that result as discordant with the other trials and note possible attenuation from higher baseline testosterone and treatment design. T-Trials improved libido, sexual activity, and satisfaction across most measures; mean IIEF erectile-function improvement was 2.64 points, below the ~4-point benchmark cited for a clinically important erectile-function effect in PDE5-inhibitor studies. T4DM showed a 2.10-point IIEF improvement (95% CI 0.95-3.26), while TRAVERSE improved libido/sexual activity but not erectile function.

The authors therefore place more weight on low libido than erectile dysfunction as a reason to consider testosterone in a hypogonadal phenotype, especially when erectile dysfunction is likely vascular or neurovascular.

Body composition and glucose metabolism

The review summarizes a general testosterone-associated reduction in fat mass (~1-4 kg) and increase in lean mass (~1.5-5 kg). T-Trials reduced fasting insulin (adjusted mean difference -1.7 µIU/mL; P=.02) and HOMA-IR (-0.6; P=.03) without lowering fasting glucose. In T4DM, diabetes at 2 years occurred in 12.4% (55/443) with testosterone versus 21% (87/413) with placebo, RR 0.59 (95% CI 0.43-0.80; P=.0007), despite no HbA1c effect.

TRAVERSE diabetes analyses did not show a significant prevention/remission effect. The authors highlight major cross-trial differences—intramuscular versus transdermal therapy, the T4DM lifestyle co-intervention, glycemic measurement by OGTT versus HbA1c, and medication-tracking differences—before hypothesizing that earlier insulin-resistant disease may be more modifiable than longstanding diabetes.

Muscle and physical function

Testosterone reliably increases lean mass and strength. TEAAM improved chest/leg press and stair-climbing power. T4DM increased total muscle mass by 1.71 kg (95% CI 1.32-2.11) and grip strength by 2.19 kg (95% CI 1.28-3.10). T-Trials did not meet the prespecified mobility-substudy walking response but showed walking/self-reported function improvements across the full cohort; falls were not reduced.

The authors consider these physical effects real but insufficient to support testosterone solely for physical-function enhancement.

Bone health

T-Trials increased trabecular-spine volumetric BMD by 6.8% (95% CI 4.8-8.7), total-hip volumetric BMD by 1.3% (0.8-1.7), and estimated spine trabecular strength by 8.5% (6.0-10.9). T4DM increased lumbar-spine areal BMD 3.3% (2.7-3.9), total hip 1.9% (1.2-2.7), and femoral neck 1.7% (0.8-2.7).

These surrogate improvements did not translate into fewer major osteoporotic fractures. TRAVERSE instead reported more overall clinical fractures: 3.50% (91/2601) versus 2.46% (64/2603), with ankle and rib fractures accounting for much of the excess. The authors therefore recommend proven antifracture therapy for men at high fragility-fracture risk regardless of testosterone decisions.

Hematologic outcomes

Testosterone corrected anemia in both T-Trials and TRAVERSE. In T-Trials unexplained anemia, hemoglobin rose by ≥1.0 g/dL in 54% with testosterone versus 15% with placebo; adjusted mean difference 0.82 g/dL (95% CI 0.48-1.39; P=.002). TRAVERSE showed more anemia resolution and less incident anemia.

Erythrocytosis was strongly formulation-dependent in this synthesis: uncommon with transdermal regimens but seen in 22% of testosterone-treated versus 1% of placebo-treated men in T4DM using long-acting intramuscular testosterone undecanoate.

Cardiovascular and thromboembolic outcomes

TEAAM did not worsen carotid intima-media thickness or coronary calcium. A T-Trials coronary-CT substudy showed greater non-calcified plaque increase but had materially unbalanced baseline plaque volumes. TEAAM, T-Trials, and T4DM had few MACE and were underpowered for cardiovascular safety.

TRAVERSE was powered for MACE: 7.0% with testosterone versus 7.3% with placebo, meeting noninferiority; all-cause mortality was 5.5% versus 5.7%. Nonfatal arrhythmias requiring intervention were higher (5.2% vs 3.3%; P=.001). VTE was 1.7% versus 1.2% (HR 1.46, 95% CI 0.92-2.32), and pulmonary emboli numbered 24 versus 12; the review treats the PE signal as uncertain but clinically relevant for high-risk men.

Prostate and lower urinary tract outcomes

PSA rose more with testosterone across trials. TEAAM mean difference was 0.28 ng/mL (95% CI 0.06-0.49); in T-Trials, PSA ≥4.0 ng/mL occurred in 1.9% versus 0.3%; TRAVERSE mean PSA change was 0.20 versus 0.08 ng/mL (P<.001). Prostate cancer events were rare and similar in TRAVERSE (12 vs 11; P=.87), but high-risk men were excluded and biopsy ascertainment was not systematic.

LUTS generally did not differ, although severe baseline LUTS was excluded. TRAVERSE showed no incident BPH/LUTS difference (45 vs 46) but a nonsignificant urinary-retention signal (50 vs 34; P=.08) and more AKI events (60 vs 40; P=.04), which the authors consider possibly due to chance.

Conclusions and outlook

The authors argue for personalized decision-making: match testosterone to specific plausible benefits rather than treating a low level alone, address lifestyle and other diseases, use proven osteoporosis therapy where indicated, and counsel about PSA evaluation, erythrocytosis, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism uncertainty, urinary retention, and fracture findings.

They explicitly caution that several outcomes are secondary analyses, all included trials were ≤4 years, and longer-term cardiovascular/prostate safety and aging outcomes such as independence and dementia remain unresolved.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for orientation; do not use as a stand-alone practice authority.
The paper efficiently crosswalks the landmark trial evidence and is especially useful for identifying which outcomes are robust, modest, heterogeneous, or safety-limited. Its narrative-review design and reliance on multiple secondary/substudy outcomes mean practice decisions should return to primary trials and current regulatory/guideline sources.
Relevance9/10

Directly addresses clinically actionable testosterone benefits and risks in older men without classical HPT pathology.

Evidence basis: Four landmark placebo-controlled trials spanning sexual, metabolic, musculoskeletal, hematologic, cardiovascular, and prostate outcomes.

Limiting factor: Not a treatment guideline and does not address every hypogonadism phenotype.

Would change with: Would be lower if the task concerned classical pituitary/testicular hypogonadism; higher only if paired with current guideline implementation detail.

Novelty8/10

The 2024 review integrates TRAVERSE, T4DM, T-Trials, TEAAM, and the unexpected TRAVERSE fracture analysis into one clinical frame.

Evidence basis: Incorporates recently available large-trial safety and fracture findings that materially changed the evidence landscape.

Limiting factor: Many individual efficacy findings predate the review.

Would change with: Would rise with a formal quantitative synthesis or new patient-level comparative analysis.

Method strength6/10

Source trials are mostly strong randomized evidence, but the review itself is narrative rather than systematic.

Evidence basis: Explicit trial-size/duration criteria and detailed source tables; major RCTs are directly compared.

Limiting factor: No reported systematic search, PRISMA flow, formal risk-of-bias method, or certainty grading.

Would change with: Would rise with reproducible search/selection methods, formal bias assessment, and prespecified synthesis methods.

Evidence strength7/10

Evidence is strong for several short-term trial outcomes, especially TRAVERSE MACE noninferiority, but mixed for secondary outcomes and rare harms.

Evidence basis: Large placebo-controlled trials with quantitative outcome reporting; TRAVERSE specifically powered for MACE.

Limiting factor: Many endpoints are secondary/substudy analyses; rare-event safety estimates and long-term outcomes remain imprecise.

Would change with: Would rise with replicated fracture/PE/arrhythmia results, longer follow-up, and more trials powered for patient-centered outcomes.

External validity6/10

Applicable to selected middle-aged/older men with low testosterone and no identifiable HPT lesion, but trial exclusions and heterogeneity narrow generalization.

Evidence basis: Broad age ranges and substantial comorbidity in TRAVERSE; multiple formulations and populations across trials.

Limiting factor: High prostate-risk/severe LUTS patients were excluded; duration ≤4 years; few data specifically in men >70 with frailty or fragility fracture risk.

Would change with: Would rise with longer pragmatic trials including older, frailer, higher-risk populations and consistent diagnostic criteria.

Implementation value8/10

The review translates trial findings into a benefit-risk conversation and flags which outcomes should not drive prescribing alone.

Evidence basis: Concrete effect sizes, adverse-event signals, formulation differences, and a personalized decision-making example.

Limiting factor: It does not provide a formal prescribing algorithm, monitoring protocol, or current regulatory/guideline reconciliation.

Would change with: Would rise with explicit monitoring thresholds, contraindications, stopping rules, and updated regulatory/guideline alignment.

Internal validity and interpretation cautions

  • Randomization supports causal inference within each primary trial, but the review sometimes compares heterogeneous trials as a group.
  • T4DM’s lifestyle co-intervention and intramuscular formulation make its diabetes result non-equivalent to TRAVERSE’s transdermal regimen.
  • Several important findings are secondary or subgroup analyses; the authors explicitly warn against overinterpreting them.
  • TRAVERSE discontinuation exceeded 60% in both groups, relevant to exposure contrast and long-term interpretation.
  • Prostate safety is constrained by exclusion of high-risk men and incomplete/systematically unequal biopsy ascertainment.
  • Bone-density improvements are surrogate outcomes; the fracture signal moves in the opposite direction for overall clinical fractures.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Potentially decision-relevant benefits
Low libido/sexual activity, unexplained anemia, lean mass/strength, and bone-density/strength outcomes have the clearest positive signals. Mood/energy benefit is modest.
Do not overclaim
No established cognitive benefit; no proof that better bone metrics reduce fracture; glycemic benefit is not consistent across trial designs; testosterone should not substitute for proven osteoporosis therapy.
Safety counseling
Discuss erythrocytosis, PSA-driven evaluation, arrhythmia signal, pulmonary-embolism uncertainty, urinary retention in men with more LUTS, and the TRAVERSE overall-fracture signal.
Monitoring logic suggested by evidence
Formulation matters for erythrocytosis; baseline prostate/LUTS risk and hematocrit matter; cardiovascular conclusions are strongest for short-term MACE, not all cardiovascular outcomes or indefinite treatment.
Best use: rapid orientation before reading TRAVERSE/T4DM/T-Trials primary papers, teaching, or constructing a benefit-risk checklist. Do not use for: standalone diagnosis, current regulatory indication, or a complete treatment/monitoring protocol.

Current verification — external to the 2024 paper

Why this is separate: the following is current regulatory context checked on 2026-08-11. It is not part of the source paper and does not alter what the paper reported.

FDA’s 2025 class-wide action, following TRAVERSE and ambulatory blood-pressure studies, removed boxed-warning language about increased adverse cardiovascular risk from testosterone products and added/required blood-pressure warnings. FDA’s current testosterone information page also states that in June 2026 the agency requested additional prescribing-information changes, including removal of wording that safety/efficacy in age-related hypogonadism had not been established and revisions to prostate-cancer/BPH safety information.

Regulatory indication remains a distinct question: FDA’s current testosterone information page continues to state that approved products are for men with low testosterone in association with a medical condition and that products are not approved for men with low testosterone who lack an associated medical condition. In April 2026 FDA encouraged sponsors to discuss development of a potential low-libido indication for idiopathic hypogonadism; this was described as a potential future indication, not an existing approval.
