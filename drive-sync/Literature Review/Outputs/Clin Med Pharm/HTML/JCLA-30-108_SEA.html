One-page synthesis

Core thesis: after the same 100 U/kg weight-based UFH loading dose, higher-BMI groups showed greater heparin exposure relative to estimated blood volume and larger late ACT responses, leading the authors to argue that actual-body-weight dosing may over-anticoagulate patients with higher BMI.

The study enrolled 78 men and grouped them into BMI quartiles. ACT was measured before UFH and at 5, 10, 30, and 60 minutes. The strongest between-group signal was late: mean ACT60 rose from 261.3 seconds in quartile I to 308.9 seconds in quartile IV, and the proportion with ACT60 >300 seconds was 0%, 0%, 44.44%, and 50% across the four groups. The estimated UFH dose per blood volume and ΔACT-time AUC also increased with BMI.

The paper is clinically relevant to questions about UFH dosing in obesity, but its evidentiary weight is low. It is small, single-center, male-only, nonrandomized, relies on ACT surrogate outcomes, gives no patient-centered bleeding/thrombotic outcomes, and contains multiple internal numeric inconsistencies. The most important use is to support the biological plausibility that actual-body-weight UFH dosing can produce disproportionate pharmacodynamic exposure at higher BMI; it does not validate ideal-body-weight, adjusted-body-weight, dose-cap, or blood-volume-based dosing.

Source integrity issue: the PDF contains apparent data/reporting inconsistencies in Tables 1-3. These were preserved rather than silently corrected and materially lower confidence in the quantitative conclusions.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames ACT as an intraprocedural pharmacodynamic marker for UFH and asks whether BMI alters ACT response under a weight-based UFH strategy. Seventy-eight men were grouped by BMI quartile, received 100 U/kg UFH, and had ACT measured at baseline and 5, 10, 30, and 60 minutes.

It reports heterogeneous ACT peak timing, increasing UFH dose per blood volume and ΔACT-time AUC across BMI quartiles, and more ACT60 values above 250 and 300 seconds at higher BMI. The abstract concludes that actual-body-weight dosing may overdose patients with higher BMI.

Introduction

The introduction reviews UFH's variable pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics and the role of ACT monitoring during PCI. It argues that actual-body-weight dosing may be problematic in obesity because adipose tissue contributes less blood volume than lean tissue and therefore may not proportionally expand UFH distribution volume.

BMI is presented as an imperfect but accessible surrogate for adiposity. The stated aim is to test whether BMI is associated with ACT response after weight-based UFH and thereby provide a theoretical basis for heparin-loading guidance.

Materials and Methods

The study enrolled 78 men from November 2010 through May 2011. Exclusions included atrial fibrillation, acute cerebrovascular disease, active ulcer, coagulation disorders, malignancy, severe hepatic/renal dysfunction, hypoproteinemia, concurrent anticoagulants, recent LMWH/UFH, tirofiban use, and emergency PCI. BMI quartiles were 15.57-<23.03 (n=20), 23.03-<25.35 (n=20), 25.35-<27.68 (n=18), and ≥27.68 kg/m² (n=20).

All groups received 100 U/kg UFH. ACT was measured using Medtronic ACT II/Hepcon at baseline and four post-dose timepoints; duplicate ACTs were averaged, with exclusion if the pair differed by >12% of the average. ΔACT was defined relative to ACT0. A blood-volume-based heparin dose and ΔACT-time AUC were calculated. ANOVA, chi-square, and Fisher exact methods were used with P<0.05.

Results

Baseline weight/BMI increased across quartiles and height differed; age, antithrombin III, ACT0, and platelet count were reported as similar. ACT peak timing was heterogeneous: Table 3 totals are 24 patients at 5 minutes, 42 at 10 minutes, and 12 at 30 minutes. The paper reports percentages of 33.33%, 51.33%, and 15.34%, but the count-derived percentages are 30.77%, 53.85%, and 15.38%.

Between-group differences emerged primarily at later timepoints. ACT60 means were 261.30, 276.50, 297.70, and 308.90 seconds across quartiles I-IV (P reported as 0.000). Table 4 reports heparin dose per blood volume increasing from 1.36 to 1.64 U/mL, ΔACT-time AUC increasing from 2.3×106 to 5.1×106, ACT60>250 seconds in 60%, 100%, 100%, 100%, and ACT60>300 seconds in 0%, 0%, 44.44%, 50% (all P<0.01).

Discussion and conclusion

The authors argue that one ACT timepoint is inadequate because peak timing varies, and that ΔACT-time AUC may better reflect UFH effect. They propose that the relationship between heparin distribution and nonadipose tissue/blood volume may explain higher pharmacodynamic exposure in patients with higher BMI when a dose is scaled to total body weight.

The conclusion is cautious in wording but clinically suggestive: actual-body-weight nomograms may overdose higher-BMI patients. The authors acknowledge the male-only sample and call for PK/PD modeling to establish specific dosing recommendations. No conflict of interest was declared.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High topical relevance to UFH dosing in obesity, but low confidence for practice because the study is small, surrogate-driven, and internally inconsistent. Do not use for practice as a dosing rule.
Relevance
8/10
Directly addresses actual-body-weight UFH dosing, BMI, ACT, and potential over-anticoagulation. Basis: exact heparin/obesity question. Limit: PCI/angiography context and ACT surrogate. Would rise: direct clinical outcomes and broader populations.
Novelty
5/10
Provides a mechanistic BMI-stratified ACT dataset, but the concept that total-body-weight UFH may overexpose obesity was already recognized in cited prior work. Would rise: validated dosing model or new clinical endpoint evidence.
Method strength
3/10
Serial standardized ACT measurement is a strength, but there is no randomized dosing comparator, no multivariable modeling, a small sample, multiple testing, and apparent table errors. Would rise: prespecified modeling, robust QC, independent replication.
Evidence strength
3/10
The source shows pharmacodynamic associations, not clinical benefit/harm, and several key numerical inconsistencies weaken confidence. Would rise: reconciled data, uncertainty estimates, clinical bleeding/thrombotic outcomes.
External validity
2/10
Single center, 78 men, narrow procedural context, unusual BMI quartile definitions, and no external validation. Would rise: multicenter mixed-sex cohort across contemporary practice.
Implementation value
3/10
Supports caution with total-body-weight scaling but does not test an alternative dosing strategy or provide a validated dose cap/weight scalar. Would rise: comparative dosing arms plus efficacy/safety outcomes.

Strengths

  • Direct serial pharmacodynamic measurements after a common 100 U/kg UFH loading strategy.
  • Explicit BMI strata and repeated ACT timepoints permit evaluation of temporal heterogeneity.
  • Attempts to connect total-body-weight dosing to estimated blood volume and ΔACT-time AUC.

Major limitations

  • No randomized comparator or alternative dosing strategy.
  • Only male participants; n=78; single center.
  • ACT is a surrogate and no clinical bleeding/thrombotic outcomes were tested.
  • No confidence intervals or adjusted modeling for key results; multiple group/time comparisons are emphasized primarily through P values.
  • Internal reporting inconsistencies in Table 1 BMI, Table 2 ΔACT arithmetic, and Table 3 count-derived percentages.
  • Potential confounding by body composition and other covariates remains unresolved; BMI is only a crude adiposity proxy.

Clinical / pharmacotherapy translation

PICO-style framing

PopulationMen undergoing coronary angiography or PCI in one Chinese center, 2010-2011.
Intervention100 U/kg weight-based UFH loading dose.
ComparatorBMI quartiles; no alternative dose strategy.
OutcomesACT time course, ΔACT, estimated heparin dose per blood volume, ΔACT-time AUC, proportions above ACT thresholds.
Useable takeaway: this paper adds mechanistic support for avoiding the assumption that UFH pharmacodynamic exposure scales linearly with total body weight in higher-BMI patients. It is reasonable literature to cite when motivating study of dose caps, alternative weight scalars, or individualized monitoring.
Do not infer: that 100 U/kg is unsafe in obesity, that a specific BMI threshold mandates dose reduction, or that ideal/adjusted body weight is superior. The study did not compare those strategies and did not measure bleeding or ischemic outcomes.

Current-context verification: the article cites 2011 PCI guidance. Contemporary U.S. ACS guidance is the 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI guideline; the source therefore should be interpreted as historical mechanistic evidence, not current standalone dosing guidance. No external guideline claim was used to overwrite the study's reported findings.

Practical literature-review placement

Best use: obesity/UFH pharmacodynamic rationale; ACT monitoring limitations; hypothesis generation for alternative dose-scaling research. Do not use for: protocol dosing tables, obesity dose caps, or clinical outcome estimates.
