One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first / practice-shaping. For adult penicillin-resistant MSSA bacteremia, this is high-yield randomized evidence that cefazolin is noninferior to flucloxacillin or cloxacillin for 90-day mortality and has a clinically meaningful renal safety advantage. It is strongest for typical adult MSSA bacteremia and weakest for CNS infection, maintenance dialysis, pediatrics, and direct extrapolation to nafcillin/oxacillin.

Core thesis. In adults enrolled within 72 hours of index blood culture collection with penicillin-resistant, methicillin-susceptible S. aureus bacteremia, cefazolin met a prespecified Bayesian noninferiority criterion for 90-day all-cause mortality vs antistaphylococcal penicillins used in the trial (flucloxacillin or cloxacillin). Mortality was 15.0% with cefazolin vs 17.0% with comparator therapy; adjusted odds ratio 0.81, 95% credible interval 0.59 to 1.12; posterior probability of noninferiority 99.2%.

Safety and implementation signal. Cefazolin was associated with lower AKI within 14 days: 13.9% vs 19.6%; adjusted odds ratio 0.67, 95% credible interval 0.50 to 0.89; posterior probability of superiority 99.7%. Treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was also lower with cefazolin: 1.6% vs 9.1%; adjusted odds ratio 0.21. The approximate absolute AKI reduction was 5.7%, yielding an estimated number needed to treat of about 18 to prevent one AKI event if the association is causal in a similar population.

Clinical question

In adults with penicillin-resistant MSSA bacteremia requiring IV beta-lactam backbone therapy, should cefazolin be preferred or accepted over an antistaphylococcal penicillin for mortality and safety?

Clinical practice translation

What should change in practice
  • For most adults with confirmed MSSA bacteremia, cefazolin can be treated as a preferred or co-preferred IV definitive backbone rather than a weaker convenience alternative.
  • In patients with AKI risk, CKD, concomitant nephrotoxins, unstable renal function, or OPAT feasibility concerns, the renal safety and administration burden favor cefazolin over antistaphylococcal penicillins.
  • Order sets and stewardship pathways should permit cefazolin 2 g IV every 8 hours for standard adult MSSA bacteremia and 2 g IV every 6 hours for critical illness or endocarditis when renal function supports it.
  • Pharmacy review should emphasize loading-dose adequacy, renal adjustment after initial dose, daily renal monitoring during acute illness, allergy reconciliation, and treatment duration by uncomplicated vs complicated disease.
What should not change based on this trial alone
  • Do not shorten therapy, skip repeat blood cultures, relax source-control requirements, or reduce endocarditis evaluation because cefazolin was used.
  • Do not apply the result to MRSA, polymicrobial bacteremia, pediatric patients, maintenance dialysis, CNS infection, or patients with severe beta-lactam hypersensitivity.
  • Do not infer that oral step-down is safe earlier than existing protocols; early oral switch was a separate SNAP domain and not answered by this comparison.
  • Do not claim proven superiority for 90-day mortality; the posterior probability of superiority was high but the study was designed and stopped on noninferiority plus safety.

Renal adjustment

Cefazolin is excreted unchanged in urine, with a label-reported IV half-life of approximately 1.8 hours in normal renal function. The label recommends renal dose adjustment when adult CrCl is below 55 mL/min, after an initial loading dose appropriate to infection severity.

Contraindications and interactions

  • Contraindication: immediate hypersensitivity or serious skin reaction to cefazolin, cephalosporins, penicillins, or other beta-lactams. The trial excluded active penicillin or cefazolin allergy.
  • Renal/neurotoxicity: seizures can occur, particularly with renal impairment and excessive dosing. Adjust maintenance doses while preserving appropriate loading.
  • Interaction: probenecid inhibits renal excretion of cefazolin; coadministration is not recommended by the label.
  • Anticoagulation/coagulation: cephalosporins may reduce prothrombin activity; monitor PT/INR in high-risk patients and supplement vitamin K when clinically indicated.
  • Hepatic considerations: no routine hepatic dose adjustment is established for cefazolin, but hepatic impairment is a risk factor for prothrombin activity changes and the trial measured acute liver injury as a safety outcome.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The article addresses uncertainty over whether cefazolin or an antistaphylococcal penicillin should be preferred for adult MSSA bacteremia. The SNAP trial used an ongoing Bayesian adaptive platform and compared cefazolin with flucloxacillin or cloxacillin in adults with penicillin-resistant MSSA bacteremia.

At 90 days, all-cause mortality was 15.0% with cefazolin and 17.0% with flucloxacillin/cloxacillin, meeting the prespecified noninferiority criterion. AKI was lower with cefazolin, 13.9% vs 19.6%, which is the most actionable safety finding for acute-care pharmacy.

Background

S. aureus bacteremia has high mortality, with the article citing more than one in four affected patients dying within 90 days globally. Expert endocarditis guidance historically favored antistaphylococcal penicillins because of concern about the cefazolin inoculum effect in high-burden infections.

The article notes that observational meta-analyses suggested lower 30-day mortality and better adverse-event profile with cefazolin, but observational data were vulnerable to bias. The trial was designed to provide randomized comparative evidence for the antibiotic backbone question.

Trial design and oversight

The comparison was pragmatic, open-label, randomized, international, and embedded in the SNAP Bayesian adaptive platform. It included 91 sites in eight countries: Australia, Canada, Israel, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Singapore, South Africa, and the United Kingdom.

SNAP simultaneously evaluates multiple S. aureus bacteremia management questions. This report covers the methicillin-susceptible, penicillin-resistant S. aureus silo in the antibiotic-backbone domain.

Patients

Hospitalized adults were enrolled within 72 hours after the index blood culture. Notable exclusions included enrollment after 72 hours, polymicrobial bacteremia, limitations to active treatment, active penicillin or cefazolin allergy, maintenance dialysis, and ongoing systemic antibacterials with S. aureus activity that could not be stopped.

The restriction to penicillin-resistant MSSA was deliberate: the cefazolin inoculum effect depends on penicillinase production, so inclusion of penicillin-susceptible isolates could bias toward cefazolin noninferiority.

Randomization and interventions

Patients were randomized 1:1 without stratification. Assignment was revealed after eligibility for the MSSA silo was confirmed, and clinicians knew the assigned treatment.

The recommended cefazolin regimen was 2 g IV every 8 hours, intensified to every 6 hours for critical illness or endocarditis. Comparator regimens were flucloxacillin 2 g IV every 6 hours, intensified to every 4 hours for critical illness/endocarditis, or cloxacillin 2 g IV every 4 hours. Renal adjustment was allowed.

Outcomes and statistics

The primary outcome was all-cause death within 90 days after platform entry. Secondary outcomes included earlier mortality, microbiologic treatment failure, new foci of infection, C. difficile, AKI, RRT initiation, acute liver injury, and serious adverse reactions.

The primary model was a hierarchical Bayesian logistic-regression model adjusted for age, country, temporal epoch, and other platform-domain eligibility/assignment. Noninferiority required posterior probability support for an adjusted odds ratio below 1.2, approximating less than a 2.5 percentage-point mortality difference if comparator mortality was 15%.

Trial population

Of 2602 adults enrolled in the platform, 1757 had MSSA and 1341 were randomized in the MSSA silo: 671 to cefazolin and 670 to flucloxacillin/cloxacillin. Primary-outcome analysis included 645 cefazolin and 642 comparator patients after withdrawal/loss to follow-up.

Median age was 66 years, about 31% were women, and about 11% were in ICU at recruitment. More than 99% had ID specialist consultation, which matters for external validity because outcomes reflect high-quality SAB management rather than drug choice alone.

Primary and secondary results

Cefazolin met the prespecified noninferiority criterion for 90-day mortality: 97/645 deaths (15.0%) vs 109/642 (17.0%); adjusted odds ratio 0.81, 95% credible interval 0.59 to 1.12; posterior probability of noninferiority 99.2%; posterior probability of superiority 89.8%.

AKI within 14 days favored cefazolin: 92/660 (13.9%) vs 127/648 (19.6%); adjusted odds ratio 0.67, 95% credible interval 0.50 to 0.89. Serious adverse reactions and treatment discontinuation due to adverse events also favored cefazolin.

Subgroups and discussion

The protocol-adherent population still favored noninferiority but with lower posterior probability: 95.4%. In endocarditis, mortality was 8/51 (15.7%) with cefazolin vs 13/53 (24.5%) with comparator; adjusted odds ratio 0.54, but the credible interval was wide at 0.20 to 1.43.

The authors argue that cefazolin’s renal safety, longer half-life, and possible mortality advantage support its use despite acquisition cost and spectrum concerns. They acknowledge open-label design, clinician discretion after randomization, uncollected source-control/diagnostic variation, and pending isolate-level inoculum-effect analysis.

Safety signals and pharmacist-focused monitoring

Trial safety signalCefazolin reduced AKI and serious adverse reactions vs comparator therapy. The data and safety monitoring committee paused recruitment because of an AKI signal, then recommended closing the MSSA silo after the noninferiority trigger was met and the renal safety signal persisted.
Label safety signalCefazolin labeling highlights serious beta-lactam hypersensitivity, seizures in renal impairment, C. difficile-associated diarrhea, reduced prothrombin activity, gastrointestinal effects, allergic reactions, cytopenias, transaminase elevations, acute tubulointerstitial nephritis, AGEP, and cephalosporin-class serious cutaneous/hematologic/renal/hepatic events.

Local pharmacy surveillance checklist

  • Confirm MSSA and beta-lactam susceptibility; avoid cefazolin if MRSA or severe beta-lactam contraindication.
  • Verify first-dose adequacy before renal dose reduction; then reassess maintenance dose daily during AKI or renal recovery.
  • Monitor SCr and urine output; reduce or adjust if renal function worsens, but involve ID/pharmacy for deep infection with high bacterial burden.
  • Track rash, eosinophilia, fever, cytopenias, LFT changes, diarrhea, and neurologic changes during prolonged courses.
  • Check PT/INR if malnourished, hepatic/renal impairment, prolonged therapy, anticoagulants, or unexpected bleeding.
  • For OPAT, coordinate dosing schedule, line compatibility, stability policies, and lab monitoring frequency.

Relevance
10/10

Directly relevant to acute-care and stewardship decisions for MSSA bacteremia, including renal safety and definitive beta-lactam choice.

Lower if the local question is CNS infection, pediatrics, dialysis, or MRSA.

External validity
7/10

Multicountry, 91-site trial with common SAB foci and typical older adult population; ID consultation exceeded 99%.

Lower in settings without reliable SAB bundle execution, different ASP comparators, dialysis populations, or restricted cefazolin access.

Implementation value
9/10

Clear dosing, outcomes, safety direction, and operational rationale for stewardship/order-set changes.

Needs local dosing policy, allergy pathway, renal dosing guidance, and institutional alignment before automatic conversion rules.

Internal validity

The primary outcome is objective and the Bayesian noninferiority rule was prespecified. Attrition was modest at 4.0%, and extreme missing-outcome sensitivity analyses were congruent with the primary result. The open-label design matters less for mortality but more for diagnostic intensity, antibiotic changes, source-control decisions, and adverse-event attribution.

External validity

The trial population resembles many hospitalized adult MSSA bacteremia populations, especially older adults with osteoarticular, skin/soft tissue, catheter, primary, or endocarditis foci. The result is less transportable to hospitals without high SAB-care reliability, to delayed salvage situations, and to populations excluded by design.

Bottom line for practice

Use now? Yes, with conditions. For most adults with confirmed penicillin-resistant MSSA bacteremia and no excluded features, cefazolin should be considered preferred or co-preferred definitive IV therapy, particularly when renal safety, dosing burden, or OPAT feasibility matters. Use institutional ID/stewardship approval for policy changes.
