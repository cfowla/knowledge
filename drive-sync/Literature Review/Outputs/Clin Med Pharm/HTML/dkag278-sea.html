Core thesis. B/F/TAF was highly effective in this treatment-experienced real-world cohort, particularly when participants were already virologically suppressed. Baseline viraemia, prior virological failure, shorter prior suppression, prolonged viraemia, lower CD4 count, and PRM/M184I/V were associated with failure in univariable analyses; however, pre-existing NRTI resistance did not meaningfully erode outcomes in the baseline-suppressed group. Emergent resistance was uncommon.

The most clinically useful distinction is not simply “mutation present versus absent,” but the broader virological context at B/F/TAF initiation. Suppressed participants maintained approximately 96% virological success regardless of NRTI PRM, whereas only 70.0% of baseline-unsuppressed participants achieved pVL <50 copies/mL and success fell to 59.5% in unsuppressed participants with PRM. At 36 months, estimated failure probability was 33.2% for baseline-unsuppressed versus 3.9% for suppressed participants.

Interpretation is constrained by genotype-based selection (636 of 1430 B/F/TAF initiators), lack of measured adherence, incomplete resistance testing at failure, reliance in some suppressed patients on proviral DNA genotyping, univariable rather than clearly multivariable association estimates, and several internal numeric/editorial inconsistencies between prose, tables, and Figure 3. These limitations make the paper useful for contextualizing risk, not for proving that any mutation or baseline factor independently causes B/F/TAF failure.

Clinical question and study framework

PopulationTreatment-experienced adults with HIV-1 in ANRS-CO3-AquiVIH-NA who initiated B/F/TAF from 2018–2021 and had historical RNA or proviral-DNA genotype data.
Exposure / strategyB/F/TAF treatment; stratification by baseline virological suppression and by presence/absence of pre-existing NRTI/INSTI resistance mutations.
ComparisonsBaseline suppressed vs unsuppressed; PRM vs no PRM; M184I/V and other clinical/virological risk factors.
OutcomesVirological success/failure, time-to-failure associations, emergence of new resistance mutations, and post-failure virological course.

Primary study definition of virological failure

Two consecutive HIV-1 pVL values >50 and <1000 copies/mL or one pVL >1000 copies/mL within 60 months. For participants starting B/F/TAF with pVL >50 copies/mL, failure also included failure to obtain pVL <50 copies/mL within the first 12 months. A separate, more sensitive criterion (>50 twice or >200 once) was used for the resistance-at-failure analysis.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction

The paper positions B/F/TAF as a high-barrier, once-daily INSTI-based regimen with strong trial data in treatment-naïve and virologically suppressed populations. Prior clinical-trial and retrospective analyses suggested that historical NRTI mutations, especially M184I/V and limited TAM patterns, often do not compromise suppression after switching; the evidence gap was treatment-experienced, real-world patients with more complex histories and, critically, patients who were viraemic when B/F/TAF was started.

The study therefore asks whether pre-existing resistance and baseline viral load modify real-world virological outcomes and whether new resistance emerges when B/F/TAF fails.

Materials and methods

Adults in a 15-hospital southwestern France cohort were eligible if they initiated B/F/TAF from 2018 through 2021 and had a follow-up viral load, CD4 measurement, and at least one pre-treatment RNA or proviral-DNA genotype. Participants were categorized as suppressed (<50 copies/mL) or unsuppressed (>50 copies/mL) at baseline. Historical resistance was cumulative across available genotypes and interpreted with ANRS algorithm v34; APOBEC-associated findings from hypermutated/stop-codon proviral sequences were excluded. A genotypic susceptibility score summed fully active B/F/TAF components.

Qualitative variables were compared with chi-square/Fisher tests. Aalen–Johansen estimates described failure probability, and Cox proportional hazards models evaluated associated factors. The reported association analysis is described as univariable in the Results/Figure 3, which limits independent causal interpretation of correlated factors such as baseline viraemia, PRM, prior failure, and adherence-related history.

Results — baseline and resistance

Of 1430 B/F/TAF initiators, 636 had genotype data: 526 (82.7%) were suppressed and 110 (17.3%) unsuppressed. Pre-existing NRTI/INSTI mutations occurred in 147/636 (23.1%); M184I/V accounted for 131/147 (89.1%). PRM prevalence was higher in unsuppressed than suppressed participants (33.6% vs 20.9%; P=0.004). Participants with PRM were older and had longer HIV duration, more prior regimens, and more previous virological failures.

The cohort therefore contains substantial confounding by treatment history: PRM is also a marker of more complex prior disease and therapy exposure. No major INSTI mutation was reported at baseline.

Results — effectiveness and factors associated with failure

Overall success was 581/636 (91.4%). Success was 92.8% without PRM and 86.4% with PRM (P=0.0147). Baseline-suppressed participants maintained >95% success regardless of PRM, whereas only 70.0% of baseline-unsuppressed participants achieved pVL <50 copies/mL; success was 59.5% when PRM was present in that unsuppressed group. At 36 months, failure probability was 33.2% in unsuppressed versus 3.9% in suppressed participants and 12.9% with PRM versus 7.2% without PRM.

Figure 3 reports strong univariable associations for baseline pVL >50 copies/mL (HR 9.53, 95% CI 5.54–16.40), previous virological failure (HR 5.83, 95% CI 2.50–13.61), previous INSTI-regimen failure (total-population HR 5.37, 95% CI 3.16–9.13), CD4 <350 cells/mm³ (HR 2.46, 95% CI 1.37–4.42), PRM (HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.10–3.29), and M184I/V (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.16–3.53). Subgroup signals included suppression for <2 years before switch in VS participants (HR 4.08) and viraemia for >1 year before switch in VU participants (HR 2.89).

Results — resistance emergence and post-failure course

Using the more sensitive resistance-analysis failure criterion, 66/636 (10.4%) met failure criteria; complete or partial failure genotypes were available for 52. New resistance was observed in six failures. Four of 31 baseline-suppressed failures developed new RAM (all with M184V; one also N155H), and two of 35 baseline-unsuppressed failures developed new RAM (one M184V without integrase sequence; one with M184V and R263K). The authors contextualized emergent resistance as 0.9% of the full 636-person genotyped cohort.

B/F/TAF was continued in 56/66 (84.8%) after failure, and 40/56 (71.4%) had pVL <50 copies/mL at the last visit. Because continuation was not randomized and adherence was not directly measured, this observation cannot determine which patients should remain on B/F/TAF after failure.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusion

The authors emphasize that suppressed patients did well despite historical NRTI resistance, while unsuppressed patients—especially those with prior failures and PRM—had materially lower success. They interpret emergent resistance as uncommon and suggest adherence may explain some failures with wild-type sequences. The paper argues for close viral-load monitoring rather than automatic avoidance of B/F/TAF solely because historical NRTI mutations are present.

Major stated limitations are exclusion of patients without genotype data, potentially underestimated PRM due to proviral-DNA sensitivity, incomplete genotype sampling at failure, and inability to quantify adherence. In addition, the association analysis does not clearly establish multivariable independence of correlated risk markers. The authors conclude that B/F/TAF achieves high real-world suppression, especially as a switch strategy in already suppressed patients, and that resistance selection at failure is rare.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High practical relevance for interpreting B/F/TAF in treatment-experienced patients with historical resistance, especially because the paper separates baseline-suppressed from baseline-unsuppressed patients. Evidence is useful but observational and not sufficient to establish independent causal effects or dictate regimen changes.
8.5/10
Relevance

Directly addresses real-world B/F/TAF effectiveness, resistance history, failure risk, and emergence of new resistance.

Evidence basis: clinically recognizable strata and outcomes. Limiting factor: HIV-specific scope. Higher with prospective practice-impact validation; lower if the use case is outside HIV pharmacotherapy.
7.0/10
Novelty

Adds long follow-up and an unsuppressed, treatment-experienced subgroup that is underrepresented in switch trials.

Limiting factor: observational design and overlap with existing real-world B/F/TAF literature. Higher with externally validated multivariable risk modeling.
6.0/10
Method strength

Large regional cohort, historical genotype aggregation, explicit failure definitions, time-to-event analysis, and up to 60 months of follow-up.

Limiting factors: retrospective analysis, genotype-selected subset, incomplete failure genotypes, unmeasured adherence, and univariable associations. Higher with prespecified multivariable adjustment and complete resistance/adherence ascertainment.
6.5/10
Evidence strength

Descriptive success and resistance-emergence findings are persuasive for this cohort; effect sizes are large for baseline viraemia and prior failure.

Limiting factor: associations may reflect correlated treatment history/adherence rather than independent effects. Several internal reporting inconsistencies also reduce confidence in exact secondary numbers.
6.0/10
External validity

Fifteen real-world hospitals and complex treatment histories improve applicability beyond tightly selected trials.

Limiting factor: one French region and exclusion of more than half of B/F/TAF initiators lacking qualifying genotype data. Higher with multinational replication and broader genotype availability.
7.5/10
Implementation value

Useful for prioritizing monitoring and contextualizing historical M184I/V when a patient is suppressed at switch.

Limiting factor: no validated decision rule and no causal evidence for post-failure continuation. Higher with prospective action thresholds and current guideline crosswalk.

Strengths

  • Separates suppressed and unsuppressed patients rather than extrapolating switch-trial findings to viraemic patients.
  • Uses cumulative historical genotype data rather than a single baseline test.
  • Reports long follow-up and resistance evolution at failure.
  • Includes patients with complex prior treatment and failure histories, a population often excluded from pivotal trials.

Principal limitations

  • Only 636/1430 B/F/TAF initiators had required genotype data, creating selection risk.
  • Adherence—a plausible major driver of failure—was not measured quantitatively.
  • Failure genotypes were available for only 52 of 66 patients meeting the resistance-analysis failure definition.
  • Some historical resistance ascertainment relied on proviral DNA, which the authors note may miss mutations.
  • Reported predictor analyses are univariable; PRM, baseline viraemia, prior failure, treatment duration, and adherence-related history are correlated.
  • Several numeric/editorial inconsistencies occur between Results prose, Table 2, Figure 1 caption, and Figure 3.

Practice and implementation takeaways

Historical M184I/V is not the whole storyIn baseline-suppressed patients, high success persisted despite NRTI PRM. Historical mutation presence alone should not be treated as equivalent to active viraemia or a failing regimen.
Baseline viraemia is a major warning signalThe largest measured association was baseline pVL >50 copies/mL (HR 9.53), and 36-month failure probability was 33.2% in VU versus 3.9% in VS.
Prior failure history mattersPrior virological failure, especially prior INSTI-regimen failure, marked a high-risk phenotype. Because analyses were univariable, use these as risk markers rather than independent causal estimates.
Resistance emergence was uncommon, not absentSix failures selected new mutations; two described cases acquired INSTI mutations associated with bictegravir resistance.
Post-failure re-suppression occurred frequently40/56 who continued B/F/TAF after failure were suppressed at last follow-up, but the observational continuation decision prevents a treatment recommendation from this result alone.
Monitoring implicationThe authors specifically recommend close HIV RNA monitoring in unsuppressed patients with prior therapeutic failure and PRM. The paper does not define a validated monitoring interval.
Do not overclaim. This study does not demonstrate that B/F/TAF should be continued after virological failure, that M184I/V is independently causal for failure, or that historical genotype can substitute for current adherence and resistance assessment. Current guideline alignment was not independently re-verified in this task.

Source QA, reporting inconsistencies, and extraction boundaries

  • Prior INSTI-regimen failure prevalence: Table 2 reports 105/636 (16.5%), with 44/110 (40.0%) VU and 61/526 (11.6%) VS; Results prose instead states 105/636 (23%), with 62.9% VU and 15.8% VS. These cannot all be correct as written.
  • Previous-VF hazard ratio: Figure 3 reports total HR 5.83 (2.50–13.61), while Results prose gives 5.86 (2.51–13.69).
  • Previous INSTI-VF confidence interval: Results prose contains “93.16–9.13”; Figure 3 displays 3.16–9.13. Figure 3 was used for the structured extraction.
  • Baseline pVL P value: Results prose prints P>0.001 for HR 9.53, while Figure 3 displays P<0.001; the figure value is internally consistent with the effect estimate.
  • Figure 1 caption: the caption text includes “VS + VS,” while the plotted combined group is labeled VU+VS. This appears editorial rather than analytic.
