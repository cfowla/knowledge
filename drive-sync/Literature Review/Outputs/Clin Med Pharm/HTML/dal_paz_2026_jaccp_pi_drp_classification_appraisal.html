3. One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Existing inpatient pharmacist intervention and drug-related problem classification systems usually have some reliability testing, but validity evidence is sparse, inconsistently reported, and methodologically limited. No classification system can be treated as a gold standard from the reviewed evidence.

The review identified 17 studies covering 14 classification systems for pharmacist interventions and/or drug-related problems in hospital inpatient settings. Most studies were European, most did not clearly conceptualize the construct being measured, and most did not disclose a literature review to support item generation. Reliability was commonly reported, especially inter-rater reliability, but evidence based on content, response process, relationships with other variables, internal structure, or consequences of testing was much less complete.

The most practice-relevant conclusion is negative but useful: hospitals should not assume that a PI/DRP classification system is fit for quality measurement, benchmarking, policy, or staffing justification simply because users can apply it consistently. Reliability supports consistent coding; it does not establish that the codes validly represent the clinical-pharmacy construct of interest. For local implementation, the paper functions as a checklist of psychometric requirements: clear construct definition, item-generation rationale, content validity with an adequate expert panel, response-process testing, inter-rater and test-retest reliability, user manual, and setting-specific validation.

High-value use. Use this review when designing or revising an inpatient pharmacist intervention documentation taxonomy, especially for EHR intervention types, clinical pharmacy KPI definitions, research data dictionaries, and pharmacist activity reporting.
Do not overuse. Do not use this review to choose a single best instrument, prove pharmacist clinical outcome impact, or justify direct practice changes without reviewing the underlying instrument and local validation needs.

5. Section condensation

Abstract

The article frames pharmacist interventions as a strategy to reduce drug-related problems and improve clinical, operational, and economic outcomes. The review asks which validated and reliable PI/DRP classification systems exist for inpatient hospital pharmacy and whether the validation methods are sufficiently rigorous.

Seventeen articles and 14 distinct classification systems were included. The authors report frequent reliability assessment but limited validity evidence: 13/17 studies did not report a literature review for item generation, 13/17 did not assess content validity evidence, and many did not report representativeness, confounding control, or evaluator blinding. The conclusion is that no gold-standard inpatient PI/DRP classification system can be identified.

Background

The background distinguishes pharmacist interventions from drug-related problems and explains why classification systems matter: they can enable measurement of clinical, economic, humanistic, epidemiologic, and policy-relevant pharmacy activity if properly developed and validated. The authors emphasize that validity is not an intrinsic property of a tool; it depends on intended interpretation, population, and context.

The rationale for an inpatient-specific review is credible. Inpatient pharmacy has features that differ from community practice, such as intravenous medication use, formulary constraints, provider-managed processes, and multidisciplinary care. The paper also states that standardized and validated PI systems suitable for inpatient practice remain rare, and that no previous systematic review had focused on psychometric properties and validation-method quality for PI classification systems.

Methods

The review followed Cochrane Handbook recommendations, PRISMA 2020 reporting guidance, and OSF registration. The research question used the SPIDER framework: hospital clinical pharmacists, pharmacist intervention classification, psychometric study design, validity/reliability evaluation, and quantitative methods.

Searches covered MEDLINE, Embase, LILACS, Web of Science, Scopus, and gray literature without date or language restrictions. A librarian reviewed the strategy using PRESS. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted data, and assessed quality. The authors used QAVALS for validity-study quality and performed narrative synthesis using a framework organized around modern sources of validity evidence: test content, response process, internal structure, relationship with other variables, and consequences of testing.

Results

The review included 17 studies after screening 1809 titles/abstracts and reviewing full-text reports. Cohen's kappa for reviewer agreement was reported as 1.00. Fourteen PI/DRP classification systems were identified, including Be-CLIPSS, APS-Doc, DokuPIK, DRP-database, GSASA V2, PI-Doc, PCNE 9.1, and unnamed systems from Jordan, Spain, and France. Two studies involved cultural adaptation of PCNE 9.00, and one related to a Conort-linked unnamed instrument.

Key descriptive patterns weaken the field: 13/17 studies were conducted in Europe; only five studies demonstrated the construct concept used for classification-system development; the median number of participants per study was 30.0, and the median number of clinical cases for reliability assessment was 24.0. Four studies reported literature-review-based item generation, seven used existing instruments as a basis, and three did not report the item-generation method. Five instruments had an instruction manual.

Psychometric evidence findings

Content validity was reported in only four studies, and only two reported quantitative CVI results: Alsayed et al. reported CVI 0.92, and Satria et al. reported scale-CVI values between 0.89 and 0.97 by domain. Expert-panel methods were often weak or underreported, including panels smaller than five judges in two studies and absent panel-size reporting in one.

Five studies reported validity evidence based on relationships with other variables, commonly by comparing case coding with a consensus answer or expert-derived reference. Response-process evidence was limited and included questionnaires, cognitive interviews, or response-error analysis. No included study assessed validity evidence related to internal structure or consequences of testing. Reliability assessment was common, appearing in 15/17 studies, with inter-rater reliability the dominant approach.

Quality assessment of validity studies

Using QAVALS, the mean number of criteria met per study was 10, with a range of 7 to 15. Eleven studies satisfied up to 10 criteria. Most studies adequately presented proposed statistical analyses and results, especially for inter-rater reliability, but many did not allow assessment of whether the sample was representative.

Common reporting weaknesses included limited information on confounder control, evaluator blinding, sample selection, and category handling for kappa-based agreement. The review notes that some studies did not specify the kappa coefficient variant used or describe whether response categories were mutually exclusive, which matters for reliability interpretation.

Discussion

The discussion emphasizes that the field is geographically concentrated, heterogeneous, and methodologically immature. The authors argue that terminology for medication-related problems has evolved and remains inconsistent, with DRP, MRP, DTP, and MTP used variably. They also stress that many studies omitted the first step of instrument development: clear construct conceptualization.

The strongest methodological warning is that reliability does not substitute for validity. A tool can produce consistent ratings while still failing to measure the intended construct. The authors recommend more rigorous development and validation methods, including stronger content validity work, response-process evaluation, and context-specific validation for intended clinical use.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that existing PI/DRP classification systems in inpatient practice use varied development and validation methods, making it impossible to identify a gold standard. Most instruments report reliability evidence, but few assess broader validity evidence, and many validation procedures have methodological limitations.

The appropriate takeaway is not that current systems are useless, but that they should be treated as incompletely validated measurement tools. Future work needs a cumulative validation program rather than isolated inter-rater reliability studies.

9. Implementation and practice takeaways

For a hospital pharmacy intervention taxonomyStart with a formal construct definition: what counts as a pharmacist intervention, what counts as a DRP, whether the taxonomy captures cause, action, acceptance, severity, outcome, or value.
For EHR documentationA coding menu should not be treated as validated because it is familiar. Validate whether pharmacists interpret categories consistently and whether categories represent the clinical work being measured.
For research datasetsRecord category definitions, rater training, manual version, inter-rater reliability method, kappa variant, adjudication rules, missing data, and whether categories are mutually exclusive.
For quality/KPI reportingReliability is necessary but insufficient. A KPI based on PI/DRP counts needs content validity and response-process testing before it becomes a staffing or performance metric.
For local adoptionDo not import PCNE, PI-Doc, GSASA, Be-CLIPSS, or another system without mapping it to local scope, medication-use processes, pharmacist services, and documentation burden.
For future validation workUse a larger and explicitly selected expert panel for content validity, pilot cognitive interviews, independent raters, blinding where possible, predefined agreement statistics, and periodic revalidation.

Practical local checklist

Before using PI/DRP codes for reporting or research:
1. Define the construct and intended use.
2. List categories and operational definitions.
3. Document item-generation source: literature review, existing tools, local workflow, expert consensus.
4. Test content validity with an adequate expert panel and report selection criteria.
5. Test response process with real users and clinical scenarios.
6. Train raters with a manual and case examples.
7. Report inter-rater reliability with the exact statistic and category-handling rules.
8. Reconcile ambiguous categories and repeat testing.
9. Separate activity counts from outcome claims unless outcomes were measured.
10. Revalidate when workflow, EHR build, services, or pharmacist scope changes.
