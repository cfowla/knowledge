One-page synthesis

Core thesis: The draft updates the 2012 AKI guideline into a broader AKI–AKD continuum that combines functional and structural markers, risk stratification, multimodal kidney protection and nephrotoxin stewardship, clinically driven RRT decisions, and structured recovery/follow-up care.

The most consequential conceptual change is that AKI is no longer framed only as a creatinine/urine-output event. The draft explicitly incorporates cystatin C and qualified kidney-damage biomarkers, separates transient and persistent AKI, defines AKD and recovery states, and treats recurrence and transition to CKD as longitudinal outcomes. The management chapters similarly move away from isolated “renal” interventions toward bundles: hemodynamic optimization, appropriate fluid choice, medication stewardship, risk-calibrated contrast practice, syndrome-specific treatment, and post-discharge reassessment.

The RRT chapter is operationally strong: timing is anchored to urgent clinical indications and trajectory rather than arbitrary azotemia thresholds; modality depends on hemodynamic/neurologic context and resources; and the draft specifies dose, access, anticoagulation, ultrafiltration, quality metrics, ECLS integration, and pediatric adaptations. The evidence base is broad but not uniformly strong. Many topics are supported by low-to-moderate certainty, and 158 practice points sit outside systematic-review-derived graded recommendations. That distinction is central when translating this draft into protocols or CDS.

Bottom line: Read first for AKI/AKD evidence architecture and anticipated practice changes. Do not operationalize as final policy until KDIGO publishes the finalized guideline.

Section-by-section condensation

Chapter 1. AKI and AKD definition, identification, and classification

Chapter 1 expands the diagnostic framework beyond serum creatinine and urine output. AKI is defined by abnormalities of kidney function or structure within 7 days; the draft adds serum cystatin C and regulatory-qualified kidney-damage biomarkers to the framework, requires a defensible baseline value, and stages creatinine, urine output, and damage-biomarker components separately. It distinguishes transient from persistent AKI at 48 hours, defines AKD over a ≤3-month interval, specifies partial versus complete resolution, and defines recurrent AKI after prior partial or complete resolution.

The chapter includes separate pediatric and neonatal rules. Neonatal criteria incorporate failure of serum creatinine to decline during the first week, neonatal cystatin-C thresholds, and a urine-output threshold different from older children/adults. The practical implication is a more explicitly longitudinal model: diagnosis, severity, trajectory, recovery, recurrence, and transition to AKD are treated as related but nonidentical states.

Chapter 2. AKI prediction, risk stratification, and diagnostic evaluation

Chapter 2 treats AKI prediction and diagnosis as a layered risk-stratification problem rather than a single creatinine trigger. It supports externally validated clinical risk models, specifically recommends risk models for RRT risk to inform shared decision-making, recommends against isolated interruptive creatinine-only alerts without a systematic response strategy, and supports furosemide stress testing and kinetic eGFR in selected settings. Biomarker use is limited to clinically validated and regulatory-approved indications.

For etiologic evaluation, the draft gives urine microscopy a graded role and treats muddy-brown casts/tubular epithelial cells, urine eosinophils, FeNa/FeUrea, NGAL, biopsy, Doppler indices, and point-of-care ultrasound as context-dependent adjuncts rather than universal tests. In children, kidney-health assessment during acute illness and the Renal Angina Index provide a structured risk pathway.

Chapter 3. Prevention and treatment of AKI

Chapter 3 covers prevention and treatment across fluid, hemodynamic, perioperative, syndrome-specific, toxic, nutritional, and pediatric contexts. Core graded recommendations favor crystalloids over colloids and buffered crystalloids over 0.9% saline when traumatic brain injury is absent; suggest intravenous bicarbonate rather than RRT for severe metabolic acidemia (pH <7.20) unless another urgent RRT indication exists; reject low-dose dopamine for kidney protection; and support multicomponent kidney-protection strategies in high-risk surgery.

The chapter separates decongestion from kidney ‘protection’: diuretics are supported for clinically significant volume overload and are preferred before extracorporeal fluid removal when no other RRT indication exists. It also addresses cardiac-surgery amino acids and remote ischemic preconditioning, vasoconstrictors for hepatorenal syndrome, tumor lysis, perinatal asphyxia, nitric oxide, tropical infections/toxins, pregnancy, nutrition, and pediatric hemodynamic/nutritional adaptations.

Chapter 4. Drug- and nephrotoxin-associated AKI

Chapter 4 reframes drug-associated AKI around kidney dysfunction, structural damage, susceptibility, and stewardship. It recommends a multimodal drug/nephrotoxin stewardship approach and gives operational detail on dynamic prescribing, therapeutic drug monitoring, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic interactions, crystalline nephropathies, natural products, pharmacist intervention, biomarker use, and drug dosing during CRRT.

For contrast and medication holds, the draft uses explicit risk-benefit logic. It supports isotonic saline prophylaxis for selected high-risk contrast exposure, does not endorse routine RAS-inhibitor withdrawal solely for contrast, and generally avoids routine perioperative RAS-inhibitor holds. For acute illnesses that predispose to volume depletion, it suggests temporary interruption of selected drugs and separately requires a clear restart plan and documentation—an important transition-of-care safeguard.

Chapter 5. Renal replacement therapy

Chapter 5 is the largest section and treats RRT as a continuum of indications, modality selection, dose, access, anticoagulation, fluid removal, quality, extracorporeal integration, and discontinuation. In adults without urgent indications, it recommends a deferred rather than pre-emptive initiation strategy. Modality is individualized to physiology and context: CRRT or acute PD is favored over conventional intermittent therapy in hemodynamic instability, while neurologic considerations, peritoneal feasibility, resource constraints, and pediatric size all modify the choice.

The chapter standardizes operational details that are often left implicit: CRRT effluent targets, weekly clearance targets for intermittent/prolonged therapy, catheter selection, regional citrate anticoagulation, filter and solution selection, ultrafiltration intensity, quality metrics, ECLS integration, and pediatric RRT. The central logic is repeated reassessment rather than a creatinine/BUN threshold: life-threatening fluid, electrolyte, acid-base, toxin, or other clinical indications determine urgency; trajectories and recovery determine continuation or discontinuation.

Chapter 6. Follow-up care after AKI or AKD

Chapter 6 treats post-AKI/AKD care as a structured phase of kidney care rather than a discharge afterthought. It recommends coordinated personalized follow-up, risk stratification at discharge, medication reconciliation, education, laboratory surveillance, and risk-based specialty involvement. It also addresses restarting prognostically beneficial therapy after AKI and monitoring RAS inhibitors, mineralocorticoid antagonists, and diuretics in alignment with CKD guidance.

A 3-month reassessment of kidney function and markers of kidney damage is a central checkpoint for resolution versus CKD. The chapter provides frameworks for outpatient AKI-RRT follow-up, multidisciplinary monitoring, dialysis recovery surveillance, and pediatric risk-stratified follow-up. It explicitly links AKI, AKD, and CKD as a continuum while preserving their distinct diagnostic time windows.

Methods and evidence development

The guideline update uses an Evidence Review Team and prespecified PICOS questions (Table 63). Searches were conducted in MEDLINE/PubMed and Embase from 2013 through January–June 2025 on a rolling basis; the draft states that an update would occur during public review. Primary-study and existing-systematic-review extraction was performed by one ERT member and confirmed by a second, with third-reviewer adjudication if needed. Risk-of-bias assessments were independently performed by two ERT members. Meta-analysis was used when at least three sufficiently similar studies were available, with random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood modeling and heterogeneity assessment.

GRADE supports outcome-level certainty and recommendation strength. The source distinguishes Level 1 “We recommend” from Level 2 “We suggest,” while Table 67 makes clear that strength is not a mechanical synonym for certainty: benefits/harms, values/preferences, resources, implementation, and exceptional circumstances can justify a strong recommendation despite lower certainty. Two methodological limits matter for appraisal: the process did not systematically review qualitative studies to inform values/preferences, and no formal cost-effectiveness analyses were reviewed.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for evidence review and implementation planning; do not use as final clinical guidance.
The document is methodologically strong and exceptionally implementation-rich, but its explicit public-review status is a hard deployment boundary.

Relevance 10/10

Rationale: The draft covers the full AKI/AKD clinical arc from definition through RRT and post-AKI care.

Evidence basis: Six substantive chapters, 195 labeled recommendations/practice points, 67 main tables, 23 figures, and a 227-page evidence supplement.

Limiting factor: It is a public-review draft, not final clinical guidance.

Would raise/lower: Final publication with stable recommendation wording would preserve the score; major scope contraction would lower it.

Novelty 9/10

Rationale: It is a major update of the 2012 AKI guideline and materially expands AKD, biomarkers, drug stewardship, and follow-up care.

Evidence basis: The source explicitly identifies itself as the 2012 update and adds new clinical questions and new guideline statements.

Limiting factor: Many individual concepts build on existing evidence and other KDIGO/radiology guidance rather than originating de novo.

Would raise/lower: A final change log quantifying retained, revised, and new statements would strengthen the novelty appraisal.

Method strength 9/10

Rationale: The development process is unusually transparent and methodologically structured for a guideline.

Evidence basis: Prespecified PICOS questions, PubMed/Embase searches, dual-confirmed extraction, independent dual risk-of-bias review, meta-analysis rules, GRADE, public review, and a detailed evidence supplement.

Limiting factor: Selected searches were still scheduled for update during public review, and the document is not yet final.

Would raise/lower: Completion of the public-review search update and final amendment log would raise confidence further.

Evidence strength 7/10

Rationale: The evidence base is broad but uneven, with many recommendations relying on moderate, low, or very-low certainty and numerous practice points outside systematic review.

Evidence basis: The supplement includes 140 evidence profiles; 131 state a balance/harms conclusion and overall certainty, ranging from high to low/very low.

Limiting factor: Evidence density and directness vary substantially by question, with pediatric extrapolation and sparse outcomes in several domains.

Would raise/lower: More high-certainty patient-centered RCT evidence and external implementation trials would raise this score.

External validity 8/10

Rationale: The draft is intentionally broad across adult and pediatric populations and multiple care settings.

Evidence basis: The Work Group is multidisciplinary and international; the questions span community, inpatient, ICU, perioperative, contrast, nephrotoxin, RRT, and follow-up settings.

Limiting factor: Resource availability, local regulatory qualification of biomarkers, modality capability, and pediatric extrapolation can limit direct transfer.

Would raise/lower: More explicit resource-stratified implementation evidence and pediatric-specific trials would raise the score.

Implementation value 9/10

Rationale: The source is rich in operational algorithms, thresholds, monitoring tasks, tables, and transition-of-care safeguards.

Evidence basis: RRT algorithms, stewardship tables, contrast-risk pathways, diuretic escalation, follow-up frameworks, and quality/performance measures can map directly to protocols or CDS.

Limiting factor: Its own cover prohibits routine practice use because the text is still a public-review draft.

Would raise/lower: Finalized wording, machine-readable recommendation metadata, and explicit change tracking would make implementation safer.

Main strengths

  • Whole-continuum framing from AKI risk/diagnosis through AKD, RRT, recovery, recurrence, and CKD transition.
  • Transparent methods, PICOS questions, risk-of-bias process, GRADE framework, and a large evidence supplement.
  • Operational content is unusually rich: medication stewardship, RRT prescriptions, anticoagulation, quality metrics, contrast pathways, and follow-up tasks.
  • Adult and pediatric considerations are integrated rather than relegated to a separate document.

Main limitations

  • Public-review draft: recommendation wording and supporting evidence can still change.
  • Many topics have sparse or indirect evidence; practice points are not systematic-review-derived recommendations.
  • Some pediatric guidance relies on adult evidence or context-dependent extrapolation.
  • No systematic qualitative evidence review for values/preferences and no formal cost-effectiveness review.
  • Regulatory qualification and local availability constrain biomarker and RRT implementation.
Interpretive caution: A strong recommendation (Level 1) should not be read as “high-certainty evidence.” Table 67 explicitly allows exceptions. For example, RRT timing can be strongly framed by the totality of clinical consequences even where trial evidence certainty is not high.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Clinical decision support

Represent AKI/AKD as longitudinal states with separate creatinine, urine-output, cystatin-C/damage-marker, trajectory, recovery, and recurrence provenance rather than a single boolean AKI flag.

Medication stewardship

Encode medication risk as dysfunction-versus-damage plus susceptibility, interaction, and exposure duration; every temporary drug hold should have a restart trigger, owner, and documentation pathway.

RRT workflow

Do not implement a creatinine/BUN-only start rule. Capture urgent clinical indications, trajectory, fluid status, hemodynamics, neurologic context, modality feasibility, goals of care, and serial reassessment.

Evidence metadata

Store recommendation strength, evidence certainty, population, setting, exclusions, source version, and draft/final status separately. Do not collapse “Recommendation” and “Practice Point.”

Post-AKI care

Build 3-month kidney-function + kidney-damage reassessment and risk-based follow-up into discharge pathways; include medication reconciliation and kidney-recovery surveillance for AKI-RRT survivors.

Do not overclaim

Supplement evidence profiles are KDIGO’s secondary synthesis. They should not be imported as if KDIGO enrolled the underlying patients or generated the trial results; primary-study atoms require the cited primary publications.
