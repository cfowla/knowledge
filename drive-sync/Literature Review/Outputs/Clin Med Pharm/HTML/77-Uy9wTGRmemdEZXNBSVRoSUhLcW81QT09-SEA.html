One-page synthesis

The authors retrospectively reviewed hospitalized CADRs over eight years and compared dermatology-ward findings with an older same-ward series. They identified 524 CADR cases among 364,636 hospitalizations (1.4 per 1000). Culprit-drug analysis was restricted to 494 cases in which a single medication preceded the skin reaction. The most frequently represented single-drug categories included cephalosporins, allopurinol, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), penicillins, fluoroquinolones, NSAIDs, and carbamazepine.

Compared with the 1984-1994 historical dermatology cohort, the 2003-2010 data showed higher reported percentages for cephalosporin-, fluoroquinolone-, allopurinol-, carbamazepine-, and TCM-associated CADRs, while penicillin and sulfonamide percentages were lower. Clinical-type comparisons showed higher EM-like eruptions, severe CADRs, SJS, and TEN, but lower exanthematous reactions, fixed drug eruption, and exfoliative dermatitis. These are descriptive temporal associations. Changes in prescribing volume, admission thresholds, case mix, surveillance, and culprit attribution can generate the same pattern without a change in drug-specific biological risk.

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best used as a compact historical pharmacovigilance signal and as a pointer to specific culprit-drug categories and severe CADR phenotypes. Do not use it to estimate current drug-specific incidence or comparative risk.

Section condensation

Rationale and objective

The letter frames CADRs as a recurring inpatient safety problem whose apparent culprit-drug profile may shift as new drugs and prescribing patterns change. The stated objective was to assess recent trends in clinical CADR types and causative agents over an eight-year period in Southern China.

Methods

Inpatients diagnosed with CADR from January 2003 through December 2010 were retrospectively analyzed. Dermatologists reviewed reactions and used a WHO CADR definition. ED, SJS, TEN, and DRESS were grouped as severe CADRs; all other reactions were classified as non-severe. Dermatology-department findings were compared with a previously published same-ward cohort of 198 CADR cases from 1984-1994. Stata 10.0 and Fisher exact probability testing were used, with P<0.05 considered significant.

Important method details are not reported: how cases were ascertained across departments, how culprit drugs were adjudicated beyond temporal exposure and dermatologist review, whether repeated admissions were possible, missing-data handling, drug-utilization denominators, or confounder adjustment. These omissions materially limit causal and comparative interpretations.

Results

The study identified 524 CADR cases among 364,636 hospitalizations (1.4/1000). Of the 524 cases, 208 (39.7%) came from dermatology and 316 (60.3%) from other departments. Mean age was 43.85 years (range 1 month-93 years). Culprit-drug analysis focused on 494 single-medication cases.

Allopurinol had a particularly large severe-CADR component in Table 1 (43 severe vs 26 non-severe cases among 69 linked cases), including 22 SJS, 11 ED, 9 TEN, and 1 DRESS. Carbamazepine had 12 severe and 12 non-severe cases among 24. The letter additionally reports 22 carbapenem-associated cases (4.5% of 494) and six biologic-associated cases.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors attribute several temporal shifts to changes in medication use, including increased cephalosporin, fluoroquinolone, allopurinol, and carbamazepine exposure and decreased sulfonamide use. They also suggest that decreased hospitalization of milder exanthematous reactions and fixed drug eruptions could contribute to lower observed percentages. These are plausible hypotheses offered by the authors but are not tested with medication-utilization denominators or adjusted models.

The paper concludes that CADRs should not be overlooked in hospitals and highlights increased proportions of allopurinol-, carbamazepine-, cephalosporin-, and TCM-associated CADRs, along with higher EM-like eruptions, SJS, and TEN. TCM attribution is specifically limited because the causative ingredient could not be identified from complex formulations.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 6/10

Useful for historical hospital pharmacovigilance and culprit-drug pattern recognition, but dated and setting-specific.

Evidence basis
Large hospital denominator and clinically recognizable severe CADR categories.
Principal limiting factor
The 2003-2010 prescribing environment and Southern China setting limit present-day relevance.
What would raise/lower the score
Current multicenter data with contemporary prescribing patterns would raise relevance.

Novelty 4/10

The paper documents local temporal shifts rather than introducing a new method or causal mechanism.

Evidence basis
Direct comparison with a 1984-1994 same-ward cohort provides a historical trend signal.
Principal limiting factor
The design is descriptive and the source is a short letter.
What would raise/lower the score
More rigorous longitudinal exposure-adjusted analyses would raise novelty.

Method strength 4/10

The retrospective single-center design and sparse causality methods constrain inference.

Evidence basis
Dermatologist review, a stated CADR definition, a large hospitalization denominator, and Fisher exact testing are reported.
Principal limiting factor
No formal causality algorithm, exposure denominators, multivariable adjustment, or detailed missing-data/ascertainment methods are described.
What would raise/lower the score
Prospective surveillance with prespecified causality assessment and exposure denominators would raise the score.

Evidence strength 5/10

The counts and temporal differences are clear, but they establish case proportions more strongly than drug-specific risk.

Evidence basis
524 CADR cases among 364,636 hospitalizations; three quantitative tables; p values for historical comparisons.
Principal limiting factor
Temporal changes may reflect prescribing volume, hospitalization threshold, case mix, or ascertainment rather than intrinsic drug risk.
What would raise/lower the score
Drug utilization denominators and adjusted analyses would raise evidence strength.

External validity 3/10

Generalizability is limited by one hospital, one region, and an older treatment era.

Evidence basis
The cohort includes multiple hospital departments and a broad age range.
Principal limiting factor
Single-center Southern China data from 2003-2010 may not transfer to other systems or current practice.
What would raise/lower the score
Contemporary multicenter replication across regions and settings would raise external validity.

Implementation value 4/10

The article supports awareness and pharmacovigilance prioritization, but not current drug-selection or prevention policies.

Evidence basis
Signals identify allopurinol, carbamazepine, antibiotics, TCM, carbapenems, and biologics as relevant culprit categories in the source setting.
Principal limiting factor
No exposure-adjusted incidence, predictive model, prevention strategy, monitoring protocol, or intervention effect is evaluated.
What would raise/lower the score
Validated contemporary risk estimates and actionable prevention workflows would raise implementation value.

Strengths

  • Large hospital denominator and broad age range.
  • Dermatologist review and explicit severe-CADR classification.
  • Three compact quantitative tables with counts, percentages, and p values.
  • Historical same-ward comparator gives a useful local trend signal.

Main limitations

  • Retrospective, single-center design with sparse ascertainment and causality methods.
  • No number-exposed denominator for individual drugs; case proportions cannot be interpreted as drug-specific incidence or relative risk.
  • Historical comparison is vulnerable to secular changes in prescribing, admissions, referral patterns, coding, case mix, and diagnostic recognition.
  • No multivariable adjustment or sensitivity analyses are described.
  • TCM formulations are heterogeneous and the causative ingredient was not identifiable.
  • The data are from 2003-2010 and should not be assumed to represent current drug-use patterns.
Practice boundary: This paper is insufficient by itself to rank present-day medications by CADR risk, choose among therapies, or define monitoring/prevention protocols.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Historical signal extraction, adverse-event taxonomy, and identifying drug categories worth following into stronger or newer evidence.
Do not overclaim
Counts among CADR cases are not incidence among exposed patients; temporal differences are not proof that drug-specific risk changed.
Read next
Prior same-ward comparator, prospective hospital survey, Northern China epidemiology, and current exposure-adjusted pharmacovigilance studies.

Bottom line: The source is concise and quantitatively useful for historical pharmacovigilance, but its strongest supported inference is descriptive: the mix of recognized culprit drugs and CADR phenotypes differed between the two local eras.
