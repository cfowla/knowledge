One-page synthesis

Verdict: Do not use for practice as stand-alone comparative evidence. Best use: a historical evidence map and a pointer to direct comparative cohorts. The review found no statistically significant difference between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin for MI, HF, cardiovascular mortality, or stroke, but the observational evidence and wide confidence intervals do not establish equivalence.

The authors searched PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL through 1 July 2022 and included four retrospective comparative studies. They pooled raw endpoint tallies with a Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect model. Across 25,715 patients, the pooled estimates were MI RR 0.81 (95% CI 0.60-1.09), HF RR 0.76 (0.56-1.04), cardiovascular mortality RR 0.46 (0.18-1.20), and stroke RR 1.07 (0.84-1.38); all reported I² values were 0%.

The clinically important limitation is interpretive: failure to reach statistical significance is not evidence of therapeutic equivalence. The component studies were nonrandomized, doses were not reported, the review did not assess publication bias, and the mortality analysis had very few events. The paper is therefore useful for locating comparative datasets and framing uncertainty, not for concluding that the two drugs are interchangeable for cardiovascular outcomes.

Section condensation

Abstract / introduction

The paper frames SGLT2 inhibitors as a cardiometabolic drug class with established cardiovascular benefit but asks whether dapagliflozin and empagliflozin differ from one another. The stated objective is a direct comparison of cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes.

The introduction discusses pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences as possible reasons for differential effects. Those mechanistic claims are background rationale rather than evidence generated by this meta-analysis.

Methodology

Two reviewers independently searched PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL through 1 July 2022 without language or publication-date restrictions. Eligible studies directly compared dapagliflozin with empagliflozin in type 2 diabetes and reported cardiovascular death, stroke, MI, or HF; placebo/other-SGLT2 comparisons were excluded. Endpoint tallies were independently extracted.

The authors used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and an adapted eight-point maximum because all included studies were retrospective. They pooled relative risks with a Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect model, used 95% CIs and p<0.05, and assessed heterogeneity with I². Publication bias was not assessed because only four studies were included.

Results

Of 288 identified records, 22 reached full-text review and four retrospective studies entered the meta-analysis. Total sample size was 25,715, and each study had mean follow-up longer than two years. The authors rated all four studies as high quality under their adapted NOS scoring.

No pooled endpoint was statistically significant. MI: RR 0.81 (0.60-1.09). HF: 0.76 (0.56-1.04). Cardiovascular mortality: 0.46 (0.18-1.20). Stroke: 1.07 (0.84-1.38). I² was 0% for each. Endpoint-specific denominators differ because mortality and stroke used only three studies.

Discussion / limitations

The authors interpret the findings as showing no detected cardiovascular-outcome difference between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin and discuss mechanistic hypotheses that might favor dapagliflozin for HF. Those mechanistic passages are not supported by a statistically significant pooled HF difference in this meta-analysis.

Stated limitations are the small number of studies, entirely retrospective designs, inability to assess publication bias, and absent dosing information. Additional appraisal concerns are residual confounding, inclusion of immature evidence sources, limited outcome harmonization, and the absence of an equivalence/noninferiority framework.

Conclusions / disclosures

The authors conclude that stroke, HF, MI, and cardiac-related death did not differ significantly between the two drug groups and call for prospective studies. A stronger interpretation is that this analysis did not detect a statistically significant difference; it did not prove that outcomes are equal.

The authors declared no financial support, no relevant financial relationships, and no other relationships or activities that could appear to have influenced the work.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 8/10

Directly addresses dapagliflozin-versus-empagliflozin cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes.

Evidence basis: Four direct comparative cohorts and four cardiovascular endpoints.

Principal limiting factor: The question is clinically relevant, but the evidence is dated and does not address many current cardiorenal use cases.

What would change the score: A current prospective head-to-head study or a stronger contemporary comparative-effectiveness synthesis would raise the score.

Novelty 6/10

At publication, the authors presented this as the first meta-analysis spanning several cardiovascular outcomes for this direct drug comparison.

Evidence basis: Direct pooled comparison of dapagliflozin and empagliflozin across MI, HF, cardiovascular death, and stroke.

Principal limiting factor: Only four retrospective datasets were available, including a preprint and a conference abstract.

What would change the score: More mature head-to-head evidence and preplanned subgroup analyses would raise the score.

Method strength 4/10

The review used explicit eligibility criteria, duplicate review, PRISMA reporting, and structured pooling, but the pooled evidence was entirely retrospective.

Evidence basis: Search across three databases; independent reviewers; Newcastle-Ottawa assessment; Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect pooling.

Principal limiting factor: No randomized studies, no publication-bias assessment, no dose information, no reported certainty framework, and no clear pooling of adjusted causal estimates.

What would change the score: Prospective comparative designs, adjusted-effect synthesis, protocol registration, certainty grading, and sensitivity analyses would raise the score.

Evidence strength 4/10

The pooled estimates are precise enough to rule out some large effects for MI and stroke, but non-significance does not establish equivalence and several confidence intervals remain wide.

Evidence basis: MI RR 0.81 (0.60-1.09); HF 0.76 (0.56-1.04); CV mortality 0.46 (0.18-1.20); stroke 1.07 (0.84-1.38).

Principal limiting factor: Residual confounding, low event counts for cardiovascular mortality, observational treatment selection, and unadjusted event-tally pooling limit causal interpretation.

What would change the score: Randomized or high-quality emulated-target-trial evidence with narrow equivalence/noninferiority bounds would raise the score.

External validity 5/10

The cohorts were large and had follow-up beyond two years, but applicability depends on the healthcare systems, prescribing eras, patient selection, and doses used in the component studies.

Evidence basis: 25,715 patients overall across four retrospective comparative cohorts.

Principal limiting factor: Doses were not reported and clinical phenotype details were heterogeneous and incompletely harmonized.

What would change the score: Broader multinational cohorts with treatment-dose, kidney-function, HF, ASCVD, and demographic stratification would raise the score.

Implementation value 4/10

The article can inform a formulary evidence map, but it should not determine drug selection by itself.

Evidence basis: Direct comparative estimates are easy to reuse and the cited studies identify primary datasets for deeper review.

Principal limiting factor: The study cannot establish therapeutic equivalence and does not evaluate formulary logistics, current indications, dosing, safety, renal thresholds, or cost.

What would change the score: Integration with current guidelines, labels, head-to-head cohorts, renal/HF subgroup evidence, safety, and economics would raise the score.

Critical interpretation

Non-significance is not equivalence. The authors' wording that risks “are not different” is stronger than the analysis supports. The CIs permit clinically meaningful differences, especially for HF and cardiovascular mortality.
Observational pooling. All component studies were retrospective. Pooling event counts cannot remove confounding by indication, channeling, baseline cardiovascular/renal risk, dose, adherence, or prescribing context.
Risk-of-bias labeling should be read cautiously. The authors classify all studies as high quality using an adapted Newcastle-Ottawa threshold, but this does not eliminate the intrinsic confounding limitations of retrospective comparative effectiveness studies.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use this paper for
Locating direct comparative cohorts; understanding the 2022 evidence landscape; extracting pooled historical estimates.
Do not use this paper for
Declaring dapagliflozin and empagliflozin cardiovascularly equivalent; choosing a formulary agent without current guideline, label, renal/HF, safety, operational, and economic evidence.
Most useful next reading
The four component comparative studies, especially mature peer-reviewed cohorts, plus later prospective or well-controlled comparative-effectiveness evidence.
Bottom line: the 2022 meta-analysis did not detect statistically significant differences across four cardiovascular endpoints, but its design is too weak to establish interchangeability or causal superiority.
