One-page synthesis

Core thesis: At first recurrence, lomustine served as the best-connected chemotherapy benchmark. Bevacizumab + lomustine improved PFS but probably did not improve OS and increased severe adverse events. Regorafenib and ABT-414 + temozolomide showed favorable OS signals versus lomustine, but the evidence was limited and not broadly confirmatory. Beyond first recurrence, comparative evidence was too sparse for a defensible network meta-analysis.

What the review found

The review included 42 studies (34 randomized and 8 non-randomized; 5,236 participants). For first recurrence, the authors constructed separate networks for overall survival, progression-free survival, and severe adverse events. The OS network connected 11 interventions and the PFS network eight. No comparable network could be constructed for re-operation, reirradiation, PCV, temozolomide rechallenge, best supportive care, or second/later recurrence because usable comparative data were insufficient.

The clearest efficacy/toxicity trade-off was bevacizumab + lomustine: PFS improved versus lomustine (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.44–0.74), but OS did not (HR 0.91, 0.75–1.10), while grade ≥3 adverse events increased (RR 2.51, 1.72–3.66). Regorafenib showed an OS signal versus lomustine (HR 0.50, 0.33–0.76), but certainty was low. QoL evidence came from only seven studies and was weakened by sparse reporting and high attrition.

What it means

The review is most useful as a rigorous map of the pre-2020 salvage evidence and its gaps, not as a current ranking of therapies. The network rankings are constrained by single-study nodes, open-label designs, sparse direct comparisons, and heterogeneous recurrence populations. The authors’ practical conclusion favored lomustine as the best-supported chemotherapy benchmark among the options evaluated, while acknowledging that re-operation and reirradiation may be appropriate in selected patients and that evidence after a second recurrence was especially weak.

For current use, the historical regorafenib signal requires particular caution because later evidence did not confirm benefit. Contemporary guidance continues to emphasize clinical-trial participation and individualized selection rather than a universally superior recurrent-GBM regimen.

Methods and validity

Eligibility and outcomes

Eligible populations were adults aged ≥16 years with recurrent or progressive glioblastoma after prior surgery and chemoradiotherapy; mixed cohorts were eligible when at least 80% met the review’s post-Stupp criteria. Eligible comparative designs included RCTs, quasi-randomized studies, non-randomized controlled studies, and controlled before-after studies with concurrent control and at least 20 participants. Crossover, case-control, uncontrolled, and dose-finding studies were excluded. Any active treatment could be compared with another active treatment, best supportive care, or no treatment.

Primary outcomes were overall survival and quality of life; secondary outcomes included progression-free survival and severe adverse events (grade ≥3). Time-to-event outcomes were summarized with hazard ratios, dichotomous outcomes with risk ratios, and continuous outcomes with mean differences when appropriate.

Search, extraction, and synthesis

The authors searched CENTRAL (Issue 12, 2019), MEDLINE (2005 to December week 1, 2019), and Embase (2005 to week 50, 2019) without language restriction, supplemented by trial registries, reference lists, and conference abstracts. Screening and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. Risk of bias used the Cochrane tool for RCTs and ROBINS-I for non-randomized evidence.

Random-effects inverse-variance pairwise methods and multivariate network meta-analysis were implemented in Stata 15, with separate treatment networks and assessment of heterogeneity/consistency. Certainty was graded with GRADE. Funnel-plot/publication-bias assessment was not performed because too few sufficiently similar studies were available.

Quality of life and later recurrence

Quality-of-life outcomes were reported in only seven studies, often with substantial attrition, preventing a reliable comparative synthesis. For second or later recurrence, the evidence base was too sparse and disconnected for network meta-analysis. A randomized comparison of tumor-treating fields versus physician’s choice chemotherapy showed little difference in OS or PFS, while evidence for best supportive care and other common salvage strategies remained inadequate.

The review found no post-Stupp randomized evidence that could establish PCV versus lomustine or standalone temozolomide rechallenge as superior strategies. Re-operation and reirradiation were supported mainly by selected non-randomized cohorts or limited comparative evidence, making treatment-selection bias a major concern.

Economic evidence

The brief economic commentary found insufficient robust evidence to establish a confident cost-effectiveness hierarchy for recurrent glioblastoma treatments. The authors did not present cost-effectiveness as a basis for choosing one salvage therapy over another.

Discussion and authors’ conclusions

Source claim

The authors concluded that lomustine monotherapy appeared to be the best-supported chemotherapy among treatments evaluated for first recurrence. Adding bevacizumab to lomustine probably did not improve OS and increased severe toxicity despite PFS improvement. Re-operation, with or without reirradiation or chemotherapy, could be considered for selected patients, but the evidence was vulnerable to selection bias. The role of treatment at second recurrence remained uncertain.

They emphasized the need for better-powered randomized trials, clinically meaningful outcomes including quality of life, and more evidence on combinations and sequencing. The review did not support confident cost-effectiveness conclusions.

Independent appraisal

The conclusions generally track the evidence the review assembled and are appropriately cautious about sparse later-line data. The main weakness is not review execution but the underlying evidence network: disconnected interventions, heterogeneous populations, open-label trials, single-study nodes, limited patient-reported outcomes, and recurrent-disease treatment-selection effects.

Network ranking should therefore not be read as a treatment hierarchy independent of certainty. A low-certainty favorable HR from one phase II trial is not interchangeable with replicated high-certainty OS benefit, and a PFS effect under VEGF inhibition is especially vulnerable to imaging-response artifacts.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High-value historical evidence map and methodologically strong review, but not current enough to function as a stand-alone 2026 practice guide.

External currency verification — not source-derived

This section is external verification, not evidence reported by CD013579. It is included because the SEA protocol requires recency checking for clinical-practice use.
  • ASCO–SNO guideline rapid update (J Clin Oncol; DOI 10.1200/JCO-25-00250): for recurrent glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4, the panel makes no recommendation for or against a specific therapeutic strategy because evidence quality is low, and strongly recommends clinical-trial referral where possible.
  • SNO–EANO 2025 consensus review (Neuro-Oncology; DOI 10.1093/neuonc/noaf177): the phase II REGOMA regorafenib signal was not confirmed in GBM AGILE; the review states that regorafenib would no longer be listed by NCCN as an option for recurrent glioblastoma.
  • EANS–EANO extent-of-resection guideline (Neuro-Oncology 2026; DOI 10.1093/neuonc/noaf217): recommendations for resection in recurrent glioblastoma are level C, consistent with selective rather than universally applicable use.

These external updates materially reinforce the main appraisal point: CD013579 remains useful for evidence history and methods, but positive low-certainty nodes should not be translated directly into present-day treatment preference.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Preserve the OS/PFS distinctionDo not infer survival benefit from bevacizumab-associated PFS improvement; the review found PFS benefit for BEV + lomustine without OS benefit.
2. Treat toxicity as decision-grade evidenceSeveral combination regimens increased grade ≥3 adverse events substantially; benefits must be interpreted jointly with toxicity.
3. Do not over-rank sparse network nodesSingle-trial, low-certainty favorable HRs are hypotheses for practice, not durable superiority claims.
4. Later recurrence is an evidence gapThe review could not construct a later-line network; individualized goals and trial availability remain central.
5. Re-operation is selection-sensitivePotential benefit is concentrated in carefully selected patients and is difficult to separate from prognostic selection in non-randomized data.
6. Use current molecular classificationModern glioblastoma is restricted to IDH-wildtype tumors; older mixed historical populations reduce direct applicability.
