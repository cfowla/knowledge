One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first for an empagliflozin vs dapagliflozin formulary evidence review. The study gives a large, well-specified observational head-to-head comparison of kidney outcomes. The overall estimates do not show a kidney-effectiveness advantage for either drug. It is useful evidence against choosing between these two agents solely on expected long-term kidney effectiveness in treated type 2 diabetes. It is not enough by itself to justify unrestricted therapeutic interchangeability.

The investigators emulated a pragmatic target trial using Danish nationwide health data. They identified new users of empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, controlled measured baseline confounding with inverse probability of treatment weighting, modeled death as a competing event, and estimated intention-to-treat and per-protocol effects. Outcomes were laboratory-defined AKI, incident CKD G3-G5, incident albuminuric CKD A2/A3, and at least 40% eGFR decline among people eligible for each endpoint.

The primary adjusted 6-year risk ratios were 0.98 for AKI, 0.97 for incident CKD G3-G5, 1.04 for CKD A2/A3, and 0.94 for CKD progression. The first three confidence intervals are reasonably informative around no clinically large difference. CKD progression is much less certain, with a 95% CI from 0.56 to 1.58 in the intention-to-treat analysis and 0.05 to 2.94 per protocol. Subgroup estimates were broadly compatible with the overall conclusion, but isolated subgroup contrasts should not be read as proof of effect modification without interaction testing and multiplicity control.

Main use: comparative renal-effectiveness evidence for empagliflozin vs dapagliflozin. Do not use it alone for: safety equivalence, heart-failure effectiveness, current labeled-indication equivalence, inpatient initiation policy, dosing substitutions, or cost equivalence.

Methods and validity

Target-trial emulation

The emulation used a prospective new-user, active-comparator cohort. Eligibility required treated type 2 diabetes, age at least 18 years, no previous SGLT2 inhibitor dispensing, no preexisting kidney failure, and routine baseline creatinine ascertainment. First-ever empagliflozin or dapagliflozin dispensing assigned exposure. The design is stronger than a prevalent-user comparison because it aligns treatment initiation and follow-up, but treatment was still chosen in practice rather than randomized.

Outcomes and analysis

AKI was defined from creatinine changes. Incident CKD G3-G5 and A2/A3 and CKD progression used repeated laboratory abnormalities. The analysis used Aalen-Johansen estimators with death as a competing event, baseline IPTW, multiple imputation with 10 datasets, 200 bootstrap samples for confidence intervals, and time-varying censoring weights for the per-protocol analysis. Supplement eFigures 2-5 show reported covariate balance after weighting, and the main text states all measured covariates were well balanced.

Residual bias remains plausible. The main limitation is nonrandom treatment assignment. Calendar time, indication channeling, unmeasured severity, clinician preference, and missing laboratory data can still bias a near-null result even when measured covariates balance well.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 9/10

Directly compares empagliflozin and dapagliflozin on kidney outcomes in treated type 2 diabetes.

Evidence basis: Nationwide active-comparator target-trial emulation with four kidney outcome families.

Limiting factor: The study does not address all formulary domains such as safety, inpatient use, indications, or cost.

What would change it: A randomized direct comparison or broader comparative-effectiveness packet covering safety and current indication groups would raise the score.

Novelty 8/10

Large head-to-head kidney-effectiveness evidence is uncommon and complements the same group’s cardiovascular comparison.

Evidence basis: 50,283 new users with laboratory-defined kidney outcomes and target-trial emulation.

Limiting factor: The question is comparative rather than a new therapeutic mechanism, and prior smaller/observational comparisons existed.

What would change it: A direct randomized comparison or an analysis of additional SGLT2 inhibitors would raise the score.

Method strength 8/10

The design uses a clear target-trial framework, new-user active-comparator sampling, IPTW, competing-risk methods, per-protocol analysis, and multiple imputation.

Evidence basis: Main Methods plus Supplement eTables 1, 4, and 5 and eFigures 2-5.

Limiting factor: Treatment was not randomized, residual confounding remains possible, and several source-document inconsistencies require caution.

What would change it: Random treatment allocation, stronger negative-control/sensitivity analyses, and fully consistent outcome documentation would raise the score.

Evidence strength 7/10

Overall adjusted estimates are tightly centered near no difference for AKI and incident CKD, with consistent intention-to-treat and per-protocol findings.

Evidence basis: Figure 2 plus eTable 8 subgroup analyses.

Limiting factor: CKD progression is imprecise; observational treatment assignment and multiple subgroup comparisons limit certainty.

What would change it: A sufficiently powered randomized head-to-head trial with adjudicated kidney outcomes would raise the score.

External validity 7/10

Nationwide population-based Danish data improve representativeness within the Danish treated type 2 diabetes population.

Evidence basis: Nationwide registries and routine outpatient/general-practice laboratory data.

Limiting factor: Ethnic composition, health-system structure, reimbursement, prescribing rules, and the 2014-2020 treatment era may not generalize to other settings.

What would change it: Replication in diverse health systems and contemporary indication groups would raise the score.

Implementation value 8/10

The paper directly informs whether kidney-effectiveness evidence should favor one of these two SGLT2 inhibitors in a formulary review.

Evidence basis: Near-null comparative kidney estimates across four outcomes and prespecified subgroups.

Limiting factor: It cannot alone justify full therapeutic interchangeability because it omits comparative safety, current labeled indications, inpatient initiation, operational issues, and cost.

What would change it: A synthesis with cardiovascular, heart-failure, CKD, safety, regulatory, inpatient, and cost evidence would raise the score.

Bottom line

The paper is persuasive evidence that large kidney-effectiveness differences between empagliflozin and dapagliflozin are unlikely for AKI and incident CKD in the studied population. It is weaker for CKD progression, where event counts and confidence intervals leave substantial uncertainty. The observational design also means "no difference detected" should not be converted into pharmacologic or regulatory equivalence.

Implementation and practice takeaways

  • For a formulary review, this study argues against selecting empagliflozin or dapagliflozin solely because one is expected to produce better long-term kidney outcomes in treated type 2 diabetes.
  • Keep CKD progression separate from the other endpoints. Its estimate is too imprecise for a strong no-difference conclusion.
  • Do not extend the kidney comparison to safety, inpatient use, heart-failure outcomes, drug-specific indications, or dosing policy without separate evidence.
  • Use the companion 2024 Circulation target-trial emulation for cardiovascular comparison and the reference queue for primary RCT, guideline, and direct-comparison follow-up.
