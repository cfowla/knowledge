One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a broad NSTE-ACS population receiving aspirin, adding clopidogrel produced an early and sustained reduction in ischemic events, driven largely by fewer myocardial infarctions and in-hospital ischemic events, at the cost of more major and minor bleeding.

The first primary composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or stroke occurred in 9.3% with clopidogrel versus 11.4% with placebo (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.72-0.90; P<0.001). The second primary composite, adding refractory ischemia, was 16.5% versus 18.8% (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.79-0.94; P<0.001). The clearest component effect was myocardial infarction: 5.2% versus 6.7% (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.67-0.89). Benefit was detectable within 24 hours and persisted beyond 30 days.

The principal tradeoff was bleeding. Major bleeding increased from 2.7% to 3.7% (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.13-1.67; P=0.001), while life-threatening bleeding was 2.2% versus 1.8% (RR 1.21, 95% CI 0.95-1.56; P=0.13). A later correction changed the overall CABG major-bleeding rates to 8.3% versus 6.6%, replacing 1.3% versus 1.1% in the original print. Model-calculated from reported rates, the first primary endpoint had an absolute reduction of 2.1% (NNT ≈ 48 over mean 9 months), while major bleeding had an absolute increase of 1.0% (NNH ≈ 100). These NNT/NNH values were not reported by the authors.

Best use: foundational evidence for the benefit-harm concept of adding a P2Y12 inhibitor to aspirin in NSTE-ACS. Do not use alone for: selecting a contemporary P2Y12 agent, current duration, peri-procedural management, or modern invasive-strategy decisions.

Methods and clinical question

PICO

PPatients hospitalized within 24 hours of symptoms with acute coronary syndromes without ST-segment elevation. After the first 3000 patients, enrollment required ECG changes or elevated cardiac enzymes/markers. Major exclusions included high bleeding risk, severe heart failure, oral anticoagulation, recent revascularization, and recent IV glycoprotein IIb/IIIa therapy.
IClopidogrel 300 mg orally immediately, then 75 mg daily for 3-12 months, with aspirin 75-325 mg daily.
CMatching placebo with aspirin 75-325 mg daily.
OPrimary 1: CV death/nonfatal MI/stroke. Primary 2: primary 1 + refractory ischemia. Secondary: severe ischemia, heart failure, revascularization. Safety: life-threatening, major, and minor bleeding.

Design and conduct

Allocation used a central computerized 24-hour service with permuted blocks stratified by center. The trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled. Primary outcomes and life-threatening/major bleeds were adjudicated by reviewers unaware of treatment assignment. Analyses were intention-to-treat using log-rank or chi-square methods; subgroup interactions used Cox regression.

The planned sample increased from 9000 to 12,500 because event rates were lower than expected. The independent data and safety monitoring board used a modified Haybittle-Peto boundary. Although the efficacy boundary was crossed by the second formal interim assessment, the board recommended continuation to the planned end to better define bleeding risk. Vital status was known for 99.9% of randomized patients.

Clinical context inside the trial

The trial did not prohibit subsequent angiography or revascularization. During follow-up, 43.7% underwent angiography, 16.5% CABG, and 21.2% PTCA; study medication was commonly interrupted around procedures and open-label thienopyridines could be used. This makes the randomized comparison pragmatic but also tends to dilute between-group contrast.

Timing

The first primary outcome was lower both within 30 days (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.67-0.92) and after 30 days through study end (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.70-0.95). By 24 hours, the broader composite of CV death, MI, stroke, refractory ischemia, or severe ischemia was 1.4% versus 2.1% (RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.51-0.86), supporting a rapid loading-dose effect.

Subgroups

Figure 4 showed generally consistent benefit across sex, age, ST-segment deviation, enzyme status, diabetes, risk category, and revascularization after randomization. A larger apparent effect occurred in patients with prior revascularization (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.43-0.72) than in those without it (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.78-0.99; interaction P=0.002), but the authors explicitly cautioned against overinterpretation because many subgroup analyses were performed.

CABG correction: the later correction states that overall post-CABG major bleeding was 8.3% vs 6.6%, not 1.3% vs 1.1% as originally printed. The original relative-risk estimate was 1.26 (95% CI 0.93-1.71). In the subgroup stopping study medication within 5 days before CABG, major bleeding was 9.6% vs 6.3% (RR 1.53; P=0.06).

Major bleeding excess was present both within 30 days (2.0% vs 1.5%; RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.01-1.70) and after 30 days (1.7% vs 1.1%; RR 1.48, 95% CI 1.10-1.99). Fatal bleeding, hemorrhagic stroke, and bleeding requiring surgery were not increased in the main safety analysis. Thrombocytopenia and neutropenia counts were similar between groups.

Temporary discontinuation for more than five days occurred in 46.2% vs 45.4%; permanent discontinuation in 21.1% vs 18.8%. Aspirin use remained high. The authors noted that temporary interruption before procedures likely mitigated bleeding risk and simultaneously reduced the observed treatment contrast.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a large, multinational, blinded randomized trial with clinically important ischemic and bleeding outcomes and remains highly useful for understanding the foundational benefit-risk evidence behind dual antiplatelet therapy in NSTE-ACS. Its direct translation to current practice requires contemporary guideline context.
Relevance
9/10
Directly addresses antiplatelet pharmacotherapy in ACS. Evidence basis: efficacy and safety outcomes in 12,562 patients. Limit: historical treatment era. Would rise: if paired with contemporary comparative trials/guidelines.
Novelty
10/10
At publication, tested prolonged clopidogrel + aspirin in a broad NSTE-ACS population. Evidence basis: early and long-term randomized exposure. Limit: novelty is historical, not current. Would fall: when judged only against present-day knowledge.
Method strength
9/10
Central randomization, double blinding, placebo control, blinded adjudication, ITT analysis, independent DSMB, and 99.9% vital-status ascertainment. Limit: protocol eligibility refinement, frequent treatment interruption/crossover, and practice pattern centered away from routine early invasive management. Would rise: stricter treatment separation and uniform contemporary invasive strategy.
Evidence strength
9/10
Large sample, prespecified composites, precise primary estimates, coherent MI/ischemia effects, and explicit bleeding tradeoff. Limit: some components are neutral and subgroup findings are multiplicity-prone. Would rise: replication under contemporary care and explicit net-clinical-benefit endpoints.
External validity
8/10
482 centers in 28 countries support broad geographic generalizability. Limit: early-2000s ACS care, no routine early invasive policy at many centers, and exclusions for high bleeding risk/oral anticoagulation. Would rise: contemporary populations including modern invasive practice and higher-risk comorbidity profiles.
Implementation value
8/10
Clear regimen, outcome definitions, timing, and safety signals support historical protocol understanding. Limit: the trial does not answer current agent selection or current DAPT duration. Would rise: integration with current guidelines and head-to-head P2Y12 evidence.

Main strengths

  • Large multinational randomized sample with excellent follow-up.
  • Double-blind placebo control and blinded clinical-event adjudication.
  • Benefits seen in both early and later periods, with component coherence centered on MI/ischemia.
  • Safety captured in clinically interpretable bleeding categories.
  • Authors acknowledged subgroup multiplicity and procedural treatment interruptions.

Main limitations

  • Historical treatment environment limits direct translation to modern NSTE-ACS pathways.
  • Many centers did not use a routine early invasive strategy.
  • Randomized therapy was often interrupted around revascularization and open-label thienopyridines could be used, diluting treatment separation.
  • Enrollment criteria were tightened after the first 3000 participants, introducing some population heterogeneity.
  • Industry sponsorship and investigator financial relationships are relevant to bias appraisal, although data management/analysis were conducted by the academic coordinating office and an independent DSMB monitored outcomes.
  • A published correction materially changed the printed overall CABG bleeding percentages, underscoring the need to use corrected source values.

Practice and implementation takeaways

What the trial establishesAdding clopidogrel to aspirin in NSTE-ACS reduces ischemic events, especially MI and recurrent/refractory ischemia, with benefit beginning early and persisting over months.
What must accompany the benefitMajor and minor bleeding increase. Peri-CABG timing matters, and corrected CABG bleeding values should be used.
What not to inferCURE alone does not establish the best current P2Y12 inhibitor, current DAPT duration, current de-escalation strategy, or management for patients on chronic anticoagulation/high bleeding risk.
Pharmacotherapy lensHistorical tested regimen: clopidogrel 300-mg load, then 75 mg daily with aspirin 75-325 mg. The study provides a clear benefit-harm framework but not a complete contemporary dosing guideline.
Clinical-use boundary: use this as landmark evidence and mechanistic/pragmatic context. For present-day treatment decisions, verify against current ACS guidelines, labeling, patient bleeding risk, planned PCI/CABG, and concurrent anticoagulation.
