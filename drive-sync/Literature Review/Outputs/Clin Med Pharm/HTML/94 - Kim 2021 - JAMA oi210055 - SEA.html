One-page synthesis

Core finding: Adding a LAMA to ICS/LABA reduced severe asthma exacerbations (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.77-0.90) and modestly improved asthma-control scores and FEV1. The asthma-control effect was statistically significant but well below the ACQ minimal important difference; quality of life and mortality were not significantly different. Dry mouth/dysphonia increased (RR 1.65), while serious and treatment-related adverse events were not significantly different.

The most clinically useful feature is the distinction between relative and absolute benefit. Supplement eTable 1 shows that the same relative exacerbation effect yields a much larger absolute reduction when baseline exacerbation risk is high. This supports risk-stratified interpretation rather than treating triple therapy as uniformly high-value for every uncontrolled patient.

The review is methodologically strong for its time: independent study selection/extraction, RoB 2, GRADE, random-effects synthesis, broad source searching, sensitivity analyses, reconstructed time-to-event data, and trial sequential analysis. Important constraints include multiple non-hierarchized outcomes, a small pediatric evidence share, short-to-moderate trial duration, crossover trials analyzed as parallel in primary analyses, and no direct comparison with ICS-formoterol MART.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction

The article frames add-on LAMA therapy as a response to persistent uncontrolled asthma despite medium/high-dose ICS/LABA. Earlier reviews were limited by few trials, one dominant LAMA, imprecision, limited harms assessment, and uncertainty around exacerbations. Newer trials and pediatric approval motivated an updated synthesis.

Methods

Searches covered MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL, WHO ICTRP, FDA, and EMA sources from November 2017 through December 8, 2020 without language restriction, supplemented by citation searching and author contact. Eligible studies were RCTs comparing triple with dual therapy in moderate-to-severe asthma. Screening, extraction, and RoB 2 assessment were performed independently.

DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models were primary; continuous scales were standardized and back-translated when useful. GRADE assessed certainty, trial sequential analysis informed imprecision/optimal information size, and sensitivity/subgroup analyses tested robustness. For time-to-event exacerbation data, Kaplan-Meier curves were digitized to reconstruct patient-level data for shared-frailty Cox analysis.

Results

Twenty RCTs (11,894 participants; median of mean ages 52 years; 57.7% female) were included. Severe exacerbation risk was lower with triple therapy (RR 0.83), annualized exacerbation rate was lower (IRR 0.85), and time to first severe exacerbation favored triple therapy (HR 0.84). Asthma control improved slightly (SMD -0.06; ACQ-7 MD -0.04), but the magnitude was below the 0.5 MID. Quality of life was not significantly different (SMD 0.05).

Trough FEV1 improved by 0.08 L, and more participants achieved a ≥200 mL increase. Mortality was similar. Dry mouth/dysphonia increased; serious, treatment-related, pulmonary infectious, ocular, and cardiovascular adverse-event analyses did not show significant excess risk. Subgroup/sensitivity analyses did not reveal credible effect modifiers and supported robustness.

Discussion

The authors interpret the main value of LAMA add-on as exacerbation prevention rather than large improvements in day-to-day symptoms or quality of life. They emphasize that baseline exacerbation risk should influence the absolute expected benefit and argue that their findings support stronger rationale for adding LAMA in patients at high exacerbation risk.

They also note that all trials used short-acting beta-agonist rescue and that direct comparison against ICS-LABA maintenance-and-reliever therapy was unavailable, limiting conclusions about the relative position of triple therapy among alternative escalation strategies.

Limitations

Only 3 trials decomposed the composite severe-exacerbation endpoint; other biomarkers could still modify treatment effect; multiple comparisons create type I error risk; and 7 crossover RCTs were analyzed as parallel trials, although they represented only 448 participants and sensitivity analyses were consistent.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that triple therapy is associated with fewer severe exacerbations and modestly improved asthma control without significant differences in quality of life or mortality.

Figure 1 — Study identification

681 records identified; 98 duplicates removed; 583 screened; 195 full texts assessed; 177 full texts excluded; 18 articles representing 20 unique RCTs and 11,894 patients included. The flow clarifies extensive duplicate publications and exclusion reasons.

Main Table — Included-study characteristics

Shows intervention/comparator regimens, participant numbers, age, sex, asthma severity/history, LAMA/ICS/LABA components, follow-up, and outcomes. Trials were heterogeneous in regimen and duration but consistently compared add-on LAMA triple therapy against ICS/LABA dual therapy.

Figure 2 — Severe asthma exacerbations

Incidence-rate meta-analysis: IRR 0.85 (95% CI 0.78-0.92; I² 0%). Risk of ≥1 severe exacerbation: RR 0.83 (95% CI 0.77-0.90; I² 17%). No credible interaction by prior exacerbation requirement.

Figure 3 — Time to first severe exacerbation

Reconstructed Kaplan-Meier analysis across 6 trials/8,296 participants; shared-frailty Cox HR 0.84 (95% CI 0.77-0.92), favoring triple therapy. The source text contains percentages discordant with the curve/HR; interpretation relies on the model estimate and curve direction.

Figure 4 — Asthma control and quality of life

Asthma control: SMD -0.06 (95% CI -0.10 to -0.02), equivalent ACQ-7 MD -0.04, statistically significant but below MID 0.5. Quality of life: SMD 0.05 (95% CI -0.03 to 0.13), not significant.

Figure 5 — FEV1

Trough FEV1 MD 0.08 L (95% CI 0.07-0.10; I² 0%); 47% vs 37% achieved ≥200 mL improvement (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.22-1.32). Magnitude is small and clinical significance is uncertain.

Supplement eTable 1 — Summary of Findings

GRADE summary translates relative effects into baseline-risk-dependent absolute effects. For severe exacerbations, a 30% baseline risk yields 24.6% with triple therapy (5.4 percentage points fewer); a 2.7% baseline risk yields 2.2% (0.5 points fewer). This strongly emphasizes baseline exacerbation risk in treatment value.

Supplement eTable 3 — Subgroup/sensitivity analyses

Explored exacerbation history, type-2 status, LAMA type, age, FEV1, smoking history, ICS dose/type, and multiple sensitivity scenarios. Some nominal interaction P values occurred, but credibility assessments were low and the authors concluded no credible effect modifiers.

Supplement eFigure 1 — Risk of bias

Most RCTs were low risk of bias; 2 trials were high risk. Sensitivity analyses restricted to low-risk studies did not materially change pooled estimates.

Supplement eFigure 2 — Funnel plots

The authors reported no strong evidence of publication bias or funnel-plot asymmetry.

Supplement eFigures 4-7 — Harms/outcomes

Support pooled analyses for asthma worsening, serious/nonserious adverse events, mortality, and detailed adverse-event categories. The clearest excess harm was dry mouth/dysphonia (RR 1.65).

Trial sequential analyses were reported as supporting robustness of the principal findings and informing GRADE imprecision assessments.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for the evidence base; do not use alone as a current-practice authority.
Best use: quantify exacerbation benefit, understand the modest symptom/FEV1 effects, and support baseline-risk-dependent shared decision-making. Do not use for: current guideline positioning without updated verification.
Relevance 9/10

Directly addresses add-on LAMA triple therapy in uncontrolled moderate-to-severe asthma.

Evidence: 20 RCTs, 11,894 participants; clinically important exacerbation and safety outcomes.

Limiting factor: Published in 2021; current guideline alignment was not externally verified.

Would raise/lower: A current guideline/updated meta-analysis confirming the same treatment position.

Novelty 8/10

At publication, substantially expanded a sparse earlier evidence base and incorporated newer LAMA regimens and adverse-event analyses.

Evidence: 17 more captured trials than the prior review described by the authors, including 2 unpublished trials.

Limiting factor: Novelty is historical rather than current.

Would raise/lower: Evidence that the pooled approach changed contemporary recommendations or remains unique after later trials.

Method strength 8/10

Comprehensive systematic-review methods with independent screening/extraction, RoB 2, GRADE, random-effects synthesis, sensitivity analyses, and trial sequential analysis.

Evidence: Prospective PROSPERO registration, PRISMA reporting, multiple databases/registries/regulators, reconstructed time-to-event data.

Limiting factor: Multiple outcomes were not prespecified as primary/secondary/exploratory; crossover trials were analyzed as parallel in primary analyses.

Would raise/lower: Clear a priori hierarchy of outcomes and consistent paired-analysis handling of crossover data.

Evidence strength 8/10

Pooled randomized evidence supports fewer severe exacerbations with high-certainty GRADE ratings; several secondary outcomes show small or null effects.

Evidence: RR 0.83 for severe exacerbation risk; high-certainty evidence for exacerbations, FEV1, mortality, and selected harms.

Limiting factor: Some effect sizes are statistically significant but below established minimal important differences; mortality estimates are imprecise despite high-certainty labeling.

Would raise/lower: More patient-centered outcome data and longer follow-up with precise mortality/serious-harm estimates.

External validity 8/10

Broad eligibility across LAMA types, doses, and single/combination inhalers supports applicability to many uncontrolled asthma populations.

Evidence: Adults and children/adolescents included; 3 LAMA types; medium-to-high-dose ICS/LABA background therapy.

Limiting factor: Pediatric evidence was a minority; current-smoker and asthma-COPD overlap populations were limited; trials were generally short.

Would raise/lower: More pediatric, smoking, diverse phenotype, and long-term pragmatic data.

Implementation value 7/10

Provides effect magnitudes and baseline-risk-dependent absolute effects useful for shared decision-making.

Evidence: eTable 1 translates RR into absolute effects at different exacerbation risks; dry mouth/dysphonia harm is quantified.

Limiting factor: Does not directly compare triple therapy with ICS-formoterol MART and predates later practice updates.

Would raise/lower: Head-to-head evidence against modern preferred controller/reliever strategies plus current guideline verification.

Key interpretive caution: statistical significance is not equivalent to clinical importance. The ACQ-7 mean difference (-0.04) is far below the 0.5 MID, while the exacerbation benefit is more clinically consequential and varies materially with baseline risk.
Source inconsistency: the narrative text accompanying Figure 3 reports event percentages that are discordant with the Kaplan-Meier curve direction and HR. The time-to-event interpretation should rely on the curve and HR 0.84 rather than those percentages.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use baseline exacerbation risk

The same RR translates to much larger absolute benefit in patients with prior exacerbations than in those at low baseline risk.

Set expectations

Exacerbation prevention is the principal benefit; average symptom-control and quality-of-life changes are small.

Monitor antimuscarinic effects

Dry mouth/dysphonia increased; serious and treatment-related harms were not significantly higher in the pooled data.

Verify current positioning

Before changing practice, compare the 2021 findings with current asthma guidelines and post-2021 comparative evidence, especially MART strategies.
