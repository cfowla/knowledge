Two-phase sequential implementation comparison (phase 1 empiric dosing; phase 2 nomogram-guided dosing).

University health science center cardiology service.

Scope warning: This is a 1995 single-center study using an assay- and institution-specific APTT range. One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. High relevance to heparin nomogram design and pharmacist-supported medication-use systems; moderate evidentiary value because the study is a nonrandomized sequential comparison with substantial phase-2 protocol nonadherence and a surrogate endpoint.

The investigators first observed clinician-directed intravenous heparin dosing, then used phase-1 data to build a modified weight-based nomogram. The phase-2 regimen recommended a 75-U/kg bolus, an initial infusion of 13 U/kg/h by total body weight, and fixed APTT-based infusion adjustments. The operational question was whether a standardized weight-referenced approach could get cardiology patients into a therapeutic APTT range faster and more consistently than empiric prescribing.

Nomogram-guided patients reached therapeutic APTT sooner: mean time to first target was 13.1±11.9 hours versus 20.7±19.1 hours with empiric dosing (P=.02). Target attainment within 12 hours was 62.2% versus 34.1% (P=.002), and within 24 hours was 77.8% versus 54.5% (P=.009). The study also demonstrated sharply lower variability in the initial weight-normalized infusion rate. These results support standardization as a process intervention, but causal confidence is limited because only 45 of 156 phase-2 patients followed the nomogram sufficiently for analysis, clinicians were not randomized, monitoring cadence changed with the intervention, and the outcome was laboratory target attainment rather than clinical thrombosis or bleeding.

Section-by-section condensation

Background / rationale

The paper frames delayed or inadequate heparin dose adjustment as a common process problem: low starting doses, delayed laboratory response, inappropriate adjustments, and inconsistent infusion delivery can prolong subtherapeutic anticoagulation. Prior protocol studies suggested fewer tests and infusion changes and faster attainment of therapeutic anticoagulation, but the authors considered nomogram performance in broader cardiovascular-service heparin use insufficiently evaluated.

The study objectives were to identify a practical patient-specific variable associated with the heparin infusion rate producing therapeutic APTT and to compare the time to target under empiric versus nomogram-guided management.

Patients and methods

Phase 1 included patients receiving heparin from April 1-June 30, 1992. Dosing was entirely physician directed, based on clinical experience and APTT response, with nursing and pharmacy staff deliberately making no dosing interventions. Phase 2 collected data March 11-June 11, 1993 after implementation of a modified nomogram. The recommended loading dose was 75 U/kg total body weight; the initial infusion was 13 U/kg/h total body weight, rounded to the nearest 100 U/h; subsequent changes followed fixed APTT bands.

Therapeutic APTT was defined as 55-95 seconds. Baseline demographics, heparin doses, APTTs, hematocrits, and documented bleeding were recorded. The investigators calculated ideal body weight, body surface area, and blood volume and used regression to identify an infusion-rate predictor. ANOVA compared time to therapeutic APTT; chi-square compared 12- and 24-hour target attainment. Bleeding had no prespecified definition and was not a primary endpoint.

Results - phase 1

Seven of 95 patients were excluded for missing information, leaving 88 analyzed. Sixty-six (75.0%) reached therapeutic APTT; among those 66, mean time to first therapeutic APTT was 20.7±19.1 hours. Nine of 88 (10.2%) were above the therapeutic range for at least the first 24 hours, while 13 (14.8%) never exceeded the lower target limit.

Blood volume showed the strongest reported correlation with infusion rate at first therapeutic APTT (r²=.3756; P=.0001). Total body weight alone was weaker but significant (r²=.3043; P=.0001) and was selected because it was practical. The mean weight-normalized infusion rate at first therapeutic APTT was 13.1±3.2 U/kg/h. An initial bolus did not significantly shorten time to target: 20.1±16.6 versus 21.7±23.0 hours (P=.73).

Results - phase 2 and between-phase comparison

Only 45 of 156 phase-2 patients were analyzed because the other 111 did not start or adjust therapy according to the nomogram. Of the 45, 41 (91.1%) reached therapeutic APTT; among those 41, mean time to target was 13.1±11.9 hours. All nine phase-2 patients who did not receive the recommended initial bolus nevertheless reached therapeutic APTT within 24 hours.

Compared with phase 1, phase 2 had faster mean target attainment (13.1 vs 20.7 hours; P=.02), more patients at target by 12 hours (62.2% vs 34.1%; P=.002), and more at target by 24 hours (77.8% vs 54.5%; P=.009). Initial weight-normalized infusion means were similar, but phase 2 was much less variable: 12.1-13.8 U/kg/h versus 7.1-26.1 U/kg/h in phase 1.

Safety observations

Two severe bleeding episodes requiring discontinuation were documented across the screened cohorts, one in each phase; both occurred in patients excluded from the main analyses. Among included patients, hematocrit fell by more than 3% over 3 days in 10 of 88 phase-1 patients and 5 of 45 phase-2 patients.

The authors explicitly caution against strong safety interpretation: bleeding was not a primary objective, had no formal definition, minor events may have been missed, and the observation period was limited. Therefore the low observed bleeding incidence is not persuasive evidence of comparative safety.

Comment / limitations

The authors attribute benefit not only to weight-based starting doses but also to the structured adjustment process and reminders for timely APTT measurement. This is important because the intervention changed both dose logic and workflow. They also note difficulty getting clinicians to follow the nomogram; only 45 of 156 phase-2 patients were protocol adherent enough for analysis.

Generalizability was constrained by the single cardiology service, predominance of unstable angina/suspected myocardial infarction, very limited VTE representation in phase 2, assay-specific APTT targets, and lack of evaluation after the first therapeutic APTT. Ten patients had received recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, potentially affecting APTT. Indication-specific dose analyses were not performed because subgroups were small.

Authors' conclusion

The authors concluded that a weight-based nomogram for initial and maintenance heparin dosing was associated with a higher proportion of cardiology patients reaching the targeted APTT range earlier than empiric dosing. They regarded total body weight as a practical variable for dose selection despite stronger statistical associations for some calculated body-size measures.

Clinical appraisal

PICO: P = cardiology-service patients receiving IV UFH; I = weight-based nomogram (75-U/kg recommended bolus, 13-U/kg/h initial infusion, APTT-based adjustments); C = empiric physician-directed dosing; O = time to first APTT 55-95 s, target attainment by 12/24 hours, and observational safety signals.

Validity: The strongest methodological weakness is selection into the analyzed phase-2 cohort: 111 of 156 patients were excluded because the nomogram was not used to initiate or adjust therapy. In addition, the intervention bundled dose standardization with reminders about when to measure APTT, so the observed effect cannot be attributed solely to the weight-based dose. There was no randomization, no concurrent control, and no patient-centered efficacy endpoint.

Practice translation: The paper supports the medication-use-system principle that explicit weight-based initiation plus deterministic monitoring/adjustment logic can reduce dosing variability and shorten time to a laboratory target. It does not establish that this exact 13-U/kg/h regimen or 55-95-second APTT range should be used in current practice.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What to preserve
Weight-referenced initiation, explicit action bands, scheduled reassessment, and routine safety monitoring are reusable protocol-design concepts.
What the study teaches about workflow
The protocol changed both dosing and test-timing behavior. A CDS or order-set implementation should treat monitoring cadence and response latency as first-class components, not just dose math.
What not to overclaim
Do not infer improved thrombosis or bleeding outcomes, and do not treat the exact APTT target or dose as contemporaneously validated.
Design lesson
Protocol adherence must be measured explicitly. Excluding nonadherent patients can make a protocol look more effective than an intention-to-treat implementation analysis would.
Best use in a heparin deep dive: historical evidence for the transition from fixed empiric infusion rates toward weight-based, rule-driven UFH management and for the operational importance of standardized monitoring.
