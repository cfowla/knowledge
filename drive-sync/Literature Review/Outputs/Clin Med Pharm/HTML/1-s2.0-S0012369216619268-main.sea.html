One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Effective VTE management requires adequate-intensity anticoagulation, sufficient treatment duration, overlap when transitioning from heparin to warfarin, careful bleeding/HIT surveillance, and targeted prophylaxis in high-risk groups. The article treats thrombolysis as a more selective strategy whose early physiologic benefits were clearer than mortality or long-term disability benefits.

The article is particularly important for its historical articulation of several anticoagulation concepts: an APTT response above roughly 1.5 times control for heparin, explicit concern about under-anticoagulation and recurrence, platelet monitoring for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, heparin-warfarin overlap, an INR target of 2.0-3.0 for VTE, and a minimum multi-month duration for most treated VTE. It also organizes prophylaxis by risk group and presents concrete regimens, including low-dose subcutaneous heparin and intermittent pneumatic compression.

The evidentiary weakness is equally important. The article summarizes many underlying trials without systematic-review methods, often reports percentages without complete denominators or uncertainty, and uses a historical evidence hierarchy. Several recommendations are stronger than the transparency of the synthesis would support by modern standards; one indefinite-treatment recommendation is explicitly grade C and not based on published data. The paper is therefore high-value as a historical architecture document for anticoagulation practice, but unsuitable as a direct implementation source without contemporary verification.

Individuals at Increased Risk for Venous Thromboembolism

Risk framework

The authors emphasize venous stasis and trauma as major risks and add age, estrogen exposure, prior VTE, carcinoma, immobility, heart failure, and major lower-extremity orthopedic surgery. Hip and knee replacement are highlighted as very-high-risk settings. The article also distinguishes moderate-risk surgical and medical populations for whom pharmacologic or mechanical prophylaxis is appropriate.

For calf-vein thrombosis, the article addresses the then-controversial question of embolic risk. It cites recurrence and proximal-extension data supporting either treatment or close serial noninvasive surveillance. The synthesis reports roughly 20% proximal extension among documented calf DVT patients, low event rates after negative serial testing, and a controlled comparison in which 5-day intermittent IV heparin was associated with 8/28 recurrences versus 0/21 after approximately three months of anticoagulation.

Load-bearing quantitative details: total hip/knee replacement DVT incidence about 40% in narrative text; Table 2 lists 40-70% for hip fracture/hip replacement/knee replacement; conventional prophylaxis is described as reducing thrombosis by roughly 50-60%.

Heparin

Mechanism, efficacy, and intensity

Heparin is described as a parenteral glycosaminoglycan that accelerates antithrombin III inhibition of thrombin, IXa, and Xa. The article argues that biologic potency varies enough that heparin should be prescribed in units rather than drug weight. Controlled evidence is cited for pulmonary embolism treatment, and the authors focus heavily on the balance between inadequate anticoagulation and hemorrhage.

The central intensity claim is that heparin must achieve a minimum anticoagulant effect. A randomized trial in proximal vein thrombosis is summarized as showing a 20-25% recurrent-VTE risk when an adequate APTT response was not achieved. The authors recommend maintaining APTT above 1.5 times control and use 1.5-2 times control in treatment guidance. Conversely, excessive anticoagulant responses are repeatedly associated with higher bleeding: examples include 20% versus 4% bleeding by clotting-time strata, 56% versus 16% with excessive versus non-excessive heparin effect, and reported bleeding relative risks of 3 and 8 at progressively higher PT/APTT prolongation.

Administration, monitoring, complications, and duration

Four randomized comparisons of intermittent versus continuous IV heparin are discussed, but route and dose are confounded because continuous-infusion groups often received less heparin. One trial of intermittent heparin found 8% versus 10% bleeding with versus without APTT monitoring. The article nevertheless emphasizes closer monitoring during the first several days, when requirements are greatest, and platelet counts every 3-4 days because of HIT.

Severe HIT is described as antibody-mediated, sometimes with platelet counts near 20,000/µL and associated thrombosis, prompting immediate heparin cessation. Other adverse effects include osteopenia with prolonged high-dose exposure and hyperkalemia in susceptible patients. For acute VTE, the authors recommend 5-10 days of heparin with 4-5 days of overlap with warfarin; shorter initial courses had conflicting trial results. If full-dose heparin is contraindicated, the article favors vena caval interruption/filtering rather than substituting prophylactic-dose heparin or aspirin.

Coumarin Derivatives / Warfarin

Mechanism and transition

Warfarin is described as inhibiting gamma-carboxylation of vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX, and X as well as proteins C and S. Its onset is delayed by persistence of circulating factors. Because protein C declines relatively quickly, the authors warn that large loading doses may temporarily favor coagulation during the first 24-48 hours and use this physiology to support overlap with heparin.

Monitoring is based on one-stage prothrombin time, with explicit recognition that thromboplastin potency complicates comparisons. The article adopts INR 2.0-3.0 for VTE and argues that this lower-intensity range preserves efficacy with less bleeding than more intense anticoagulation. It notes that the target range is achieved only about 50-70% of the time because of diet, vitamin K absorption, and adherence variability.

Duration, bleeding, reversal, pregnancy

Duration is individualized: at least three months for slowly resolving risk factors; indefinite therapy for tumors, antithrombin III deficiency, protein C/S deficiency, or recurrent VTE; and potentially shorter courses for fully transient factors. A controlled trial is cited as showing that two weeks is insufficient. The final summary grades indefinite therapy only C and explicitly says it is not based on published data.

Hemorrhage is the major complication. The article recommends lowering intensity to INR 2.0-3.0 to reduce bleeding. It describes parenteral vitamin K as capable of reversing warfarin within 24 hours and fresh frozen plasma for very serious bleeding. Warfarin-associated skin necrosis is linked to protein C deficiency and malignancy. Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy in this source, with adjusted-dose subcutaneous heparin favored.

Cost-Effectiveness of Anticoagulant Therapy

The authors summarize a cost-effectiveness analysis in which each long-term strategy followed 10-14 days of initial IV heparin and at least three months of subsequent therapy. Low-dose subcutaneous heparin is described as ineffective for long-term therapy and costly because of recurrence. Less-intensive warfarin is ranked most cost-effective and associated with relatively low bleeding; more-intensive warfarin is effective but bleeds more; adjusted-dose subcutaneous heparin is effective with low bleeding but somewhat more expensive.

The practical conclusion is to favor less-intensive warfarin for most long-term VTE therapy and adjusted-dose subcutaneous heparin for pregnancy, warfarin hypersensitivity, or inadequate laboratory access. Thrombolytic Therapy

Agents and dosing

Streptokinase, urokinase, and tPA are discussed. Streptokinase is described as 250,000 IU IV loading followed by 100,000 IU/h, with 24 hours for PE and 48-72 hours for DVT. Urokinase is described as 4,400 IU/kg loading followed by 4,400 IU/kg/h for 12 hours in PE. Both streptokinase and urokinase are nonspecific enough to disrupt hemostatic plugs; tPA is more fibrin-selective but still causes bleeding.

Laboratory tests such as APTT or thrombin time demonstrate a lytic state, but the authors state that neither bleeding nor thrombolysis correlates well enough with these tests to justify elaborate post-activation dose adjustment.

Comparative effect and uncertainty

Large PE trials are summarized as showing about threefold greater angiographic thrombus resolution at 24 hours with urokinase/streptokinase than with heparin, plus a 35% reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance versus 4% with heparin. Early lung-scan and hemodynamic responses favored thrombolysis, while later scan improvement converged. The source recommends 12 hours of urokinase or 24 hours of streptokinase for PE.

The authors do not claim a proven mortality benefit. They note prior trials were aimed at thrombolysis, not mortality, and were too small against a heparin-warfarin mortality under 10%. Thrombolysis is therefore reserved conceptually for massive, hemodynamically unstable PE in patients not prone to bleeding. For DVT, possible improvements in pain, swelling, valve preservation, and postphlebitic syndrome are described as inconsistent and in need of more controlled study.

Prevention of Venous Thromboembolism

The prevention section strongly favors low-dose subcutaneous heparin for many high-risk groups and describes 5,000 units every 8-12 hours as a repeatedly studied regimen. In elective hip replacement, the paper lists warfarin, dextran, adjusted-dose subcutaneous heparin, heparin plus dihydroergotamine, and low-molecular-weight heparin as effective options and cites approximately 50-60% risk reduction with conventional low-dose heparin.

Dextran is presented as comparable to low-dose heparin for moderate-risk patients and potentially useful when heparin is contraindicated, but with fluid-overload and rare allergic risks. Aspirin is not recommended for DVT prophylaxis or treatment. Intermittent pneumatic compression is emphasized for total knee, urologic, and neurosurgical procedures. Other historical approaches—including dihydroergotamine combinations and ancrod—are discussed with important safety or availability limitations.

Primary Pulmonary Hypertension

The article finds no convincing evidence that antithrombotic or fibrinolytic agents improve outcomes in primary pulmonary hypertension. The authors note continuing interest in thrombosis as a mechanism and call for a controlled prospective anticoagulant trial.

This section is explicitly hypothesis-generating rather than practice-defining and should not be used as evidence of benefit.

Summary and Recommendations

Prevention

  1. Grade A: moderate-risk patients — low-dose heparin 5,000 units SC every 12 h or intermittent pneumatic compression.
  2. Grade A: neurosurgical procedures, major knee surgery, and urologic surgery — intermittent pneumatic compression.
  3. Grade A: elective hip surgery — adjusted-dose heparin targeting the upper half of the normal APTT range or moderate-dose warfarin targeting INR 2.0-3.0.
  4. Grade A: fractured-hip surgery — moderate-dose warfarin targeting INR 2.0-3.0.

Treatment of venous thromboembolism

  1. Grade A: DVT or PE — IV heparin or adjusted-dose SC heparin sufficient for APTT 1.5-2 times control.
  2. Grade A: continue heparin 5-10 days and overlap oral anticoagulation for 4-5 days.
  3. Grade A: continue anticoagulation for at least 3 months; use INR 2.0-3.0 for oral therapy or adjusted-dose heparin when oral therapy is unsuitable.
  4. Grade C: indefinite therapy for recurrent VTE or persistent risk such as antithrombin III deficiency, protein C/S deficiency, or malignancy; the source explicitly states this was not based on published data.
  5. Isolated symptomatic calf DVT — three months of anticoagulation based on accumulated level I, II, and IV evidence; if anticoagulation cannot be given, perform serial noninvasive studies for proximal extension.
  6. Thrombolytic therapy — highly individualized; more investigation needed before more definite recommendations.
Practice boundary: These are the source's 1989 recommendations. They are preserved for historical analysis, not endorsed here as current therapy.

Evaluation and Appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best used to understand the historical development of heparin intensity targets, heparin-warfarin overlap, VTE prophylaxis logic, and early thrombolysis reasoning. Do not use as a standalone current clinical protocol.

Main strengths

  • Concrete heparin intensity, monitoring, overlap, and duration logic.
  • Quantitative discussion of under- and over-anticoagulation.
  • Explicit recognition of HIT, pregnancy constraints, bleeding, and warfarin interaction complexity.
  • Clear separation between routine anticoagulation and selective thrombolysis.
  • Risk-stratified prophylaxis tables and graded summary recommendations.

Main limitations

  • Narrative review rather than systematic synthesis.
  • Underlying trial results are secondary summaries, often without full denominators or uncertainty.
  • Historical evidence grading and practice framework.
  • Some recommendations exceed the transparency of the evidence base; one grade C recommendation is explicitly unsupported by published data.
  • Table 5 is unavailable in the evaluated PDF.
