One-page synthesis

Core thesis: IV heparin management among surveyed internists was highly variable, especially when APTT values were outside the physician's chosen therapeutic range; substantial disagreement about the therapeutic APTT range itself helped produce systematically different dose-adjustment behavior.

The survey found a modal initial regimen of a 5,000-unit bolus followed by 1,000 units/hour, but wide ranges around both initial and corrective dosing. Mean initial bolus was 6,925 units (SD 2,427; range 1,000-12,000) and mean initial infusion was 1,029 units/hour (SD 141; range 500-1,500). Dose changes at nontherapeutic APTTs varied much more than initial dosing: coefficients of variation for infusion changes were 0.67-0.81. Respondents also disagreed markedly on what counted as therapeutic, with reported APTT ranges spanning 37-120 seconds (1.2-4.0 times control).

The most revealing result is the presence of two mutually exclusive therapeutic-range groups comprising 61% of respondents. Physicians using the higher range rebolused more often at low APTT values and reduced infusions less aggressively at high values. This indicates that variation was not merely random: it was partly a consequence of incompatible monitoring targets. The paper therefore functions best as historical evidence for why institution-specific, standardized heparin management protocols became important, not as evidence for any particular current dose or APTT target.

Section condensation

Abstract / background

The authors frame IV heparin dosing as clinically important but incompletely standardized, citing prior evidence that delayed achievement of therapeutic anticoagulation was associated with recurrent thromboembolism and that excessive anticoagulation could contribute to hemorrhage. Their stated gap was a lack of detailed descriptions of routine physician dose-adjustment practices.

The structured abstract reports wide variation in dose choices and APTT targets, with especially high variability for responses to nontherapeutic APTT values. It previews two mutually exclusive therapeutic-range groups and concludes that experimental comparisons of standardized dosing protocols were needed.

Methods

Sixty-one board-certified or board-eligible attending internists who had used IV heparin during the preceding year were surveyed. Questions used a hypothetical 70-kg patient with confirmed DVT and no age, sex, or comorbidity details. Respondents supplied an initial bolus, initial infusion rate, therapeutic APTT range, and management choices for APTT bands <1.2, 1.2-1.5, 1.5-2.3, 2.3-3.0, and >3.0 times control. APTT values were assumed to be measured at least six hours after a dose change.

All three sites used the same reagent/test platform and an approximately 20-30 second normal APTT range. For analysis the investigators used the upper normal limit as the control APTT and converted respondent-supplied numeric ranges to their midpoint. Proportions were analyzed with chi-square; continuous variables with two-tailed Student t tests, including paired tests for within-group means.

Results

Initial dosing showed moderate dispersion, whereas corrective dosing showed large variation. Mean initial bolus was 6,925 units and mean initial infusion 1,029 units/hour. Only 8% used precise weight-based initial-dose formulas; another 20% considered weight informally. At APTT <1.2 times control, mean rebolus was 3,913 units and mean infusion increase +214 units/hour; at >3.0 times control, mean hold was 84 minutes and mean infusion change -222 units/hour. Responses within every nontherapeutic band spanned large ranges.

Among 52 physicians reporting a therapeutic APTT range, the modal range was 45-60 seconds, mean boundaries were 49-72 seconds, and the full range was 37-120 seconds. Two mutually exclusive groups (n=19 and n=13) used mean ranges of 44-56 and 60-83 seconds. Their dose responses differed in the expected direction: the higher-target group rebolused more often at low APTT and reduced infusion less aggressively at high APTT. Apart from a longer 71-90-second hold among subspecialists versus generalists (37 vs 7 minutes; p=0.007), physician age, specialty, and practice type did not explain the observed variation.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the results as evidence that usual-care heparin management was highly variable and that disagreement about the therapeutic APTT range was a major driver. They emphasize laboratory dependence of APTT response to heparin, arguing that nomograms developed with one reagent may not transport reliably to institutions using another. Their Figure 1 places the survey's modal responses beside several published management approaches to show the lack of a single dosing standard.

The authors explicitly note that they measured stated rather than actual practice, imposed the APTT decision intervals themselves, used a non-universal definition of control APTT, sampled only 61 physicians with possible selection bias, and focused on attendings in teaching hospitals where house staff may perform detailed dose management. They call for prospective comparisons of standardized dosing approaches rather than treating the survey itself as a dosing standard.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Read the methods, Tables 1-2, Figure 1, and limitations. The paper has high historical value for protocolization and monitoring-standardization questions, but limited direct value for current dosing decisions.
Relevance - 8/10Rationale: directly addresses IV UFH dosing variability and APTT-driven adjustment. Evidence basis: the entire survey centers on initial dosing, target ranges, and corrective actions. Limiting factor: no patient outcomes. Higher score would require direct protocol/outcome comparison; lower if the review question were not focused on heparin systems.
Novelty - 6/10Rationale: for its era, the detailed mapping of clinician behavior and incompatible APTT targets is informative. Evidence basis: quantitative survey of multiple decision nodes and subgrouping by target range. Limiting factor: descriptive rather than mechanistic or interventional. Higher with prospective protocol testing; lower if similar practice surveys were already well established.
Method strength - 5/10Rationale: clear survey scenario, common laboratory context, and explicit statistical methods support internal description. Evidence basis: 61 attending physicians across three hospitals. Limiting factor: stated rather than observed behavior, investigator-imposed decision bands, uncertain representativeness. Higher with validated instrument and observed practice audit; lower with major missing-response or sampling problems.
Evidence strength - 6/10Rationale: the data directly support the claim that reported practices varied widely. Evidence basis: broad ranges, high coefficients of variation, and significant between-target-group differences. Limiting factor: evidence supports practice variation only, not superiority of any regimen. Higher with concordant audit data; lower if survey responses proved poorly correlated with actual dosing.
External validity - 4/10Rationale: three teaching hospitals and a shared APTT reagent context limit transportability. Evidence basis: authors note reagent-dependent APTT response and possible selection bias. Limiting factor: attending-only sample and historical laboratory practices. Higher with multi-region/multi-reagent replication; lower if local workflow was unusually atypical.
Implementation value - 7/10Rationale: strong as a rationale for protocol standardization, laboratory-specific calibration, and separating target definition from dose-response rules. Evidence basis: variation aligns with differing therapeutic goals and Figure 1 shows competing adjustment schemas. Limiting factor: it does not identify the best protocol. Higher with prospective comparative outcomes; lower if used as a dosing recipe rather than a systems-design lesson.

Validity

  • Selection: incomplete sampling of all heparin prescribers; selection bias possible.
  • Measurement: self-reported intended behavior rather than chart-audited behavior.
  • Construct: investigator-selected APTT bands may not match clinicians' real decision thresholds.
  • Laboratory dependence: APTT ratios are reagent/system dependent, which the authors identify as a major transportability problem.
  • Outcome limitation: no bleeding, recurrent VTE, time-to-therapeutic anticoagulation, or other patient outcome was measured.

Practice and implementation takeaways

Use this paper forHistorical justification for standardizing IV heparin management; identifying how target-range disagreement propagates into dosing variation; understanding why laboratory context matters when transferring APTT-based nomograms.
Do not use it forSelecting a contemporary initial bolus, infusion rate, therapeutic APTT range, or hold/restart algorithm. The survey describes reported 1993 practice and was not an efficacy trial.
System-design lessonDefine the monitoring target and laboratory calibration before encoding dose-adjustment rules. Otherwise two internally coherent protocols can recommend opposite actions for the same APTT.
Research lessonProtocol evaluation should measure time-to-target, recurrence, bleeding, and portability across assay systems rather than only concordance with clinician preferences.
Bottom line: The source is valuable as a historical systems paper demonstrating that “usual care” was not one coherent heparin-dosing strategy. Its strongest contribution is showing that variance in the therapeutic target itself was associated with materially different dose-adjustment behavior.
