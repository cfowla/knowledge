One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. High relevance for SGLT2 formulary comparison, but use it as a map of indirect comparative signals rather than proof of empagliflozin–dapagliflozin non-equivalence or superiority.

The review combines 14 large SGLT2 outcome trials and asks a clinically useful question: whether individual agents behave differently across diabetes, CKD, and HF populations. Versus placebo, the network consistently supports cardiorenal benefits for several agents. The most striking active-agent findings are an empagliflozin-versus-dapagliflozin mortality RR of 0.81 (95% CI 0.69–0.96) in diabetes, canagliflozin-versus-dapagliflozin RR 0.75 (0.58–0.98) for CV death/HHF in people without HF, and sotagliflozin-versus-dapagliflozin MACE RR 0.73 with a reported CI near 0.57/0.58–0.92 in HF.

Those comparisons require restraint. The authors state that the trial network contains no direct comparisons between active SGLT2 inhibitors; the supplement’s CINeMA assessments frequently rate the key active-agent contrasts low or very low confidence. The HF sotagliflozin-versus-dapagliflozin CV-death/HHF estimate is RR 0.90 (0.80–1.01), so its interval includes the null despite the abstract describing it as “borderline significantly lower.” The source also contains several internal reporting discrepancies. For formulary use, the paper is useful for identifying hypotheses and evidence gaps, not as a stand-alone interchangeability decision source.

Methods and development process

Searches covered PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov through March 28, 2023. Eligible studies were English-language double-blind RCTs in adults, with placebo/non-SGLT2 controls and mortality, cardiovascular, kidney, AKI, or safety outcomes. Two reviewers performed search/selection work, and disagreements were adjudicated. The protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO.

The analysis used frequentist random-effects NMA in R 4.2.2, MetaInsight network maps, SUCRA rankings, RoB 2, CINeMA, funnel plots/Egger testing, and trial sequential analysis with 90% power and O’Brien-Fleming boundaries. A clinically important design constraint is explicit: no active SGLT2 inhibitor was directly randomized against another in the included trials, so active-agent contrasts are indirect.

Transitivity/indirectness: subgroup definitions, follow-up, doses, background therapies, and disease definitions vary across trials. The article says no direct-versus-indirect inconsistency assessment was required because there were no direct active-agent comparisons; meanwhile, several CINeMA tables show major incoherence concerns. This should be treated as a limitation in certainty rather than proof of comparability.

Safety

The authors’ qualitative synthesis flags increased lower-limb amputation with canagliflozin in diabetes and urinary-tract infection with ertugliflozin. The supplement’s CANVAS summary is internally inconsistent: it prints amputation HR 1.97 with 95% CI 1.41–1.75, which does not contain the point estimate.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 9/10

Directly addresses comparative SGLT2 efficacy across DM, HF, and CKD and is highly relevant to formulary questions.

Evidence: Broad clinically relevant outcomes and individual-drug network estimates.

Limiting factor: The most decision-sensitive active-drug contrasts are indirect.

Would change score: Would rise with head-to-head randomized evidence aligned to formulary populations.

Novelty 7/10

Provides an updated multi-comorbidity individual-agent NMA with TSA and CINeMA.

Evidence: Combines 14 major RCTs and separates DM/CKD/HF strata.

Limiting factor: Similar SGLT2 class and HF network meta-analyses already existed.

Would change score: Would rise with direct comparative trials or more robust individual-drug causal contrasts.

Method strength 6/10

Uses protocol registration, duplicate review, RoB 2, random-effects NMA, TSA, Egger testing, and CINeMA.

Evidence: PROSPERO registration and multiple formal evidence-quality methods.

Limiting factor: No active-agent head-to-head edges; heterogeneous subgroup definitions/doses and source-reporting inconsistencies.

Would change score: Would rise with direct evidence, prespecified transitivity checks, and cleaner consistency reporting.

Evidence strength 5/10

Placebo comparisons are supported by large RCT evidence, but active-drug superiority signals are much less secure.

Evidence: 75,334 participants and precise placebo-connected estimates for several endpoints.

Limiting factor: CINeMA rates key active-agent contrasts low/very low; some TSA analyses remain under information size.

Would change score: Would rise with direct active-comparator RCTs and high-confidence network estimates.

External validity 5/10

The trials span diabetes, CKD, HFrEF, HFpEF, and acute/worsening HF settings.

Evidence: Large international outcome-trial evidence base.

Limiting factor: Eligibility, disease definitions, follow-up, doses, and background therapies vary; placebo network differs from real formulary choice.

Would change score: Would rise with pragmatic head-to-head comparative-effectiveness evidence.

Implementation value 6/10

Useful for mapping hypotheses and identifying where agent-specific differences might matter, but insufficient alone for interchangeability policy.

Evidence: Clear subgroup estimates, safety signals, and evidence-confidence tables.

Limiting factor: Indirectness and low/very-low certainty weaken drug-selection claims.

Would change score: Would rise with direct evidence plus current guideline/label synthesis and local formulary constraints.

Main strengths

Large RCT evidence base; prospective registration; clinically important DM/CKD/HF stratification; formal risk-of-bias, CINeMA, publication-bias, SUCRA, and TSA analyses; detailed supplement with full comparison matrices.

Main limitations

No randomized active-agent head-to-head edges; active comparisons depend on transitivity assumptions. Disease definitions, follow-up, doses, and background therapies vary. Some analyses remain under information size. The high/low selectivity grouping is artificial. Multiple source-reporting inconsistencies reduce confidence in precise interpretation.

Do not use for: declaring empagliflozin and dapagliflozin clinically non-equivalent, selecting one as universally superior, or establishing hospital formulary interchangeability without direct comparative evidence, current guidelines/labels, and local implementation considerations.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Use as a structured map of agent-specific hypotheses and subgroup evidence, especially for where direct evidence should be sought.
Formulary implication
The paper does not establish a robust efficacy hierarchy between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin; the most prominent differences are indirect and low-certainty.
Read next
Direct comparative observational studies or randomized evidence, current HF/CKD/diabetes guidelines, regulatory labels, and local inpatient-use constraints.
Monitor
Agent-specific safety, renal function/eGFR rules, perioperative/acute-illness holding guidance, and indication-specific label coverage.
