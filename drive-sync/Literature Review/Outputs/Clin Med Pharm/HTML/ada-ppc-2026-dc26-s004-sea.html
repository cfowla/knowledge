One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Comprehensive diabetes care should be person-centered and iterative, deliberately assess comorbidities and structural barriers, and use risk-stratified screening/referral pathways rather than treating A1C as the sole focus of the encounter.

The chapter spans routine medical evaluation, immunization, autoimmune disease, bone health, cancer, cognition, dental care, disability, hepatitis C, sexual health, MASLD/MASH, sleep apnea, pancreatitis, and sensory impairment. Its greatest operational value lies in the checklists and explicit thresholds: DXA intervals and fracture-treatment triggers; annual dental referral; sexual-health and disability screening; and a two-step FIB-4 → elastography/ELF pathway for MASLD fibrosis risk.

The largest 2026 practice-impact area is MASLD/MASH. The chapter integrates new terminology, cirrhosis-prevention algorithms, stage-specific treatment, semaglutide phase 3 evidence, resmetirom specialist pathways, and cautions for compensated versus decompensated cirrhosis. This makes the section useful for clinical workflow design, but its primary-study claims should be verified against cited sources before being reused as independent evidence.

Section condensation

Person-Centered Collaborative Care

The chapter places person-centered, culturally sensitive, strengths-based communication and active listening at the start of comprehensive diabetes care. It emphasizes eliciting preferences and beliefs, assessing literacy/numeracy and barriers, avoiding stigmatizing language, and using shared decision-making rather than a directive model.

Interprofessional care is explicitly recommended and may include diabetes care and education specialists, primary and specialty clinicians, nurses, dietitians, exercise specialists, pharmacists, dentists, podiatrists, and behavioral health professionals. Figure 4.1 operationalizes this as a recurring assess–decide–plan–implement–review cycle rather than a one-time treatment choice.

Comprehensive Medical Evaluation

A complete evaluation is expected at the initial visit and at follow-up as appropriate: confirm/classify diabetes; assess glycemic status and prior/current treatment; evaluate complications, comorbidities, and overall health; identify care partners/resources; assess social determinants and structural barriers; review risk-factor management; formulate goals; and plan continuing care. Follow-up frequency is individualized, with the narrative stating at least every 3–6 months and then at least annually.

Table 4.1 expands the evaluation across history, behavioral/psychosocial factors, medication and technology use, social determinants, examination, and laboratory testing. Table 4.2 links assessment to planning and referral. The chapter repeatedly frames glycemic management as one component of the encounter, not the sole goal.

Immunizations

Recommendation 4.5 directs age-indicated routine vaccination for children, adolescents, and adults with diabetes. The narrative highlights COVID-19, hepatitis B, influenza, pneumococcal, RSV, Tdap, and zoster vaccination because diabetes can increase infection-related morbidity and because vaccination gaps remain clinically important.

Table 4.3 summarizes ACIP/CDC schedules and the ACIP evidence type. These ACIP GRADE evidence types (1–4) are not the same construct as the ADA letter grades attached to recommendations; both should be preserved without conflation.

Autoimmune Diseases

Type 1 diabetes is linked with autoimmune thyroid disease and celiac disease, with other autoimmune conditions occurring less commonly. The chapter recommends thyroid screening soon after type 1 diabetes diagnosis with repeated testing when clinically indicated and celiac screening in adults with type 1 diabetes when symptoms, signs, laboratory manifestations, or suspicion are present.

These recommendations are screening-oriented rather than exhaustive treatment guidance. Abnormal findings require disease-specific evaluation and management beyond the chapter’s scope.

Bone Health

Bone health receives unusually detailed treatment. Diabetes increases fracture risk through skeletal fragility, complications, hypoglycemia/falls, and medication effects; the chapter notes that fracture risk may be underestimated by conventional BMD alone in type 2 diabetes. It recommends routine fracture-risk assessment, DXA monitoring in adults ≥65 years and younger high-risk individuals every 2–3 years, and targeted calcium/vitamin D intake.

Medication choice and glycemic goals should account for fracture and fall risk. Thiazolidinediones and sulfonylureas are singled out as higher-fracture-risk options to avoid when possible in high-risk people, while low-hypoglycemia-risk agents are prioritized. Drug therapy is considered at T-score ≤−2.5, prior fragility fracture, FRAX hip risk ≥3%, or major osteoporotic fracture risk ≥20%; treatment may also be considered at T-score −2.0 to −2.5 when additional risk factors are present.

Cancer

The chapter notes associations between diabetes and several cancers but stresses shared risk factors and uncertainty about causal mechanisms. It advises routine age- and sex-appropriate cancer screening and reduction of modifiable risks rather than diabetes-specific cancer-screening programs.

Atypical new-onset diabetes in a lean middle-aged or older person may precede pancreatic cancer, but the chapter does not recommend routine pancreatic cancer screening without additional symptoms or risk factors.

Cognitive Impairment/Dementia

Diabetes is associated with higher risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. The chapter emphasizes bidirectionality: cognitive impairment can worsen self-management and increase hypoglycemia risk, while severe hypoglycemia is also associated with worse cognitive outcomes.

When cognitive impairment is present, treatment plans should be simplified and tailored to minimize hypoglycemia. Intensive glycemic targets have not consistently improved cognitive outcomes, reinforcing the need for individualized goals rather than aggressive glucose lowering for cognition itself.

Dental Care

Diabetes and periodontal disease have a bidirectional relationship, and periodontal treatment may modestly improve glycemic outcomes. The chapter recommends at least annual dental examination and coordination between medical and dental teams around glucose-lowering therapy and procedural risk.

U.S. adults with diabetes have lower dental attendance than adults without diabetes in the cited evidence, making access and referral implementation relevant rather than merely preventive counseling.

Disability

Diabetes increases risk of work limitation, ADL/IADL impairment, mobility restriction, and disability through vascular, neurologic, visual, musculoskeletal, and other complications. Disability assessment is recommended at the initial visit and functional decline at subsequent visits.

When disability affects functional ability or diabetes self-management, referral to disability-focused professionals is recommended. The practical implication is to adapt devices, education, medication delivery, monitoring technology, and care plans to functional capacity rather than assuming standard self-management ability.

Hepatitis C

Chronic hepatitis C is associated with insulin resistance and diabetes; successful antiviral treatment may improve glycemia. The chapter summarizes a meta-analysis showing an A1C reduction of 0.45 percentage points after successful treatment in people with diabetes.

This is not a stand-alone antiviral treatment guideline; it supports recognition of the metabolic implications of HCV and coordination of diabetes therapy during and after curative treatment.

Sexual Health

The chapter recommends proactive sexual-health inquiry because erectile dysfunction and female sexual dysfunction are common yet underdiagnosed. Men with diabetes or prediabetes should be screened for erectile dysfunction, especially with vascular, renal, neurologic, glycemic, depression, or hypogonadal risk; symptoms/signs of hypogonadism prompt morning total testosterone testing.

Women with diabetes or prediabetes should be asked about desire, arousal, and orgasm difficulties, especially with depression/anxiety or recurrent urinary tract infection, and postmenopausal women should be screened for genitourinary syndrome of menopause. The chapter emphasizes the importance of clinicians initiating these discussions rather than waiting for the person with diabetes.

MASLD/MASH — Screening

The chapter adopts MASLD/MASH terminology and treats type 2 diabetes as a major cirrhosis-risk state. Adults with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes—particularly with obesity, other cardiometabolic risks, or cardiovascular disease—should undergo FIB-4 assessment even when liver enzymes are normal. Persistently elevated aminotransferases for >6 months despite low FIB-4 require evaluation for other liver disease.

FIB-4 ≥1.3 triggers second-line risk stratification with transient-elastography liver stiffness or ELF when elastography is unavailable. Figure 4.2 uses FIB-4 >2.67, liver stiffness ≥8.0 kPa, and ELF ≥9.8 as high-risk signals for specialist management, while lower-risk people are generally followed in primary/interprofessional care with repeat FIB-4 every 1–2 years.

MASLD/MASH — Management

Management combines lifestyle/weight loss, cardiovascular risk reduction, optimized diabetes treatment, and stage-specific liver therapy. The chapter states that at least 5% weight loss—and preferably ≥10%—is needed for histologic improvement, with larger reductions generally needed for fibrosis. GLP-1 receptor agonists, dual GIP/GLP-1 therapy, and pioglitazone are prioritized in selected type 2 diabetes/MASH contexts; insulin is preferred for hyperglycemia in decompensated cirrhosis.

The 2026 chapter incorporates phase 3 semaglutide evidence and current resmetirom treatment pathways. ESSENCE showed semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly for 72 weeks improved fibrosis without worsening steatohepatitis in 36.8% versus 22.4% with placebo and resolved steatohepatitis without worsening fibrosis in 62.9% versus 34.3%. Resmetirom requires specialist-oriented selection/monitoring. Figure 4.3 organizes obesity, diabetes, and MASH pharmacotherapy by fibrosis/cirrhosis stage; metabolic surgery is an option in appropriate candidates but requires caution in compensated cirrhosis and is not recommended in decompensated cirrhosis.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea, Pancreatitis, and Sensory Impairment

Obstructive sleep apnea is common with obesity and type 2 diabetes; weight loss and OSA-directed treatment are relevant, while emerging incretin data are noted but not yet diabetes-specific enough to define a full treatment pathway here. Diabetes and acute pancreatitis have a bidirectional relationship, and the chapter states that causality between incretin-based therapies and pancreatitis remains unestablished.

Hearing impairment is about twice as prevalent in people with diabetes in cited adjusted analyses, and long-term glycemic exposure may contribute. The chapter also notes smell impairment. These sections function mainly as recognition and assessment guidance rather than providing numbered treatment recommendations.

Figure 4.2 — Diagnostic algorithm for cirrhosis prevention in MASLD

Highest-risk entry groups: type 2 diabetes; prediabetes; obesity plus ≥1 cardiovascular risk factor.
Rule out secondary steatosis causes / consider elevated ALT.
First test: FIB-4. Lower risk (<1.3 in usual adults) → primary/interprofessional management, optimize lifestyle/comorbidities, repeat FIB-4 every 1–2 years.
Second test when FIB-4 ≥1.3: liver stiffness measurement (LSM); if unavailable consider ELF.
High risk: LSM ≥8.0 kPa, FIB-4 >2.67, or ELF ≥9.8 → liver specialist for additional imaging/biomarker stratification, treatment, and long-term follow-up.

Figure 4.3 — MASLD treatment algorithm

Cross-cutting care: healthy lifestyle, weight loss if indicated, optimal diabetes management, cardiovascular risk reduction, and surgical evaluation as appropriate.
F0–F1: obesity pharmacotherapy prefers GLP-1 RA/dual GIP-GLP-1; diabetes pharmacotherapy prefers GLP-1 RA/dual GIP-GLP-1/pioglitazone/SGLT2 inhibitor; MASH pharmacotherapy not indicated.

Compensated cirrhosis: obesity/diabetes pharmacotherapy requires caution; MASH pharmacotherapy is avoided.
Decompensated cirrhosis: obesity/MASH pharmacotherapy avoided; insulin is the preferred diabetes agent.

Guideline-specific appraisal

Recommendation framework: The chapter provides ADA letter grades (A/B/C/E) beside recommendations. Those grades are preserved exactly in the extraction. Table 4.3 also reports ACIP evidence types for vaccines; these are a separate evidence framework.

Population and exclusions: Scope varies by recommendation: all people with diabetes, adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, older adults, postmenopausal women, and MASLD/cirrhosis subgroups. Stage-specific exclusions and cautions are particularly important for decompensated cirrhosis.

Implementation: High. The chapter offers concrete screening intervals, thresholds, specialist-referral triggers, medication-class preferences, and repeat-testing windows. Local implementation still needs current vaccine schedules, formulary/access review, referral capacity, and integration with other ADA Standards sections.

Uncertainty: Some recommendations are based on grade C or E evidence, and multiple topic areas summarize heterogeneous observational or secondary evidence. The section itself does not provide the full evidence-to-recommendation tables or methodology needed for an independent guideline-development audit.

Confidence in appraisal: High for content coverage and operational interpretation of this chapter; moderate for independent evidence-strength judgment because the cited primary literature and separate ADA methodology were not re-appraised.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Visit designUse Table 4.1/4.2 as a longitudinal checklist; review diabetes status, comorbidities, function/cognition, psychosocial factors, SDOH, prevention, and referrals—not glycemia alone.
Bone healthAssess fracture risk routinely; DXA at ≥65 years or younger with multiple risks every 2–3 years; adjust glycemic targets/medication choice to reduce hypoglycemia/falls; use osteoporosis thresholds explicitly.
VaccinesBuild age- and risk-based vaccine review into diabetes visits, but reconcile Table 4.3 with the current CDC/ACIP schedule at the time of care.
MASLD screeningFor type 2 diabetes/prediabetes risk groups, calculate FIB-4 even with normal enzymes; FIB-4 ≥1.3 → transient elastography or ELF; route high-risk results to hepatology/gastroenterology.
MASLD treatmentStage liver disease before selecting therapy. Favor weight-loss strategies and MASH-beneficial agents when indicated; respect cirrhosis-stage restrictions and specialist pathways for resmetirom.
Function and sexual healthProactively screen for disability and sexual dysfunction; adapt self-management tasks and refer when function or specialty needs exceed routine diabetes care.
Do not overclaim: Trial effects quoted in this chapter are secondary reports. They become primary-study evidence only after the cited publications are directly acquired and reviewed.
