One-page synthesis

Bottom line: The trial provides credible early evidence that constrained, asynchronous GPT-4 feedback can add benefit to a structured worry-log intervention for state anxiety and worry in young Chinese community adults with mostly mild anxiety symptoms. It does not establish autonomous AI psychotherapy, equivalence to trained human coaches, benefit for generalized anxiety or depression, or safety in higher-acuity clinical populations.

What changed
All groups improved over time. Added coaching produced incremental gains for state anxiety and worry, but not generalized anxiety or depression.
Human vs GenAI
No statistically detectable differences were found between active arms. This supports similarity of observed outcomes but is not an equivalence or noninferiority result.
Acceptability
Participants rated human and GenAI feedback similarly across encouragement, empathy, feeling heard, accuracy, emotional connection, and task-focused support while feedback source remained blinded.
Mechanisms
Exploratory mediation suggested human feedback acted more through reduced risk perception, while GenAI feedback was associated with improved emotion clarity, especially for worry.
Safety
No serious adverse events or unintended harms were reported, but high-risk participants were excluded and safety monitoring was not a dedicated, systematic clinical safety endpoint.
Deployment reality
The intervention was not fully autonomous: all participants received live human psychoeducation and repeated RA contact; all feedback was monitored in real time by the first author.

Clinical / research question

PICO-style framing

  • Population: Chinese community adults ≥18 years with GAD-7 ≥5; mean age 22.99 years; 68.6% female; severe depression, self-harm/suicide risk, hazardous alcohol use, and current/recent psychotherapy or psychotropic medication were excluded.
  • Intervention: 10-day digital worry log plus standardized GPT-4-generated personalized written feedback every two days.
  • Comparators: identical worry log plus trained student-coach feedback; and worry log alone.
  • Primary outcomes: state anxiety (STAI-state), pathological worry (PSWQ), generalized anxiety (GAD-7).
  • Secondary outcome: depressive symptoms (PHQ-9).
  • Follow-up: post-intervention and one month; state anxiety additionally measured every two days during treatment.

Question actually answered

The study asks whether adding personalized feedback to a brief, structured self-help worry-log intervention improves outcomes, and whether GPT-4 can deliver that feedback with observed efficacy and participant ratings approaching a trained human coach.

Question not answered: whether an autonomous GenAI mental-health system is safe or effective without human psychoeducation, RA support, source blinding, and real-time feedback monitoring; or whether GenAI is statistically noninferior/equivalent to licensed clinicians.

Section condensation

Abstract

Source-derived

The authors position GenAI-generated feedback as a scalable substitute for labor-intensive human feedback within digital worry logs. In the three-arm RCT, GenAI feedback improved state anxiety and worry relative to worry-log-only control, with observed improvements and acceptability described as comparable to human feedback.

Appraisal

The abstract accurately signals the two outcomes with incremental benefit but compresses important qualifications: no added effect on generalized anxiety or depression, differential attrition, lack of an equivalence design, and substantial human involvement in intervention delivery and monitoring.

Introduction

Source-derived

The rationale combines two established observations: unguided digital CBT/self-help has engagement and efficacy limits, while human guidance can improve outcomes but is hard to scale. The authors propose a deliberately constrained hybrid architecture—structured worry logs plus asynchronous GenAI feedback—rather than a free-form multi-turn chatbot, partly to reduce safety exposure.

The worry log targets threat appraisal through self-monitoring and later reality checking of feared outcomes. The authors frame feedback as mechanistically active: clarifying emotions, recalibrating perceived likelihood/severity of feared outcomes, and supporting engagement. They identify a gap in randomized evidence directly comparing GenAI feedback with both human feedback and no feedback.

Methods — design, recruitment, allocation

Source-derived

Participants were recruited online and through university-affiliated platforms in China during May 2025. A total of 169 were randomized simultaneously in Excel by an independent researcher to human coach (57), GenAI coach (57), or no coach (55). Allocation concealment was intended by randomizing only after enrollment. Participants were told they might or might not receive feedback but were not told the feedback source until post-study debriefing.

The trial was prospectively registered. The a priori sample target was 150 to detect Cohen f=0.15 with 80% power at α=.05 while allowing 25% attrition. Age differed at baseline (p=.04) and was included as a covariate. The efficacy analysis was changed from planned ANOVA to longitudinal LMM, which the authors justify as better suited to missing repeated measures.

Appraisal

Randomization and concealed allocation are strengths. Blinding was partial: feedback source was masked, but participants necessarily knew whether they received feedback. The analysis change is methodologically reasonable but should be interpreted as a protocol deviation/refinement rather than a preregistered analytic invariant.

Methods — intervention

Source-derived

All groups received approximately one hour of live online psychoeducation with Q&A, then completed a 10-day digital worry log. Participants were asked to make at least three entries daily and later reality-check predicted outcomes. Reminders were sent at 10:00, 14:00, and 18:00 each day.

Human and GenAI arms received feedback after 20:00 on days 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10. Feedback followed five standardized components: encouragement/affirmation; summary of recent entries; refinement of worrying thoughts toward short-term specificity; reality-check review and reappraisal; and motivation to continue logging. The first author monitored all feedback in real time for adherence and consistency.

Methods — human coach and GenAI implementation

Source-derived

Six psychology students served as human coaches after two-stage training, including CBT-informed coaching, readings, manual review, 6–8 hours of role-play, supervised practice, and a 10-day pilot. None had prior independent clinical practice experience.

The GenAI coach used GPT-4 without additional training or fine-tuning. Literature used in human training and a worry-log manual were supplied as external context. System prompts defined an empathetic, nonjudgmental, structured role and prohibited diagnosis/medical advice, hallucinated or off-topic content, and harmful bias. The system was tested on two simulated datasets and piloted, with the first author reviewing outputs for clinical appropriateness, instruction adherence, and obvious safety concerns.

Appraisal

This is a useful constrained-assistance design, but the manuscript does not provide enough main-text detail to reproduce the exact AI system. Exact prompt text, model snapshot/version, decoding parameters, context assembly, failure handling, and supplement-only examples are unavailable in the uploaded file. Human monitoring also means the evaluated system is a supervised GenAI workflow, not autonomous deployment.

Methods — outcomes and statistics

Source-derived

State anxiety used the 20-item STAI state subscale; worry used the 16-item PSWQ; generalized anxiety used GAD-7; depression used PHQ-9. Acceptability/alliance used six indicators covering feeling heard, response accuracy, encouragement, empathy, emotional connection, and task-focused support. Mechanism candidates were emotion clarity, a single-item risk perception measure, and engagement.

ITT efficacy used repeated-measures LMMs with group, time, group×time, and baseline-imbalanced covariates. Complete-case LMMs were sensitivity analyses. Effect sizes used Feingold's longitudinal method. Mediation used PROCESS on completers with residualized change scores and 10,000 bootstrap resamples.

Results — efficacy

Source-derived

All three groups improved significantly over time for state anxiety, generalized anxiety, worry, and depression (time ps<.01). Incremental coaching effects were outcome-specific: group×time was significant for state anxiety (ITT p=.02) and worry (ITT p=.04), but not generalized anxiety (p=.42) or depression (p=.56). At follow-up, both active coaching arms had lower state anxiety and worry than control, with no detected difference between human and GenAI arms.

For GenAI versus control, state-anxiety effect sizes were d=0.51 (95% CI 0.09–0.93) post and d=0.67 (0.19–1.14) at follow-up. Worry was d=0.45 (−0.18 to 1.08) post and d=0.78 (0.09–1.48) at follow-up. Complete-case sensitivity preserved the state-anxiety interaction (p=.04), while worry moved to p=.06.

Appraisal

The follow-up effects are clinically interesting but imprecise. The wide confidence intervals and complete-case attenuation for worry matter. “No difference” between human and GenAI arms should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence because the trial did not specify a noninferiority/equivalence margin or design.

Results — engagement, acceptability, attrition

Source-derived

Mean worry-log entries were 21.25 (human), 19.91 (GenAI), and 16.73 (control), against a recommended 30; the between-group comparison did not reach significance (p=.08). Reality-check completion rates were similar (66.0%, 64.7%, 67.8%; p=.87). Human and GenAI groups did not differ significantly on any feedback rating at post-assessment or averaged across five assessments.

Attrition differed significantly at post (p=.002) and follow-up (p=.009), driven by lower attrition in the human arm than the GenAI arm (adjusted pairwise ps=.018). No serious adverse events or unintended harms were reported, and reported dropout reasons were unrelated to intervention content.

Appraisal

Source blinding strengthens the content-quality comparison but weakens any claim about real-world acceptability of feedback explicitly labeled as AI. Differential attrition is a meaningful caution because the GenAI arm lost more assessment completers despite similar measured engagement; LMM validity depends on the missing-at-random assumption.

Results — exploratory mechanisms

Source-derived

Human coaching versus control showed significant indirect effects through reduced risk perception for state anxiety and worry to one-month follow-up. GenAI coaching versus control showed an indirect effect through improved emotion clarity for worry, including at follow-up, despite a nonsignificant direct effect in that mediation model. Time-parallel pre-to-post analyses broadly supported the same pattern.

Appraisal

These analyses are hypothesis-generating. They used completers, one mediator was a self-developed single item, mediators were not experimentally manipulated, and multiple candidate paths were tested in a modest sample. The mechanism contrast should not be treated as established evidence that humans and AI operate through distinct causal pathways.

Discussion and limitations

Source-derived

The authors interpret the trial as first direct randomized evidence that LLM-generated feedback can be integrated into worry logs with efficacy and relational ratings comparable to trained human coaches. They propose the asynchronous format as a scalable, potentially safer alternative to multi-turn chatbot interactions and as a component of stepped care.

They acknowledge limited generalizability from a Chinese community sample with mostly mild symptoms, exploratory mediation, a single-item risk measure, human-delivered psychoeducation, use of trained students rather than licensed clinicians, and absence of post-debriefing assessment about suspected AI involvement or reactions to disclosure.

Appraisal

The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower than the paper's most promotional language: constrained GenAI feedback showed incremental efficacy over worry-log-only on two anxiety-related outcomes under a supervised research workflow. Claims that it “matches” human coaches, alleviates treatment-resource shortages, or establishes a scalable real-world treatment model require replication, more representative clinical samples, explicit equivalence testing, stronger safety evaluation, and substantially more implementation transparency.

Validity & AI-system appraisal

Internal validity — strengths

  • Prospective registration and ethics approval.
  • Randomized parallel groups with independent allocation process.
  • Common base intervention across all arms isolates incremental feedback effect.
  • Feedback source blinded during the intervention.
  • ITT longitudinal mixed models plus complete-case sensitivity analysis.
  • Effect sizes with confidence intervals reported.
  • Human and GenAI feedback constrained to the same standardized content framework.

Internal validity — threats

  • Differential attrition, especially GenAI vs human, challenges missing-at-random assumptions.
  • Analysis changed from planned ANOVA to LMM after registration.
  • Participants could not be blinded to receiving vs not receiving feedback.
  • No equivalence/noninferiority design despite strong “comparable/matching” language.
  • Mechanism analyses are completer-based and exploratory.
  • Risk perception mediator is a self-developed single item.

External validity

  • Mean age ~23 and recruitment through online/university-linked channels.
  • Mostly mild baseline anxiety; high-acuity and treated participants excluded.
  • Single-country, Chinese-language context.
  • Human psychoeducation and frequent RA interactions increase support beyond the digital tool itself.
  • Human benchmark was trained students, not licensed clinicians.

AI implementation & safety

  • GPT-4, no fine-tuning; external reference context plus manual and system prompt.
  • Explicit role and safety constraints are reported conceptually.
  • Exact prompt, model snapshot, decoding parameters, context packaging, and safety escalation logic are not available in the uploaded source.
  • Every feedback item was monitored in real time by the first author.
  • No dedicated systematic adverse-event/safety instrument is described; acceptability indicators were partly used as nonsystematic harm checks.
  • High-risk users were screened out, so safety findings cannot be extrapolated to crisis/high-acuity populations.
Key claim-boundary: The trial supports a supervised, constrained, asynchronous GenAI augmentation workflow. It does not validate unsupervised multi-turn mental-health chatbots or replacement of licensed therapy.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first. High-value paper for understanding a pragmatic, constrained GenAI augmentation pattern and for seeing where randomized efficacy evidence is stronger than deployment claims.
Relevance 9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses GenAI augmentation, human comparison, acceptability, and mechanisms in a clinical-adjacent digital intervention.

Evidence basis: Three-arm RCT with detailed intervention and outcome reporting.

Limiter: narrow mental-health task and mild community sample.

Would rise: replication across clinical populations and other structured interventions.

Novelty 9/10

Rationale: Direct randomized comparison of GenAI feedback, human feedback, and no feedback within the same worry-log protocol is uncommon and methodologically informative.

Evidence basis: authors frame it as the first such RCT; source itself provides the design.

Limiter: underlying CBT and feedback concepts are established.

Would rise: fully reproducible system and independent replication.

Method strength 7/10

Rationale: Randomization, controlled intervention, ITT LMM, sensitivity analysis, and effect sizes are substantial strengths.

Evidence basis: prospective registration, N=169, standardized arms.

Limiter: differential attrition, partial blinding, exploratory mediation, and post-registration analytic refinement.

Would rise: larger preregistered multisite trial with robust missing-data analyses and noninferiority design.

Evidence strength 6/10

Rationale: Evidence supports incremental benefit on state anxiety and worry, but not across all primary/secondary outcomes and with imprecise estimates.

Evidence basis: significant ITT interactions for state anxiety/worry; CIs reported; worry sensitivity p=.06.

Limiter: no equivalence test, limited follow-up, modest sample, exploratory mechanisms.

Would rise: replicated durable effects, clinically meaningful thresholds, independent safety evaluation.

External validity 4/10

Rationale: Generalizability is constrained by age, culture, symptom severity, exclusion criteria, and research-support intensity.

Evidence basis: mean age 22.99, China-only recruitment, mostly mild symptoms, human facilitation.

Limiter: not representative of routine clinical anxiety treatment.

Would rise: diverse multisite samples including moderate/severe clinical cases and routine-care implementation.

Implementation value 7/10

Rationale: The constrained asynchronous feedback pattern, standardized five-part response structure, and safety role boundaries are highly reusable design ideas.

Evidence basis: methods specify schedule, content components, training logic, and human monitoring.

Limiter: exact prompts and system parameters are supplement-dependent/unavailable; workflow still needs substantial human infrastructure.

Would rise: complete prompt/version package, audit logs, failure taxonomy, cost/latency data, and autonomous-safety evaluation.

Overall judgment

Best use: evidence and design precedent for constrained GenAI augmentation of a structured, evidence-based self-help task; useful for research design, product architecture, and safety-boundary thinking.
Do not use for: claiming GPT-4 is equivalent to clinicians, that GenAI psychotherapy is established, that autonomous mental-health deployment is safe, or that the intervention reduces generalized anxiety/depression beyond the base worry-log program.

Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high for main efficacy/design conclusions because the full main-text manuscript, tables, and figures are available; lower for reproducibility and prompt-level safety because the referenced supplementary materials were not included.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Constrain the interaction surface
The strongest system-design lesson is not “build a therapy chatbot,” but “embed GenAI inside a narrow evidence-based workflow with defined inputs, outputs, cadence, and forbidden behaviors.”
2. Separate augmentation from autonomy
The evaluated package includes live psychoeducation, RA reminders/contact, source masking, and real-time human monitoring. Any product that removes those elements is a different intervention.
3. Treat human-comparable as a hypothesis
Future work should preregister noninferiority/equivalence margins if the practical claim is substitution for human coaches.
4. Measure safety directly
High-risk exclusions and absence of reported serious harms are not a substitute for structured adverse-event detection, crisis escalation, hallucination auditing, and longitudinal failure analysis.
5. Preserve model provenance
Deployment-relevant studies should report model snapshot, prompt/version, decoding settings, retrieval/context sources, safety rules, audit trail, and human override criteria.
6. Measure acceptability after disclosure
Blinded-source ratings answer “how did the feedback feel?” but not “will users accept AI-delivered feedback when they know it is AI?”
7. Keep outcome specificity
The added-feedback effect was demonstrated for state anxiety and worry, not GAD-7 or depression. Avoid collapsing these into “anxiety treatment efficacy.”
8. Replicate in clinical populations
Before practice translation, test diverse age groups, moderate/severe symptoms, treated patients, longer follow-up, and licensed-clinician comparators.
