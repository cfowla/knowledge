One-page synthesis

Core thesis: A nonacademic regional health system can reduce biased vendor selection and generate clinician buy-in by using blinded simulation testing, workflow demonstrations, technical/security review, a pragmatic ambulatory pilot, and staged implementation metrics before scaling ambient AI documentation.

The paper describes McLeod Health's process for choosing and scaling an ambient AI documentation solution. Phase 0 screened vendors using KLAS and AVIA listings, excluding vendors with concerns about security, financial stability, or scalability. Phase 1 used live simulated patient encounters in family medicine, cardiology, and general surgery: 4 vendors generated notes from 15 complex outpatient scripts, and 25 evaluators ranked blinded notes for quality. Phase 2 compared the 2 highest-ranked vendors in Epic workflow demonstrations, with physician voting and independent technical/security assessment. Phase 3 tested the selected vendor in a 90-day ambulatory pilot beginning October 2024, followed by system-wide rollout beginning January 2025.

The pilot analyzed 23 clinicians after excluding low-use participants. Documentation time improved: median pajama time fell from 5.6 to 3.6 hours per 4-week period (-35.4%; P=.054), and median time in notes per appointment fell from 2.8 to 2.0 minutes (-28.3%; P<.001). Same-day note closure was unchanged. Coding shifted modestly toward level 4 established visits, established patient volume increased 8.5%, and the authors projected a combined coding-volume revenue gain of US $2629 per provider per month. Patient satisfaction improved on several NRC Health domains, including listening, trust, knowing what to do with questions, and treatment information. System-wide deployment reached 250 onboarded providers, 81% adoption, and nearly 150,000 generated notes.

The main value is operational rather than causal: the study supplies a practical selection-and-implementation framework for ambient AI in a resource-constrained, nonacademic health system. The main weakness is attribution. The pilot was not randomized, had no concurrent control group, selected efficient clinicians, excluded a sizable share of active participants, did not audit note accuracy or safety beyond usual processes, and modeled financial gains without total cost of ownership. Patient satisfaction and revenue changes may reflect secular trends, case mix, provider selection, or workflow changes other than ambient AI.

Best use

Designing an ambient AI evaluation, vendor-demo process, pilot metric set, and rollout governance checklist.

Do not use for

Claims that ambient AI causally improves patient satisfaction, reduces burnout, increases revenue, or is safe without local validation.

Key caution

The measured pilot effects come from high-adopting clinicians in one system and may overestimate benefits in broader deployment.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames ambient AI documentation as a potential response to documentation burden, physician dissatisfaction, and financial pressure in nonacademic health systems, while warning that vendor selection can be distorted by cognitive bias and vendor marketing. McLeod Health implemented a 3-phase process: simulated clinical testing of 4 vendors, Epic workflow demonstrations for the top 2, and a 90-day pilot across 5 ambulatory specialties before system-wide rollout.

Reported results include reduced note work, improved patient satisfaction, a coding shift toward higher-complexity established visits, increased established patient volume, projected revenue gain, and 81% adoption after rollout. The abstract's conclusions are operationally plausible but stronger than the design can prove because the pilot is uncontrolled and implementation effects are vulnerable to selection, seasonality, and secular trends.

Introduction / background

The authors argue that most US health systems are local or regional, nonacademic, and less resourced than academic medical centers. They position ambient AI as a tool that could reduce documentation burden, improve satisfaction, and capture revenue, but note that nonacademic systems lack standard, practical frameworks to select and scale these tools.

McLeod Health is described as a 7-hospital nonprofit, nonacademic system serving 18 counties in North and South Carolina with more than 1200 providers. The stated objective is to create and implement a structured process that reduces vendor influence and cognitive bias while measuring documentation efficiency, physician satisfaction, patient satisfaction, and financial return.

Methods - team and evaluation design

The project was led by the chief medical informatics officer with participation from clinical, operational, IT, revenue integrity, patient satisfaction, marketing, informatics, physician, and nursing leaders. The broad team structure is a strength for implementation because it aligns vendor selection with clinical workflow, data safety, and revenue-cycle needs.

The evaluation began in February 2024. Phases 1 and 2 ran from March to June 2024, and the 90-day pilot began in October 2024. The framework deliberately separated note quality, Epic workflow integration, technical/security review, and real-world pilot outcomes.

Methods - Phase 0 market scan

Potential vendors were identified using KLAS “Ambient Speech” listings and AVIA “Digitally Assisted Provider Documentation” vendors. Vendors were excluded for concerns about data security, financial stability, or scalability, and preference went to vendors with roadmaps beyond documentation.

Four vendors advanced to simulation testing. This is a reasonable screening strategy for a health-system purchase, but the paper does not provide the complete inclusion/exclusion rubric, vendor list, or scoring thresholds.

Methods - Phase 1 simulation testing

Four vendors participated in live simulations using 15 standardized outpatient scripts: 5 each in family medicine, cardiology, and general surgery. Scripts included realistic complexity such as difficult accents, contradictory family input, and interruptions. Vendors recorded encounters in real time and submitted notes immediately without edits.

Twenty-five evaluators from operations, clinical practice, revenue cycle, and patient satisfaction reviewed blinded notes and ranked vendors. This phase has a strong anti-marketing design, but the scoring used ordinal ranks rather than a validated note-quality instrument, and 8 of 25 evaluators submitted only a first-place preference.

Methods - Phase 2 workflow demonstrations

The top 2 vendors demonstrated Epic integration, coding support, and real-time note generation in a standardized complex outpatient case. Approximately 30 physicians observed, with voting limited to physicians who attended both demonstrations. Informatics and IT security teams independently reviewed the technology stack, roadmap, data safety, and beyond-documentation capabilities.

Physician and technical reviewers converged on one vendor, leading leadership to cancel a planned side-by-side phase 3 comparison. This decision may have saved time and cost, but it also removed the strongest possible real-world comparative test between the top vendors.

Methods - Phase 3 pilot testing

The 90-day pilot included obstetrics and gynecology, orthopedic surgery, oncology, primary care, and emergency medicine. The system initially bought 500 licenses but limited the formal pilot to physicians in the top 30% for documentation efficiency who were not already using human scribes. The authors explicitly intended efficiency gains to reduce after-hours documentation rather than increase volume.

Outcome metrics included adoption, Epic Signal documentation metrics, coding and revenue modeling using CMS reimbursement values, NRC Health patient satisfaction surveys, and a provider survey. Analysis required more than 20% encounter utilization, 3 months of baseline data, and at least 1 month of active use, which narrowed the evaluated cohort to meaningful users but also introduced high-adopter selection bias.

Ethics

The McLeod Health Institutional Review Board determined the project did not constitute human subjects research under 45 CFR 46.102 and characterized it as quality assessment and quality improvement. No formal IRB review was required.

This classification is plausible for a local implementation evaluation, but the manuscript still raises governance questions typical of deployed AI documentation systems: note accuracy auditing, clinician attestation, patient consent or notification practices, model-change monitoring, and compliance review are not reported as dedicated study outputs.

Results - Phase 1

All 25 evaluators submitted rankings. Seventeen submitted complete 3-choice rankings, while 8 submitted first-place preference only. Across 45 notes, vendor 1 received 57 points (45.2%), vendor 2 received 44 points (34.9%), and vendor 3 received 25 points (19.8%). Physician subgroup rankings mirrored the overall results: 45.8%, 37.5%, and 16.7%, respectively.

The fourth vendor's notes were reviewed but excluded from further analysis because of noncompetitive pricing and contractual infeasibility. This is operationally relevant but complicates interpretation because a vendor could be technically evaluated but removed for commercial reasons.

Results - Phase 2

Nearly 20 physicians who attended both demonstrations overwhelmingly preferred vendor 2, with 90% favorability compared with vendor 1. Technical reviewers also favored vendor 2, citing a more advanced technology stack, conversational command capabilities within the EHR, specialty-specific chart summarization, and voice-guided billing and coding assistance.

This result reversed the phase 1 note-quality ranking, where vendor 1 scored higher. The authors justify the final selection on workflow integration and strategic functionality. That is a defensible health-system decision, but the evidence base becomes a composite of note quality, usability, strategy, and technical assessment rather than a pure clinical documentation accuracy comparison.

Results - Phase 3 pilot

During the pilot, 49 clinicians were onboarded in 2 cohorts and 39 were active users, for 79.6% adoption. The analyzed cohort included 23 providers: family medicine (12), orthopedics (5), oncology (4), and emergency medicine or urgent care (2). Of analyzed providers, 70% were physicians and 30% were advanced practice providers. Obstetrics and gynecology participants did not meet analysis criteria.

Median pajama time decreased from 5.6 to 3.6 hours per 4-week period (-35.4%; P=.054), median time in notes per appointment decreased from 2.8 to 2.0 minutes (-28.3%; P<.001), same-day note closure changed from 68.0% to 69.8% (P=.43), and time on unscheduled days decreased from 5.4 to 2.2 hours (P=.09). The strongest statistical signal is reduced time in notes; the other time metrics trend favorably but do not meet conventional significance thresholds.

Results - coding, revenue, patient satisfaction, and provider satisfaction

Established patient coding shifted modestly: level 3 visits decreased 2.4 percentage points (P=.17), level 4 visits increased 3.8 percentage points (P=.05), and level 5 visits decreased 0.4 percentage points (P=.66). Established patient volume rose from 223.3 to 242.3 per provider, an 8.5% increase (P=.002). The authors estimated US $379 per provider per month from coding complexity and US $2629 per provider per month when volume and complexity were combined.

For 21 providers with available NRC data, median patient satisfaction improved from baseline to postpilot for listening (+8.4 points), trust (+7.5), knowing what to do if questions (+8.8), and treatment information (+10.4), all P<.001. Provider recommendation and overall rating remained high without significant change. The provider survey had 18 responses among 49 clinicians (36.7%): 50% reported being able to schedule or see more patients, 89% reported spending 5 minutes or less per note, median satisfaction was 8/10, median accuracy was 7.5/10, and all respondents rated colleague recommendation as 10/10.

System-wide implementation progress

During rollout, McLeod negotiated usage-based pricing with a capped monthly rate to reduce waste from unused licenses. Preliminary results were shared with about 675 ambulatory clinicians. Access was then expanded to interested physicians and advanced practice providers not using human scribes, and additional licenses were purchased for inpatient teams.

Implementation occurred in phases. Communication across decentralized clinics was challenging, so the system used an educational campaign and physician testimonial videos. Self-onboarding started in March 2025. Since system-wide implementation began in January 2025, 250 providers have been onboarded, adoption reached 81%, and nearly 150,000 notes were generated.

Discussion, comparisons, limitations, and conclusions

The authors argue that transparent, performance-based vendor selection is especially valuable for nonacademic systems vulnerable to marketing pressure and peer-adoption bias. They also argue that ambient AI benefits extend beyond documentation time to patient experience, coding, revenue, and clinician satisfaction. Comparisons with prior work are consistent in direction: other studies have also reported documentation-time reductions, relative value unit shifts, and differences across ambient AI tools.

The limitations section is unusually important: single-system design, small pilot, no control for seasonality or case mix, no validated burnout assessment, potential bias from the CMIO serving as simulated patient in phase 2, high-adopter selection in phase 3, no special audit/compliance review, no total cost of ownership, no new-patient revenue accounting, and no accounting for unused-license savings. The conclusion is credible as an implementation narrative but overstated if read as causal evidence of ambient AI effectiveness.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. This is a useful implementation paper for ambient AI evaluation and scaling in a nonacademic health system. It should be read as a pragmatic workflow and governance case study, not as definitive evidence that ambient AI independently improves patient experience, clinician well-being, revenue, or safety.
8.5

Relevance

Rationale: Highly relevant to clinical AI implementation, documentation burden, Epic workflow, and health-system operational evaluation.

Evidence basis: The article reports a full selection-pilot-rollout pathway with documentation, satisfaction, adoption, and financial metrics.

Principal limiting factor: It is less relevant to direct pharmacotherapy or bedside clinical decision-making.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with more safety-governance detail; would fall if the reader only needs controlled efficacy evidence.

7.0

Novelty

Rationale: The contribution is not a new AI method but a practical nonacademic-health-system selection framework with real-world rollout metrics.

Evidence basis: The paper combines blinded simulations, workflow demos, technical review, pilot data, and usage-based pricing lessons.

Principal limiting factor: Many components are known implementation tactics, and ambient AI documentation evidence is growing rapidly.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with a reusable scoring instrument or open evaluation scripts; would fall if similar community-system frameworks become common.

5.5

Method strength

Rationale: The vendor-selection process is stronger than the pilot outcome design.

Evidence basis: Blinded note review, standardized scripts, multidisciplinary scoring, Epic demonstrations, and objective EHR metrics strengthen the methods.

Principal limiting factor: The phase 3 pilot is uncontrolled, small, high-adopter selected, and vulnerable to seasonality and case-mix confounding.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with randomized or stepped-wedge rollout, concurrent controls, prespecified quantitative success thresholds, and note accuracy audits; would fall with undisclosed vendor influence or incomplete outcome data.

5.0

Evidence strength

Rationale: The evidence supports feasibility and association with improved documentation time but does not prove causality for satisfaction or revenue outcomes.

Evidence basis: Time in notes improved significantly; patient satisfaction domains and volume changed in favorable directions; vendor-supplied extracts were reportedly independently verified against EHR/revenue data.

Principal limiting factor: No concurrent control group, proprietary data, no total cost analysis, and no dedicated safety/compliance auditing limit confidence.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with external validation, audited note-quality outcomes, cost accounting, and controlled attribution; would fall if effects were not sustained among lower-use or broader clinicians.

5.0

External validity

Rationale: The setting is more representative of many nonacademic systems than academic medical centers, but generalizability remains limited.

Evidence basis: McLeod is a decentralized nonprofit regional system with multiple specialties and system-wide rollout experience.

Principal limiting factor: Single health system, selected efficient pilot users, excluded low-use clinicians, and a vendor-specific workflow constrain transferability.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with multisite community-system replication and specialty-stratified effects; would fall if benefits depend heavily on one vendor, one EHR configuration, or unusually motivated champions.

8.0

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper gives enough operational detail to inform a local ambient AI evaluation and rollout plan.

Evidence basis: It provides phases, stakeholders, simulations, metrics, pricing lesson, communication barriers, and adoption tactics.

Principal limiting factor: It lacks a full scoring rubric, total cost model, error taxonomy, and ongoing governance thresholds.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with appendices containing scripts, scoring forms, dashboards, audit tools, and vendor-contract considerations; would fall if used as a turnkey protocol without local adaptation.

Main strengths

  • Vendor selection used blinded simulated encounters rather than only sales demonstrations or peer reputation.
  • The framework separated note quality, EHR workflow integration, technical/security review, and real-world adoption.
  • Metrics were operationally meaningful: pajama time, time in notes, same-day closure, coding distribution, patient satisfaction, provider survey, adoption, and note volume.
  • The authors acknowledge key limitations, especially lack of controls, high-adopter bias, seasonality, and no total cost analysis.

Main limitations and red flags

  • Attribution: Pre-post improvements cannot be confidently attributed to ambient AI without a concurrent control group or randomized/stepped-wedge design.
  • Selection bias: The pilot intentionally selected top-documentation-efficiency clinicians and then analyzed only users meeting utilization criteria.
  • Revenue model fragility: Volume increases and coding shifts may reflect scheduling, demand, seasonality, case mix, or documentation behavior rather than AI-driven productivity.
  • Safety gap: The study does not report structured note-accuracy auditing, hallucination/error rates, clinician edit burden, patient complaints, privacy incidents, or compliance findings.
  • Vendor transparency issue: Vendor labels are anonymized in the methods/results, but the acknowledgments thank Suki staff for summary extracts used in coding and financial analyses. This makes the vendor effectively identifiable while leaving comparative vendor data opaque.
  • Manuscript consistency issue: The methods state that a co-author “DS” independently verified vendor-supplied data, but the listed authors are BKF, VEC, FS, JP, and JJ. This should be clarified because it affects provenance of the verification claim.

Bottom line

Use this paper to build an evaluation playbook. It is especially useful for structuring vendor demonstrations, choosing pilot metrics, gaining clinician buy-in, and anticipating pricing/adoption issues.
Do not use this paper as standalone proof of clinical or financial benefit. Local implementation should add controlled evaluation where feasible, formal note-quality/safety auditing, total cost of ownership, specialty-level effect analysis, and governance for model updates and documentation errors.

Confidence in appraisal: Moderate. The article is internally coherent and data-rich for an implementation report, but key outcomes are confounded and many raw data elements are proprietary or summarized.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Build

A local vendor-evaluation workflow with standardized clinical scripts, blinded note review, physician usability voting, IT/security review, and revenue-cycle review.

Measure

Track pajama time, time in notes, same-day closure, clinician edit burden, note error rates, adoption, active-use distribution, patient satisfaction, coding distribution, volume, and total cost.

Govern

Require clinician attestation, error escalation, audit samples, privacy/security review, patient-notification policy, specialty-specific templates, model/version-change logs, and compliance oversight.

Contract

Prefer pricing that avoids paying for unused licenses. Usage-based pricing with a cap aligns cost with adoption while limiting exposure.

Adopt

Use clinician champions and specialty-specific workflow examples. Peer evidence appears more persuasive than general training for changing documentation habits.

Avoid overclaiming

Do not market projected revenue or satisfaction improvements as causal until compared with a control group and adjusted for case mix, seasonality, and operational changes.

Suggested local pilot design if repeating this work

Minimum stronger design:
  - Step-wedge or matched-control rollout across specialties.
  - Include high-burden and low-burden documenters, not only efficient clinicians.
  - Predefine primary outcomes and minimum clinically meaningful changes.
  - Audit a random sample of AI notes for omissions, hallucinations, wrong attribution, medication/allergy errors, diagnosis errors, and billing-support errors.
  - Track clinician edits and time-to-final-signature, not only generated-note count.
  - Report costs: licenses or usage charges, implementation labor, training, support, compliance review, and any human scribe substitution.
  - Stratify by specialty, clinician type, baseline documentation burden, adoption intensity, and use of templates/scribes.
  - Monitor patient-facing effects: consent/notification, complaints, perceived attention, and trust.

Practice translation

What should change: Health systems considering ambient AI should not rely on reputation, market share, or generic vendor demos. A structured evaluation with blinded simulated cases, EHR workflow testing, multidisciplinary scoring, and real-world KPI tracking is justified.

What should not change based on this paper alone: Staffing models, patient scheduling templates, coding expectations, or clinical documentation policies should not be changed solely on the projected revenue and satisfaction findings. Those require local validation, compliance review, and sustained monitoring.
