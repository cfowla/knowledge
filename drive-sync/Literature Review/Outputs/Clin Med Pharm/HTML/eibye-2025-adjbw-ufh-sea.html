2. One-page synthesis

Core finding. In 166 adults treated for VTE with the same AdjBW-based, anti-Xa–guided UFH protocol, obese and nonobese groups had similar median time to first target anti-Xa: 12.45 vs 13.03 hours (P=0.49). Initial target attainment was also similar (32.0% vs 34.07%, P=0.78), as was the study's evidence-of-major-bleed outcome (8.00% vs 8.89%, P=0.84).

The paper's strongest contribution is operational: it demonstrates that a single uncapped AdjBW-based UFH pathway can be applied across BMI strata at one academic center without an observed difference in time-to-target anti-Xa. The dosing pathway is clinically interpretable and unusually concrete: 80 units/kg optional bolus, 18 units/kg/h suggested initial infusion, anti-Xa goal 0.3–0.7 IU/mL, six-hour reassessment when out of range, nurse-driven titration, and pharmacist verification.

The evidentiary ceiling is lower than the conclusion may initially imply. Both groups received AdjBW-based dosing, so the design tests comparability across obesity status under the protocol, not whether AdjBW is superior or noninferior to TBW. The manuscript states an objective of determining “equivalency,” but the reported analysis is conventional null-hypothesis testing for between-group differences with P values; no equivalence margin or confidence-interval-based equivalence framework is reported. Absence of a statistically significant difference therefore should not be interpreted as formal proof of equivalence.

Signal requiring attention. Class 3 obesity had more below-target first anti-Xa values (47.83%; 11/23) than class 1 or 2 obesity (P=0.049), although median time to first target remained similar (12.58 hours; across-class P=0.91). This is a small subgroup finding and should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than a settled dosing rule.

4. Section-by-section condensation

Introduction / rationale

The authors frame UFH dosing as having evolved from fixed nomograms and aPTT adjustment toward weight-based dosing and anti-Xa monitoring. They emphasize that obese patients were underrepresented in foundational weight-based UFH studies and that the literature on TBW dosing in obesity is inconsistent. The physiologic rationale offered is that UFH remains largely intravascular and adipose tissue is less vascular than lean mass, raising concern that TBW-based dosing could overestimate dosing needs.

The claimed evidence gap is more specific than “UFH in obesity”: prior studies evaluated TBW versus AdjBW approaches largely within obese cohorts, whereas the authors sought to compare obese and nonobese patients managed using the same AdjBW-based protocol. The proposed implementation advantage is simplified order entry and reduced human error.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort at a single 620-bed academic medical center. Adults treated with IV UFH for VTE for ≥24 hours between 2018 and 2023 were eligible. The protocol used AdjBW for all dosing unless TBW was lower than AdjBW. The recommended initial regimen was an optional 80 units/kg bolus and 18 units/kg/h infusion, without caps. Anti-Xa was measured every 6 hours while out of range, with a target of 0.3–0.7 IU/mL and nurse-driven titration.

The primary endpoint was time from infusion start to first target anti-Xa. Obesity was BMI ≥30 kg/m²; obesity classes were analyzed separately. Mann–Whitney U, chi-square/Fisher exact, and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used as appropriate, with α=0.05. No multivariable adjustment, propensity strategy, prespecified equivalence margin, or confidence-interval-based equivalence analysis is reported. The manuscript also does not report the specific AdjBW equation, which limits exact reproducibility of the central dosing exposure.

Results

The final cohort comprised 166 patients: 75 obese and 91 nonobese. Median age was 68 years overall; the obese group was younger in the descriptive table (65 vs 72 years). Median BMI was 35.2 kg/m² in obese patients and 24.6 kg/m² in nonobese patients. VTE indications were DVT in 91 patients, PE in 58, and both in 17. Most patients (148/166; 89.16%) started at the suggested 18 units/kg/h infusion rate.

Median time to first target anti-Xa was 12.45 hours in obese patients versus 13.03 hours in nonobese patients (P=0.49). Time to the second consecutive target was 27.91 vs 30.67 hours (P=0.55). First anti-Xa values were in range in 32.00% vs 34.07% (P=0.78), above range in 37.33% vs 46.15% (P=0.25), and below range in 30.67% vs 19.78% (P=0.11). Evidence of major bleed occurred in 6/75 (8.00%) vs 8/91 (8.89%) (P=0.84). In class 3 obesity, 11/23 (47.83%) had a below-target first anti-Xa, producing the only nominally significant obesity-class comparison (P=0.049).

Discussion / interpretation

The authors interpret the similarity in time-to-target and first anti-Xa distribution across obesity status as supporting use of AdjBW in both obese and nonobese patients. They compare their observed time-to-target with earlier AdjBW studies using aPTT and note that their initial supratherapeutic anti-Xa proportions were comparatively low. They also acknowledge that the class 3 obesity signal may indicate initial underdosing, although its clinical importance is unclear because time to first target was not longer.

The paper emphasizes practical distinctions of its pathway: anti-Xa rather than aPTT adjustment, no dose caps, and a nurse-driven protocol with pharmacist verification. The authors note anti-Xa cost and feasibility constraints and caution that their findings may not extrapolate to aPTT-based institutions. They also acknowledge the retrospective design, small sample, single-center setting, possible mistimed laboratory draws, optionality of the recommended bolus/initial rate, and absence of the total UFH dose at target attainment.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that obese and nonobese patients had no observed difference in median time to first target anti-Xa or evidence of major bleeding when managed with the AdjBW protocol, and they support a simplified AdjBW-based approach for both groups when anti-Xa is used for monitoring.

That conclusion is directionally consistent with the observed data, but it is stronger if read as an implementation feasibility statement rather than a comparative efficacy statement. The study supports “similar observed process performance across BMI groups under one AdjBW protocol”; it does not directly establish the superiority, noninferiority, or formal equivalence of AdjBW versus another dosing-weight strategy.

6. Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High relevance and useful protocol detail, but the evidence is best used to inform local UFH protocol evaluation rather than to justify a stand-alone practice change.
Relevance
9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses UFH dosing weight, obesity, VTE, and anti-Xa monitoring.

Evidence basis: Full institutional protocol and clinically familiar monitoring endpoints. Limit: laboratory target attainment rather than patient-centered efficacy. Would rise: clinical VTE and bleeding outcomes linked to dosing strategy.

Novelty
7/10

Rationale: Adds obese-versus-nonobese experience under a single all-comers AdjBW/anti-Xa pathway.

Evidence basis: Authors identify this comparison as previously unreported. Limit: no direct AdjBW-vs-TBW comparison. Would rise: prospective strategy comparison.

Method strength
5/10

Rationale: Clear cohort and protocol, but retrospective, single-center, unadjusted, and not designed as a formal equivalence study.

Evidence basis: n=166; standard nonparametric/categorical tests. Limit: no equivalence margin, no multivariable confounding control. Would rise: prespecified prospective equivalence/noninferiority design with adjusted analyses.

Evidence strength
5/10

Rationale: Data support similar observed anti-Xa process performance across BMI strata, but not comparative superiority or clinical outcome benefit.

Evidence basis: primary and secondary laboratory endpoints are internally coherent except noted source discrepancies. Limit: surrogate endpoint and low safety-event count. Would rise: confidence intervals, formal equivalence testing, recurrent VTE and robust bleeding outcomes.

External validity
4/10

Rationale: One academic center, 96% White cohort, local anti-Xa infrastructure, and only 23 patients with class 3 obesity.

Evidence basis: reported demographics and single-site design. Limit: uncertain transfer to diverse populations, aPTT-based sites, and different UFH workflows. Would rise: multicenter validation.

Implementation value
7/10

Rationale: The titration protocol is detailed enough to benchmark local workflow design.

Evidence basis: exact bolus, infusion, anti-Xa thresholds, and adjustment increments are reported. Limit: the actual AdjBW formula and UFH dose at target are not reported. Would rise: complete calculation specification and dosing-at-target distributions.

Major strengths

  • Clinically focused question with explicit UFH protocol, target anti-Xa range, and titration thresholds.
  • Includes both obese and nonobese patients under the same electronic ordering framework.
  • No empiric dose caps, reducing one common source of protocol heterogeneity.
  • Reports several complementary process endpoints rather than only “ever therapeutic.”
  • Obesity-class subgroup is reported rather than collapsing all BMI ≥30 kg/m².
  • Protocol adherence was operationally supported by nurse dual sign-off and pharmacist verification.

Critical limitations

  • “Equivalency” is not established statistically. Failure to reject a difference with P>0.05 is not the same as demonstrating equivalence; no equivalence margin or corresponding interval analysis is reported.
  • No alternative dosing-weight comparator. Both BMI groups received the AdjBW protocol, so the study cannot determine whether AdjBW is better than TBW or another strategy.
  • Surrogate primary endpoint. Anti-Xa attainment does not itself establish reduced VTE recurrence or improved patient-centered outcomes.
  • Confounding remains possible. The obese and nonobese groups differ descriptively in age and likely other clinical features; no adjusted outcome model is reported.
  • Safety precision is limited. Fourteen bleed events are insufficient for strong comparative safety claims, and the study's hemoglobin-drop criterion had no specified time frame.
  • Class 3 obesity subgroup is small. The below-target signal rests on 11 events among 23 patients and should not be treated as a definitive dosing failure.
  • Protocol reproducibility is incomplete. The manuscript does not report the AdjBW equation and does not report the absolute UFH dose or rate at target attainment.
  • Source reporting inconsistencies. Exclusion counts and the overall median time-to-target differ across manuscript locations.

What not to concludeDo not cite this study as proof that AdjBW is superior to TBW, formally equivalent across BMI groups, or safer than other dosing weights.
Class 3 obesityThe 47.83% below-target first anti-Xa rate is a reason to inspect local early target attainment in very high BMI patients, not an automatic reason to abandon AdjBW.
Monitoring boundaryApplicability is strongest where UFH is titrated to anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 IU/mL with approximately six-hour reassessment and comparable nurse/pharmacist workflow.
Local QI opportunityCompare first anti-Xa distribution, time-to-first and sustained target, infusion rate at target, bolus use, recurrent VTE, and bleeding by BMI class and dosing weight.
Policy thresholdA practice change should incorporate the local AdjBW equation, EHR implementation, laboratory turnaround, patient mix, clinical outcomes, and stronger comparative evidence.
Clinical bottom line. This is a useful implementation paper for an AdjBW/anti-Xa UFH pathway. Its data support feasibility and similar observed process performance across obesity status, but the study does not establish formal equivalence or comparative superiority and should not be the sole basis for changing a therapeutic UFH dosing policy.
