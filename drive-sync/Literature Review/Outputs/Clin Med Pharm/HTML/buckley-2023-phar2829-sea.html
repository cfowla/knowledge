2. One-page synthesis

Core finding: anti-Xa activity and standard TEG R time were poorly aligned during therapeutic UFH in critically ill COVID-19 patients. The reported relationship was weak (r²=0.032, p<0.0001), and categorical agreement was only 44.1%. Among 259 assessments with anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 units/mL, only 67 had an R time in the study-defined 9–13 min “therapeutic” band; 75 were <9 min and 117 were >13 min.
Why the paper mattersIt directly tests whether two clinically plausible UFH-monitoring signals—anti-Xa and viscoelastic R time—track together in a high-risk ICU population.
Best-supported inferenceStandard kaolin TEG R time and anti-Xa should not be assumed to be interchangeable measures of UFH anticoagulant intensity in this population.
Most important cautionThe study-defined R-time categories are exploratory and not clinically validated therapeutic targets; discordance therefore does not prove that anti-Xa or TEG is “wrong.”
Clinical outcomesClinically relevant bleeding occurred in 11.8% and confirmed thromboembolism in 8.9%; outcome analyses were univariable and susceptible to confounding/reverse causation.

The article is most useful as a monitoring-discordance study. It shows that a conventional biochemical measure of heparin effect and a global viscoelastic measure of clot initiation can diverge substantially, even when the anti-Xa is within the institution’s therapeutic range. Statistical significance should not be confused with strong association: r²=0.032 means the fitted linear relationship explained only about 3.2% of R-time variation.

Interpretation is constrained by the retrospective design, physician-selected TEG ordering, evolving COVID-era anticoagulation practice, non-simultaneous paired samples (median separation ~61 min), repeated observations within patients, an exploratory R-time “therapeutic” definition, and unadjusted clinical-outcome analyses. The paper therefore supports caution about substituting TEG R time for anti-Xa, but does not identify an optimal monitoring strategy.

4. Section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame severe COVID-19 as a hypercoagulable ICU syndrome in which thrombosis risk, impaired fibrinolysis, and uncertainty around anticoagulation created interest in viscoelastic testing. Conventional coagulation measures may incompletely describe global hemostasis, whereas TEG captures clot initiation, propagation, strength, and lysis. The clinical gap was whether TEG meaningfully reflects UFH anticoagulant effect when anti-Xa is being used for therapeutic infusion monitoring.

The study question is narrower than “Is TEG useful in COVID-19?”: it asks whether TEG R time tracks anti-Xa during continuous therapeutic UFH. The paper also acknowledges that empiric therapeutic-dose anticoagulation in critically ill COVID-19 later failed to show benefit versus prophylactic strategies, so the study population reflects a historically evolving practice environment.

Methods — design, patients, and treatment context

Single-center retrospective cohort over 15 months. Inclusion required ICU admission, laboratory-confirmed COVID-19, continuous therapeutic UFH for ≥24 h, and at least one paired standard TEG/anti-Xa measurement within ≤2 h. ECMO patients were excluded. TEG 5000 testing used citrated whole blood, kaolin activation, and recalcification; specimens were tested 10 min to 2 h after collection.

Both initiation of therapeutic UFH in patients without known VTE and ordering of TEG were at attending-physician discretion. No standardized TEG-guided UFH protocol existed. During part of the study, clinicians could target the upper anti-Xa therapeutic range (0.5–0.7 units/mL) when respiratory status worsened or TEG appeared hypercoagulable; this practice later ceased after publication of a major ICU COVID-19 anticoagulation trial. That evolving practice is important to interpretation.

Methods — data, definitions, and statistics

Patients contributed up to the first 10 qualifying paired assessments. Anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 units/mL was defined as therapeutic. TEG R time 9–13 min was defined as therapeutic based on prior reports; values below and above were labeled subtherapeutic and supratherapeutic. The coagulation index classified hypercoagulability as >+3 and hypocoagulability as <−3. Confirmed VTE required positive CT or ultrasound; “clinically relevant bleeding” was known or suspected bleeding that prompted interruption of UFH in a physician note.

The primary analysis used Pearson correlation. Category agreement was examined between anti-Xa and R-time bands; other TEG parameters and aPTT were explored. Group comparisons used t-test/Wilcoxon and chi-square/Fisher exact testing as appropriate. No multivariable model was performed for bleeding or thromboembolism because few variables were associated on univariable analysis.

Results

Of 884 patients screened, 237 met criteria, contributing 413 paired assessments. Median age was 60.2 years, weight 93.2 kg, SOFA 5, and 58.7% were male. Median anti-Xa was 0.5 units/mL (IQR 0.3–0.6) and median R time 9.1 min (5.9–15). The median first paired-assessment UFH rate was 12 units/kg/h; median pair separation was 60.9 min (39.4–84.0).

Anti-Xa and R time had a very weak linear relationship (r²=0.032, p<0.0001). Other anti-Xa/TEG relationships were absent to weak: K time r²=0.007, LY30 r²=0.001, α-angle r²=0.050, MA r²=0.036, and coagulation index r²=0.093. aPTT likewise showed essentially no relationship with TEG metrics. Overall category agreement between anti-Xa and R time was 44.1% (182/413). Of 259 therapeutic anti-Xa assessments, 192 (74.1%) were discordant by R-time category. Clinically relevant bleeding occurred in 28/237 (11.8%); confirmed thromboembolism in 21/237 (8.9%). Mortality was higher in patients with confirmed thromboembolism (71.4% vs 47.7%, p=0.038).

Discussion

The authors interpret the low correlation and high category discordance as evidence that anti-Xa and standard TEG R time provide inconsistent signals during UFH therapy. They emphasize the practical problem: a patient with “therapeutic” anti-Xa could appear under- or over-anticoagulated by R time. They do not claim that TEG should replace anti-Xa; instead, they conclude routine TEG use for UFH monitoring remains inconclusive.

The authors also caution against overinterpreting lower anti-Xa values in patients with bleeding or thromboembolism. In bleeding, anti-Xa may have been drawn after UFH interruption, creating reverse temporal interpretation. They note the disease-specific complexity of COVID-19 coagulopathy and argue that discordance itself creates a clinician-interpretation problem rather than resolving which test best reflects desired anticoagulation.

Limitations and conclusion

Author-reported limitations include retrospective outcome ascertainment; broad, documentation-dependent bleeding definition; inability to grade bleeding consistently; selection bias from clinically driven imaging and physician-selected TEG ordering; non-simultaneous paired tests despite the ≤2-h window; and uncertainty around the study-defined 9–13 min therapeutic R-time range. The authors explicitly call the R-time therapeutic categorization exploratory.

The conclusion is appropriately restrained: correlation was low, therapeutic anti-Xa frequently disagreed with R time, routine TEG to assist UFH dosing in COVID-19 ICU patients remains inconclusive, and further study of TEG-based dosing/decision-making is needed.

6. Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. The paper is directly useful for understanding why TEG R time should not be treated as a simple surrogate for anti-Xa during UFH in severe COVID-19. Its strongest contribution is descriptive discordance, not a validated monitoring or dosing strategy.

Relevance

8/10

Rationale: Addresses UFH monitoring in a high-acuity ICU context with direct pharmacotherapy implications.

Evidence basis: 237 patients, 413 paired measurements, clinically familiar monitoring tests.

Limiting factor: COVID-specific population and historical pandemic practice.

Would rise with: Replication in contemporary, non-COVID critical-care UFH populations.

Novelty

7/10

Rationale: Directly compares anti-Xa with TEG R time during therapeutic UFH in this population.

Evidence basis: Authors position it as the first such report in critically ill COVID-19 adults.

Limiting factor: Observational correlation rather than testing a new intervention.

Would rise with: Prospective validation tied to dosing decisions and outcomes.

Method strength

5/10

Rationale: Clear inclusion criteria, paired measurements, predefined ranges, and transparent cohort flow.

Evidence basis: 413 qualifying pairs, ≤2-h pairing rule, explicit laboratory methods.

Limiting factor: Retrospective single-center design, physician-selected TEG, evolving treatment practice, no adjusted outcome model.

Would rise with: Prospective protocolized sampling, prespecified repeated-measures statistics, and external validation.

Evidence strength

5/10

Rationale: Strong enough to establish substantial discordance in this sampled cohort, not enough to define which test better guides therapy.

Evidence basis: r²=0.032 and only 44.1% category agreement.

Limiting factor: Exploratory R-time therapeutic range and lack of outcome-validated monitoring targets.

Would rise with: A prospective strategy trial or outcome-linked validation of R-time thresholds.

External validity

3/10

Rationale: Findings arise from one center during a unique phase of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Evidence basis: TEG ordered at clinician discretion; therapeutic UFH sometimes used without proven VTE; institutional practice changed during study.

Limiting factor: Severe disease-specific coagulopathy and selection of patients thought to need extra coagulation assessment.

Would rise with: Multicenter replication across diverse ICU indications and standardized UFH protocols.

Implementation value

6/10

Rationale: Useful mainly as a guardrail against assuming TEG and anti-Xa are interchangeable.

Evidence basis: Three-quarters of therapeutic anti-Xa measurements were outside the study’s therapeutic R-time band.

Limiting factor: No validated TEG-based dose-adjustment pathway.

Would rise with: A reproducible algorithm showing improved time in target, safety, or thrombotic outcomes.

Independent validity appraisal

Strengths. The study asks a focused question, describes TEG sample handling, defines pairing and categories, provides a clear screening flow, reports the paired-assessment distribution, and presents the discordance matrix rather than relying only on a p value.
Critical limitation: category validity. The practical headline—high discordance—depends on defining R time 9–13 min as “therapeutic.” The authors themselves state that this range is controversial and exploratory. Thus, discordance demonstrates lack of equivalence between the chosen classifications, not misdosing by one test.
Critical limitation: selection and confounding. TEG was ordered at attending discretion, often in patients for whom conventional coagulation tests or clinical status were concerning. Therapeutic UFH could also be used empirically without confirmed VTE. The included cohort is therefore not a neutral sample of all UFH-treated ICU patients.
Model appraisal — repeated measurements. The article reports 413 assessments from 237 patients (median 2, up to 10 per patient) but describes pooled Pearson correlation and standard group tests without a patient-level repeated-measures model. This may overstate precision and mixes within-patient and between-patient information. The source does not report a clustering adjustment.
Clinical-outcome inference is weak. Bleeding and thrombosis analyses were univariable, events were few, imaging was clinically triggered, and the bleeding definition depended on documentation and UFH interruption. The lower anti-Xa seen with bleeding can plausibly reflect measurement after the infusion was stopped, a limitation the authors acknowledge.

7. Practice translation

Use nowUse the study as evidence that standard TEG R time and anti-Xa may diverge substantially during UFH in severe COVID-19 and should not be treated as interchangeable measurements.
Do not use forDo not use this paper alone to titrate UFH to a TEG R-time target, override a therapeutic anti-Xa solely because R time is “discordant,” or define a universal TEG therapeutic range.
Monitoring implicationIf TEG is obtained for global hemostatic assessment, interpret R time in the broader TEG/clinical context rather than as a direct anti-Xa equivalent.
Research implicationThe next useful study would protocolize simultaneous sampling, account for repeated measures, prespecify validated thresholds, and compare TEG-informed vs standard monitoring on meaningful anticoagulation and clinical outcomes.
Clinical translation boundary: This SEA summarizes and appraises the 2023 paper only. It does not independently verify current COVID-19 anticoagulation guidelines, current TEG recommendations, or present-day UFH monitoring standards.
