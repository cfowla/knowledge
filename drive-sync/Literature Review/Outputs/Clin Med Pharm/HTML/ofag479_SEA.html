Scope warning. This is an observational causal-inference study, not a randomized trial. It informs the cardiovascular safety question for switching virally suppressed, treatment-experienced, INSTI-naïve adults without prior cardiovascular disease in Swiss routine care. It does not establish individual-drug cardiovascular effects, biological mechanism, or safety in populations outside those eligibility boundaries.

One-page synthesis

Core finding: Switching to an INSTI-based regimen was not associated with a higher adjusted 6-year cumulative cardiovascular disease risk compared with remaining on non-INSTI ART, but both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses showed a small early excess risk at 1 year.

The analysis included 8,815 people and 539,017 sequential person-trials. In the intention-to-treat analysis, estimated 6-year CVD risk was 4.11% after switching versus 3.97% with continued non-INSTI ART (adjusted risk ratio [aRR] 1.03, 95% CI 0.88–1.16; adjusted risk difference [aRD] 0.13 percentage points, Table 2 CI −0.49 to 0.58). At 1 year, estimated risks were 0.78% versus 0.58% (aRR 1.34, 95% CI 1.03–1.69; aRD 0.20 percentage points, 95% CI 0.02–0.37). Per-protocol estimates followed the same pattern: 1-year aRR 1.44 and aRD 0.26 percentage points, with attenuation by year 6.

The study is methodologically stronger than a conventional exposure cohort because it explicitly aligns eligibility, treatment assignment, and time zero through 167 monthly emulated trials; uses extensive baseline adjustment for the ITT contrast; applies inverse-probability weighting for the per-protocol contrast; and relies on centrally validated CVD outcomes adjudicated blinded to ART history. The main unresolved issue is whether the small early signal is causal. The authors themselves consider residual confounding and reverse causation plausible, and the available manuscript does not include the supplemental weighting diagnostics, full target-trial specification, or full model outputs needed to probe that question more deeply.

Methods

Study design and data

The investigators emulated a sequence of hypothetical randomized trials at the start of each calendar month from November 2011 through September 2025. Individuals could enter multiple trials when repeatedly eligible. This design was chosen to align eligibility, treatment assignment, and follow-up start, thereby addressing time-zero problems that can bias observational comparisons of treatment switching.

The underlying SHCS is an ongoing prospective cohort reported to include approximately 72% of people receiving ART in Switzerland. Follow-up occurs every 6 months for about 90% and every 12 months for about 10% of participants. The cohort collects sociodemographic, behavioral, clinical, laboratory, complete ART, and comedication histories under standardized protocols.

Statistical analysis

The ITT analysis used pooled logistic regression across 167 sequential trials. Time-varying characteristics were carried forward between cohort visits, and missing values were handled as separate categories. Baseline adjustment included age, sex, education, race, HIV acquisition group, family CVD history, years on ART, smoking, BMI, CD4 count and nadir, total cholesterol, kidney function, hypertension, diabetes, TAF, abacavir, antiplatelet therapy, lipid-lowering therapy, and calendar month, with treatment-by-time interaction and linear/quadratic time terms.

For the PP analysis, participants were artificially censored when they switched to the alternative strategy or stopped ART for more than two months. Time-varying non-stabilized inverse-probability weights were used for treatment nonadherence and other censoring, with weights truncated at the 99th percentile. Cumulative risks were standardized across sequential trials. aRRs and aRDs were reported with 95% CIs from 200 non-parametric bootstrap samples.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Exploratory ITT subgroup analyses evaluated sex, age (<65 vs ≥65 years), and baseline abacavir use, using a treatment-by-subgroup interaction and a prespecified interaction p-value threshold <0.1 as suggestive of effect modification. A sensitivity analysis censored at raltegravir initiation because raltegravir was historically used in different clinical circumstances than later INSTIs.

Important unavailable method detail: the uploaded accepted manuscript references the full target-trial protocol (Table S1), full model estimates (Tables S4–S7), subgroup outputs (Table S8), and raltegravir sensitivity outputs (Table S9/Figure S1), but those supplements are not present in the evaluated file. Weight distributions, post-weighting balance, and full coefficient diagnostics therefore cannot be independently appraised here.

Results

Cohort and treatment patterns

From 10,502 individuals followed after November 2011, 8,815 were included. They contributed 539,017 person-trials: 533,267 assigned to continue non-INSTI ART and 5,750 assigned to the INSTI-switch strategy. The median participant contributed 51 trials (IQR 28–86). At trial baseline, median age was 48 years (IQR 41–55), median time on ART was 11 years (IQR 6–16), and median CD4 count was 631 cells/µL (IQR 473–826). Overall, 30.3% were female and 16.0% were of African ancestry.

Among INSTI switches, dolutegravir accounted for 56.8%, elvitegravir 17.9%, bictegravir 12.3%, raltegravir 11.8%, and cabotegravir 1.2%. The largest measured baseline imbalances in Table 1 were TAF use (31.4% switch vs 15.9% non-INSTI; SMD 0.372), abacavir use (33.1% vs 20.2%; SMD 0.295), renal-function distribution (SMD 0.215), and years on ART (SMD 0.142). Most other displayed SMDs were below 0.1.

Cardiovascular events

In the ITT analysis, 507 CVD events occurred at the individual-event level: 145 myocardial infarctions, 125 strokes, and 237 invasive cardiovascular procedures. Across replicated person-trials, the manuscript reports 17,815 events in the non-INSTI arm and 222 in the INSTI arm. In the PP analysis, 462 CVD events were recorded, corresponding to 10,943 non-INSTI-arm and 197 INSTI-arm events across sequential trials.

The time pattern was the central result. At 1 year, INSTI switching had higher adjusted cumulative risk in both ITT and PP analyses. By 2 years the confidence intervals for relative and absolute effects included the null; by 6 years there was no evidence of increased cumulative CVD risk. No significant treatment interactions were reported for age, sex, or baseline abacavir use, and results were reported as unchanged when censoring at raltegravir initiation.

Discussion and interpretation

Authors' interpretation

The authors conclude that switching to INSTI-based ART did not increase long-term CVD risk in well-controlled PWH, while acknowledging a small transient excess during the first year. They argue that slower atherosclerotic mechanisms—such as lipid changes after stopping TDF or INSTI-associated weight gain—fit the rapid early timing poorly. Mean weight gain at one year was reported as 1.2 kg.

They present acute metabolic or hemodynamic effects as speculative alternatives, but also emphasize residual confounding and reverse causation: clinicians may have switched ART in people whose cardiometabolic profile was changing or around planned procedures. The authors state that the findings do not support changes to clinical guidelines at this stage.

Independent interpretation

The long-term result is reasonably reassuring because the ITT and PP estimates converge toward the null and their confidence intervals exclude neither modest harm nor modest benefit. The early signal is less secure as a causal finding. It is small in absolute magnitude, occurs in the period most vulnerable to clinician-driven switching and reverse causation, and is not supported here by a mechanistic analysis.

The class-level exposure also spans several drugs and treatment eras. More than half of INSTI switches involved dolutegravir, while elvitegravir, bictegravir, raltegravir, and cabotegravir contributed smaller shares. Because companion agents differed materially between arms—especially TAF and abacavir—the result should be interpreted as a strategy-level comparison rather than evidence that all INSTIs share the same cardiovascular profile.

Source consistency checks

Exclusion-count discrepancy: Results prose reports 1,687 exclusions but lists 160 + 5 + 80 + 52 + 1,389, which sums to 1,686. Figure 1 shows 53—not 52—for the “not receiving potent ART” category, which makes the total 1,687. This appraisal does not silently choose between the two values.
6-year ITT aRD CI discrepancy: the Results narrative gives a 6-year aRD of 0.13 percentage points with 95% CI −0.49 to 0.50, whereas Table 2 gives −0.49 to 0.58. The structured results in this artifact use the Table 2 value and retain this discrepancy explicitly.
Unit-of-analysis wording: Figure 2's caption describes N=533,267 for the non-INSTI group as “individuals,” while Figure 1 and Table 1 establish that 533,267 is the number of person-trials contributed by 8,521 individuals. This does not change the model estimates but can mislead readers about sample size.
Terminology typo: Table 1/Methods use “eGRF” in places where the surrounding text and CKD-EPI description indicate eGFR. This appears editorial rather than analytic.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Strongly useful for contextualizing INSTI cardiovascular safety and for understanding a well-executed sequential target trial emulation. The 6-year result is reassuring, but the early 1-year signal should remain hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Main strengths

  • National prospective cohort with standardized longitudinal data and centrally adjudicated cardiovascular outcomes.
  • Sequential target-trial design addresses a key time-zero problem in treatment-switch research.
  • Both ITT and PP estimands are presented, with broadly concordant temporal patterns.
  • Effect estimates include absolute risk differences as well as relative risks, preventing overemphasis of the 1-year relative-risk signal.
  • Sensitivity analysis addressing raltegravir's historically different use did not materially change the reported conclusion.

Main limitations

  • Residual confounding remains plausible, especially for why and when clinicians switch ART.
  • The early signal could reflect reverse causation rather than a drug-class effect.
  • INSTI class exposure is heterogeneous and confounded with companion-drug changes; TAF and abacavir were notably more frequent in switch trials.
  • Population excludes prior CVD and requires viral suppression, limiting applicability to higher-risk or uncontrolled-HIV populations.
  • Supplemental protocol, model, weighting, subgroup, and sensitivity details are absent from the uploaded manuscript.
  • Internal manuscript inconsistencies in exclusion counts, one CI, and person-trial wording warrant cautious transcription.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use nowUse as reassuring class-level evidence that switching a stable, virally suppressed, treatment-experienced patient to an INSTI is not associated with a clear long-term excess in composite CVD risk over 6 years in this cohort.
Do not overclaimDo not interpret the 1-year aRR of 1.34 as proof of INSTI-induced acute cardiovascular toxicity; the absolute difference was 0.20 percentage points and residual confounding is a plausible explanation.
Regimen selectionThis study alone does not justify preferring or avoiding a specific INSTI. The exposure was class-based, and companion drugs differed between strategies.
Risk counselingFrame the early signal in absolute terms and within the patient's established cardiovascular risk profile rather than using the relative risk alone.
Guideline impactThe authors explicitly state they do not foresee guideline changes on the basis of current evidence.
Research next stepThe most useful follow-up would better separate specific INSTI effects from regimen changes and clinician-switching indications, while reproducing the early signal in independent populations.
Clinical bottom line: Use cautiously now as reassurance, not as a prescribing rule. The long-term result weakens concern about a sustained INSTI class cardiovascular hazard in this study population. The small 1-year excess should prompt scientific attention, not automatic ART changes.
