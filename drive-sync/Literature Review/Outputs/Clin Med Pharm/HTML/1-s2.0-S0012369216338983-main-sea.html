One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The article links unfractionated heparin's molecular heterogeneity to its variable pharmacokinetics and laboratory response, then uses the clinical evidence available through 1995 to argue for adequate early dosing, reagent-aware monitoring, standardized nomograms, and close attention to bleeding, osteoporosis, and immune HIT. It also explains why LMWHs can produce more predictable anticoagulation: shorter chains retain anti-Xa activity, bind less extensively to proteins/cells, have greater subcutaneous bioavailability, and have longer half-lives.

Why it still matters. Several concepts remain structurally important to contemporary heparin practice: antithrombin-mediated mechanism, chain-length dependence of anti-IIa activity, nonlinear UFH clearance, dose-dependent half-life, APTT reagent variability, the anti-Xa 0.3-0.7 U/mL convention, early use of the 80-U/kg bolus/18-U/kg/h VTE regimen, and recognition of HIT as a highly prothrombotic immune drug reaction. The article is also unusually valuable for tracing where common protocol numbers came from.

Why it is not practice-complete. The review predates DOACs, modern GRADE methods, contemporary HIT diagnostic algorithms and drug options, current VTE treatment strategies, modern assay platforms, and decades of subsequent comparative evidence. Several recommendations are explicitly based on small studies, subgroup analyses, animal data, or historical practice patterns.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction / scope

The authors frame IV heparin as the rapid-onset parenteral anticoagulant of choice for situations requiring immediate effect. They distinguish low-dose prophylaxis from high-dose therapeutic use, state that therapeutic heparin requires frequent monitoring and adjustment, and summarize evidence levels for VTE, acute coronary syndromes, cardiac bypass, angioplasty/stents, and selected disseminated intravascular thrombosis indications.

The chapter also signals the transition occurring in the mid-1990s: LMWHs had begun to demonstrate advantages over UFH for VTE prevention and treatment. The stated purpose is review rather than primary experimentation.

Structure and mechanism of action

UFH is described as a heterogeneous glycosaminoglycan whose principal anticoagulant action depends on a high-affinity pentasaccharide that binds antithrombin III. Activated antithrombin accelerates inhibition of thrombin, factor Xa, and factor IXa. Thrombin inhibition requires a heparin chain long enough to bind both thrombin and antithrombin, whereas factor Xa inhibition does not require this ternary bridge.

The molecular-weight distribution (about 5,000-30,000; mean ~15,000) drives heterogeneity in activity and clearance. Higher-molecular-weight chains are cleared faster, leaving relatively smaller chains in vivo; because those smaller chains can retain anti-Xa activity with less anti-IIa activity, the chapter links molecular heterogeneity directly to anti-Xa/APTT discordance.

Administration, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics

Heparin is not orally absorbed. IV and SC routes are preferred; IM injection is discouraged because of hematoma risk. SC administration has delayed onset and reduced bioavailability at low/moderate doses, so the authors recommend an IV bolus when rapid anticoagulation is needed. Heparin binds multiple plasma proteins, endothelial cells, and macrophages, which contributes to variable recovery and response.

Clearance combines a rapid saturable cellular pathway and a slower nonsaturable component that is partly renal. The resulting nonlinear kinetics produce a dose-dependent half-life: approximately 30 minutes after 25 U/kg IV, 60 minutes after 100 U/kg, and 150 minutes after 400 U/kg. In the cited IV-vs-SC comparison, therapeutic levels/APTT at 24 hours occurred in 71% vs 37%, illustrating how route and early dose affect time to adequate anticoagulation.

Laboratory monitoring and dose-response

The APTT is presented as the usual therapeutic UFH test but with major caveats. Acute-phase factor VIII elevation, other heparin-binding proteins, reagent composition, instrument/clot-detection method, and batch variation all influence APTT response. The authors reject a universal 1.5-times-control threshold because different reagents can map the same APTT ratio to materially different heparin concentrations.

They recommend local APTT calibration to roughly 0.2-0.4 U/mL by protamine titration or 0.3-0.7 U/mL by anti-Xa activity. Anti-Xa is presented as a more direct but historically costlier alternative. At heparin levels high enough for cardiac bypass or some high-risk angioplasty procedures, APTT becomes unsuitable and activated clotting time is used.

Dose, efficacy, and safety relationship

The chapter’s central treatment argument is that failure to achieve adequate anticoagulation early is associated with recurrent VTE. Table 1 summarizes three trials in which high/adequate starting regimens produced lower recurrence than absent, subcutaneous, or lower fixed-dose regimens. Table 3 pools contemporary studies using at least 30,000 U/day and shows an overall recurrence rate of 5.7%.

However, the evidence for an upper APTT boundary that predicts bleeding is much weaker. The authors emphasize patient-related hemorrhage factors and recommend avoiding excessive heparin exposure, especially in high-risk patients. This asymmetry—stronger evidence for avoiding underdosing than for a universal upper target—is a key interpretive point.

Heparin dose-adjustment nomograms

Physician-directed dose adjustment was described as highly variable. Standardized nomograms, including the Raschke weight-based regimen, were developed to improve early target attainment. The cited randomized study found fewer recurrent thromboembolic events with weight-based dosing (reported RR 0.2; 95% CI 0.05-0.91), although the comparator began at a rate the authors considered inadequate.

The familiar regimen in Table 4—80 U/kg bolus followed by 18 U/kg/h—was paired with APTT-based rate adjustments. The authors explicitly warn that the seconds/ratio thresholds cannot simply be transplanted across laboratories because APTT reagents differ in heparin sensitivity.

Other indications and bleeding

For cardiac bypass and other settings requiring very high heparin concentrations, the article describes activated clotting time monitoring. For unstable angina and acute MI, it discusses APTT-based management but acknowledges that a therapeutic range had not been validated for those specific indications.

Bleeding is identified as heparin’s major complication. In thrombolysis-era MI studies, bleeding rose with excessive APTT prolongation; the review also notes increased bleeding when heparin was combined with powerful antiplatelet agents. These observations are context-dependent and substantially historical.

Osteoporosis

The review argues that long-term high-dose heparin can cause clinically important osteoporosis, while brief courses are unlikely to do so. The evidence base is heterogeneous: case reports/series, small randomized trials, and observational pregnancy cohorts.

Reported signals include 1/20 clinical osteoporosis in one small antenatal trial, 6/40 spinal fractures with 10,000 U twice daily versus 1/40 with a comparator anti-Xa regimen in another study (p≈0.054), and radiographic osteopenia in 12/70 pregnant women receiving heparin. The authors regard ≥3 months of high-dose exposure as a meaningful risk window, but acknowledge uncertainty in true incidence.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia

The chapter distinguishes benign early nonimmune thrombocytopenia from immune HIT, correctly emphasizing the latter as a prothrombotic rather than primarily hemorrhagic disorder. Typical onset is 5-15 days (median ~10), with rapid-onset presentations after recent exposure. The platelet nadir is often moderate rather than profound.

Pathogenesis is described as IgG-mediated platelet activation through Fc-gamma receptors, with heparin-PF4 identified as the major antigenic complex. Prospective therapeutic-dose UFH studies in Table 5 total 978 patients with 29 HIT cases (3.0%) and 9 HIT-plus-new-thrombosis cases (0.9%). The treatment section is historically limited to agents such as danaparoid and ancrod and therefore requires major modern updating.

Other adverse effects

The article describes injection-site skin lesions, heparin skin necrosis, hypersensitivity reactions, rare hypoaldosteronism with hyperkalemia, and case reports of priapism. Skin necrosis and abrupt systemic reactions after IV heparin are linked to HIT antibodies in some patients.

The hypoaldosteronism discussion is notable because the authors distinguish plausibility from certainty: only case reports were available, but reversibility after heparin withdrawal and rechallenge evidence supported a causal association.

Low-molecular-weight heparins

LMWHs are presented as a then-new class produced by depolymerizing UFH. Their shorter chain distribution shifts activity toward factor Xa relative to thrombin (anti-Xa:anti-IIa ratios about 2:1 to 4:1), reduces binding to plasma proteins and endothelial cells, increases subcutaneous bioavailability, and lengthens half-life by roughly two- to fourfold. Renal clearance becomes more important, and half-life increases in renal failure.

Clinical evidence available in 1995 suggested effective VTE prevention and treatment, practical fixed/weight-adjusted dosing, and in some settings less bleeding than UFH. The authors also stress that commercial LMWHs differ and were not necessarily interchangeable. Their 1995 statements on cost, availability, HIT management, and the place of LMWH relative to other anticoagulants are now historical rather than current practice guidance.

2026 currency check — external verification, separate from the source

Adult VTE treatment has moved substantially beyond the 1995 framework. ASH’s currently surfaced adult DVT/PE guideline is the 2020 guideline; it prefers DOACs over VKAs for many patients and states that when warfarin is used, UFH/LMWH overlap should continue for at least 5 days and until the INR is therapeutic. This makes the review’s approximately 4-day overlap statement outdated.

HIT treatment is also materially different. ASH’s currently surfaced HIT guideline recommends stopping heparin and starting a non-heparin anticoagulant; contemporary options include argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, danaparoid where available, and selected DOACs. The chapter’s danaparoid/ancrod discussion is therefore historical.

Several UFH protocol concepts remain recognizable but their evidence base is imperfect. Later CHEST/practical-management guidance continued to use weight-based initial dosing and the anti-Xa 0.3-0.7 U/mL convention, while explicitly noting weak clinical-outcome validation of therapeutic monitoring ranges and the need for laboratory-specific APTT calibration.

Preserve as foundational
  • Antithrombin-binding pentasaccharide mechanism.
  • Chain-length dependence of anti-IIa vs anti-Xa activity.
  • Nonlinear UFH clearance and dose-dependent half-life.
  • APTT reagent variability and need for local calibration.
  • Historical origin of the 80 U/kg + 18 U/kg/h VTE regimen.
  • HIT as an immune, highly prothrombotic adverse reaction.
Require contemporary verification
  • Exact APTT/anti-Xa therapeutic ranges and assay implementation.
  • UFH-vs-LMWH-vs-DOAC selection.
  • Warfarin overlap duration.
  • HIT diagnostic testing and treatment agents.
  • Procedural ACT targets.
  • Pregnancy, renal impairment, obesity, and special-population dosing.
Do not copy forward
  • Ancrod-era HIT treatment protocols.
