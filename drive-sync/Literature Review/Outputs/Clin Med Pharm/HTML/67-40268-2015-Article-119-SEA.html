One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Serious neurologic and vascular complications after epidural corticosteroid injection appear uncommon in the published literature but can be catastrophic. The review cannot estimate their true incidence. The authors place particular concern on vascular penetration and intra-arterial injection, especially with particulate corticosteroids.

The review searched six named databases or resources, limited eligibility to English-language original articles from a 20-year period, and added case reports. The Results prose says 3,255 papers were identified and 162 met inclusion criteria. Figure 1 says 159 were included, and the reported 58 studies plus 101 case reports also totals 159. The review reports data from more than 100,000 ESIs in prospective or retrospective studies, but the serious events that drive its safety concern come mainly from case reports and a physician survey rather than from denominator-rich cohorts.

Minor events varied by approach and study. Interlaminar cervical studies reported dural puncture from 0.07% to 2.6% and vasovagal reactions from 0.04% to 1.7%. Two large lumbar transforaminal cohorts reported intravascular penetration near 7.4% to 7.9%. The Discussion cites broader transforaminal intravascular rates of 9% to 32.8%, with 19.4% for cervical, 8.1% for lumbar, and 21.3% at S1 in one cited study. These are secondary-reported values from heterogeneous studies, not pooled estimates.

The prevention discussion favors fluoroscopic guidance, contrast injection, live rather than static fluoroscopy, attention to contrast-flow patterns, and non-particulate corticosteroids. Digital subtraction angiography and test dosing are presented as possible safeguards, not guarantees. One cited case remained catastrophic despite both. The authors also note that the evidence base had weak safety ascertainment, incomplete procedural detail, heterogeneity, and probable underreporting.

Source integrity. The paper contains several internal numerical conflicts. Selection counts differ between prose and Figure 1. Some percentages in Tables 1 and 3 do not match the printed event counts if the listed injection total is used as denominator. Infection-frequency statements also differ materially across sections. This appraisal preserves those discrepancies instead of correcting them.

Section condensation

Abstract and key points

The abstract frames ESI as a common treatment for radicular back pain with rare reports of devastating harm. It states that serious complications appear uncommon but can be severe, and that true incidence remains unclear. The key points stress vascular penetration, contrast use, fluoroscopy, digital subtraction angiography, blunt needles, test dosing, anatomy, and operator familiarity.

The abstract already contains the review's main limitation. The literature has incomplete documentation, unreported data, and inherent bias. The authors call for large registries and stronger observational studies.

1 Introduction

The introduction explains the inflammatory rationale for corticosteroid injection and outlines transforaminal, interlaminar, and caudal routes. It notes that the interlaminar route is less target-specific and that transforaminal delivery may place medication closer to the affected nerve root. Imaging options include fluoroscopy, ultrasound, and CT, but the paper says comparative safety evidence was limited.

The section is background, not evidence generated by this review. It relies on cited studies to describe technique and possible efficacy.

2 Risk versus efficacy of ESI

The authors summarize conflicting efficacy reviews. Several sources found short-term pain relief with limited or absent long-term benefit, while other reviews supported cervical interlaminar or lumbar transforaminal use in selected settings. The paper also cites the FDA's 2014 warning about rare serious neurologic events.

This section sets up the risk-benefit problem but does not perform a new efficacy meta-analysis. The safety review should not be read as a comparative efficacy synthesis.

3 Methods

The authors state that they followed PRISMA and used a standardized protocol. Eligible publications were English-language original articles from September 1994 through September 2014 reporting complications related to cervical or lumbar ESI and specifying the injection approach. Searches covered PubMed MEDLINE, Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library, with bibliography hand-searching.

Two authors assessed citations independently and without blinding. The paper does not report a formal risk-of-bias instrument, evidence-certainty grading, duplicate data-extraction procedure, protocol registration, or pooled statistical method for complication incidence.

4 Results

The Results prose reports 3,255 initial papers and 162 included. It then reports 58 studies, 38 with complications and 20 without, plus 101 case reports. The 58 plus 101 total is 159, which matches Figure 1 rather than the prose count of 162. The approach-specific sections summarize interlaminar cervical, transforaminal cervical, interlaminar lumbar, and transforaminal lumbar cohorts, then a large set of case reports.

Serious outcomes in case reports include infarction, spinal-cord injury, hematoma, infection, blindness, cardiopulmonary arrest, and death. Minor cohort-reported events include headache, pain, flushing, vasovagal reactions, dural puncture, intravascular uptake, and transient neurologic symptoms. Most study-level estimates are heterogeneous and unpooled.

5 Discussion

The authors state that the review covers more than 100,000 ESIs in prospective or retrospective studies. They categorize adverse-event mechanisms as direct structural or vascular injury, intravascular drug administration, and local or systemic reactions. The discussion focuses on vascular uptake and particulate embolic risk, while also covering hematoma, dural puncture, HPA-axis suppression, blood pressure, glucose, bone effects, infection, and the 2012 fungal outbreak.

The prevention section recommends fluoroscopy, two-plane confirmation, contrast, live fluoroscopy, recognition of flow patterns, and non-particulate corticosteroids. It discusses blunt needles, test doses, and digital subtraction angiography. The authors explicitly acknowledge that these measures cannot eliminate risk and that evidence for route-specific risk is insufficient.

Limitations and 6 Conclusions

The authors identify incomplete documentation, incidental safety reporting, heterogeneous outcomes, missing procedural details, and likely underreporting of short-term and long-term steroid adverse effects. These limitations directly weaken any attempt to infer incidence.

The conclusion calls ESI relatively safe but says major-complication incidence remains obscure. It attributes a large share of serious events to vascular penetration or intra-arterial administration and recommends technical risk-reduction measures plus more research.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. This is useful as a historical map of ESI safety signals and as a citation index for older reports. It should not be used alone for current incidence estimates, current regulatory status, or current procedural standards.

Relevance 8/10

The source directly addresses clinically important ESI complications and procedural risk reduction.

Evidence basis. Large safety-focused review, approach-specific tables, and extensive case-report bibliography.

Limit. The evidence and regulatory context stop in 2014 to 2015.

Would change the score. A current systematic review with modern procedural practice and current labels or guidance would raise practical relevance.

Novelty 6/10

For its publication period, the review assembled a broad safety record across approaches and rare-event case reports.

Evidence basis. Three approach tables, a case-report map, and discussion of vascular mechanisms.

Limit. Much of the content is a synthesis of previously published reports without new pooled analysis.

Would change the score. New denominator-based comparative incidence estimates or validated mechanistic data would raise novelty.

Method strength 4/10

The search is broad and eligibility rules are stated, but key systematic-review safeguards are missing or unreported.

Evidence basis. Named databases, date window, PRISMA statement, and two independent unblinded reviewers.

Limit. English-only restriction, no reported risk-of-bias tool, no certainty grading, no pooled method, and internal count inconsistencies.

Would change the score. Protocol registration, duplicate extraction, formal bias assessment, and prespecified synthesis methods would raise the score.

Evidence strength 4/10

The review supports the existence and plausible mechanisms of rare harms better than it supports their frequency.

Evidence basis. Large aggregate exposure in cohorts plus many case reports and some route-specific complication rates.

Limit. Catastrophic events are mainly case reports or survey reports, with under-ascertainment and unclear denominators.

Would change the score. Prospective registries with active safety capture and standardized denominators would raise the score.

External validity 5/10

The review spans several techniques and settings but combines heterogeneous studies from a 20-year historical period.

Evidence basis. Cervical and lumbar, interlaminar and transforaminal approaches, multiple corticosteroids and imaging methods.

Limit. Practice patterns, drug preparations, imaging, and safety protocols have changed since the search ended in 2014.

Would change the score. Contemporary multicenter data using standardized definitions would raise the score.

Implementation value 6/10

The paper gives concrete procedural precautions but does not establish how much each precaution reduces risk.

Evidence basis. Contrast, live fluoroscopy, two-plane confirmation, non-particulate steroid preference, and informed consent are discussed.

Limit. Recommendations are mostly based on observational evidence, mechanistic reasoning, and case reports.

Would change the score. Comparative safety studies or registry analyses of specific safeguards would raise the score.

Usually none. This was not a comparative safety meta-analysis.

Practice boundary. The source supports historical safety signal recognition. Current clinical use requires separate review of current guidance, labeling, procedural standards, and patient-specific risk. No current verification was performed in this run.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use this source for

Finding older safety literature, understanding why intravascular uptake matters, and tracing case reports of catastrophic neurologic events.

Do not use it for

A current major-complication incidence, a modern particulate versus non-particulate risk estimate, or a stand-alone clinical policy.
