One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Two physician-selected, weight-based IV heparin protocols with different PTT targets produced similar times to early therapeutic anticoagulation in this retrospective cohort, while protocol violations were associated with clinically meaningful delays in selected time-to-target outcomes.

What the source reports. Median time to PTT above the lower limit was 6.0 h with the standard protocol and 6.3 h with the lower-target protocol (p=0.24). Median time to PTT within target was 14.4 h and 14.3 h, respectively (p=0.93). Eighty percent of each group had reached an in-range PTT within 24 h. Major bleeding was rare (1 event in 100 patients), and no recurrent DVT/PE was reported in the current cohorts.

Independent appraisal. The equality claim is limited by nonrandomized protocol selection, very different indication mixes, a small convenience sample, low event counts, single-center assay-specific PTT targets, and absence of individual 1996 data for statistical historical comparison. The strongest reusable finding is operational: compliance failures—especially wrong infusion adjustments and mistimed/missed PTTs—were common and associated with slower target attainment in some analyses.

Internal source inconsistency. Table 1 labels the lower-target PTT range as 60–120 s, while the Introduction and Appendix 2 specify 60–100 s. The appraisal preserves this discrepancy rather than choosing one silently.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors re-evaluated their institution's weight-based IV heparin approach after substantial protocol and laboratory changes since a 1996 evaluation. In 100 consecutive evaluable patients, the two 2006 protocols had nearly identical median times to PTT above the lower target boundary and within the therapeutic range, with infrequent adverse events.

Introduction

The standard protocol was intended for active thromboembolic disease and linked anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 units/mL to PTT 70–130 s. A lower-target protocol was expanded beyond the cardiac care unit to ACS, neurologic, and early postoperative indications without active DVT/PE/peripheral arterial thrombosis; the narrative links anti-Xa 0.2–0.5 units/mL to PTT 60–100 s. The authors sought a contemporary institutional reassessment because both dosing increments and PTT assay sensitivity had changed.

Methods

The retrospective, open-label study ran for 5 months. Physicians chose the protocol based on indication. Patients treated for less than 24 h or with deliberate protocol modifications were excluded, except that a bolus could be omitted. PTT data were collected for the first 48 h or longer if target was not reached; initial PTT values were included in efficacy analysis only when obtained 4 to under 8 h after initiation.

Major bleeding was prespecified using a composite clinical definition (overt bleeding plus a major hemoglobin drop, transfusion threshold, or critical-site hemorrhage). Protocol adherence was audited for the first 48 h. A convenience sample of 50 patients per protocol was chosen to match the 1996 study; Mann–Whitney U tests compared current protocols. Because the individual 1996 data were unavailable, historical comparisons were qualitative.

Results

Fifty-three of 153 reviewed patients were excluded. The two current cohorts differed in indication: DVT/PE predominated in the standard group, while ACS predominated in the lower-target group. Primary efficacy outcomes were statistically indistinguishable: 6.0 vs 6.3 h to exceed the lower limit and 14.4 vs 14.3 h to enter the therapeutic range. Eighty percent in each group had an in-range PTT within 24 h.

Protocol violations occurred in 29% of current-study patients. In the lower-target group, violations were associated with a longer median time to exceed the lower limit (11.1 vs 6.3 h; p=0.04); in the standard group, violations were associated with a longer time to enter the therapeutic range (22.0 vs 12.8 h; p=0.017). One major bleed occurred; no recurrent DVT/PE was reported in the two current cohorts. A standard-protocol death possibly related to PE occurred after prolonged subtherapeutic PTT and two documented protocol violations, but cause was not confirmed.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpreted the two 2006 protocols as comparably effective and safe for rapid anticoagulation and noted apparently faster performance than the 1996 protocol. They emphasized protocol adherence and reported that initially extreme PTT values above 200 s required more rate adjustments under the standard protocol. Bolus subgroup analyses did not show statistically significant differences, but the authors explicitly treated them as underpowered and hypothesis-generating.

Important limitations include retrospective nonprobability sampling, physician-selected treatment, different indication distributions, small sample size, short primary efficacy observation, sparse safety events, incomplete historical data, and possible documentation error. External validity is further weakened because the institution changed its PTT reagent/method and revised both protocols shortly after the study.

Conclusion

The paper's conclusion is that both protocols appeared equally efficacious and safe, with protocol adherence critical to rapid anticoagulation. The evidence supports this as an institutional performance description; it does not establish equivalence between dosing strategies or current generalizable dosing targets.

Validity and interpretation

The observed similarity in median PTT attainment does not demonstrate clinical equivalence. The groups were not comparable by indication, assignment was discretionary, and the sample was not powered for rare clinical outcomes. The more defensible conclusion is that both order sets achieved similar early laboratory target times in this particular institutional cohort.

The compliance association is more actionable but still observational. A violation may be both a cause and marker of difficult anticoagulation. Still, the timing and direction of selected differences—especially the longer time to target when dose adjustments or PTT timing were incorrect—support attention to execution reliability.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Treat PTT targets as assay-specific.The paper explicitly tied PTT ranges to local anti-Xa calibration and later changed targets after a reagent change. Do not transplant the historical seconds-based ranges into another system.
2. Adherence is a major design target.Wrong infusion changes and mistimed/missed PTTs were the dominant violations and were associated with delayed target attainment in selected analyses.
3. Separate protocol logic from workflow reliability.The paper's strongest operational lesson is not that one target is superior, but that even a detailed nomogram can fail through execution errors.
4. Do not infer bolus dispensability.The bolus analyses were underpowered, nonrandomized, and explicitly described by the authors as hypothesis-generating.
5. Safety evidence is weak.One major bleed and no recurrent DVT/PE cannot support a robust safety comparison between protocols.
6. Historical order sets are not current authority.The institution modified the protocols shortly after this study; use these appendices for design history and failure-mode analysis, not direct bedside dosing.
