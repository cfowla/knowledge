One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. The assay-validation part is the strongest reason to keep this paper. The treatment cohorts are useful proof-of-principle signals, not comparative efficacy evidence.

plasmaMATCH tested whether a blood-based genomic screen could do more than describe tumour biology. It had to return results reliably, agree with another ctDNA method and with tissue, find actionable mutations, and move patients into matched treatment cohorts. The trial registered 1051 patients. Among 1044 registered for ctDNA testing, 1034 received a result. Digital PCR and targeted sequencing had high gene-level agreement. Digital PCR sensitivity against advanced-disease tissue sequencing was 93% overall and 98% with contemporaneous tissue. Targeted sequencing sensitivity was 95% overall and 100% with contemporaneous tissue.

The therapeutic signal was uneven. Cohort B, HER2-mutant disease treated with neratinib with fulvestrant when hormone-receptor positive, had a 25% confirmed response rate. Cohort C, AKT1-mutant hormone-receptor-positive disease treated with capivasertib plus fulvestrant, had a 22% response rate. These cohorts met or exceeded their prespecified activity thresholds. Extended-dose fulvestrant in ESR1-mutant disease produced an 8% response rate and capivasertib monotherapy in the AKT or PTEN basket produced an 11% response rate, so cohorts A and D did not meet their activity targets.

Best use
ctDNA clinical-validity evidence, trial-platform design, assay-to-treatment workflow, and early evidence for rare HER2 and AKT1 targets.
Do not use for
Head-to-head treatment selection, current standard-of-care claims, or precise effect estimates for rare genomic subgroups.
Main limitation
Small non-randomised treatment cohorts with wide intervals and investigator-assessed response.
Source issue worth remembering
The abstract labels 4 of 20 grade 3-4 diarrhoea events as 25%. Main Results and the supplement support 20%.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction and research context

The paper starts from a practical problem. Advanced breast cancers evolve after diagnosis, so archival tumour tissue may miss acquired ESR1, HER2, AKT1, or other targetable changes. Repeat metastatic biopsy is invasive and samples one site. ctDNA could provide a current, multi-site genomic signal from a blood draw, but clinical adoption requires reliable assay performance and evidence that mutation calls can direct useful therapy.

The authors framed plasmaMATCH as a clinical-validity and clinical-utility experiment. They searched prior ctDNA literature and noted that liquid-biopsy studies had not yet shown, at this scale, that ctDNA screening could efficiently feed mutation-matched breast-cancer cohorts without confirmatory tumour testing.

Methods

Eligible women had measurable advanced breast cancer, ECOG 0 to 2, adequate expected survival, and prior treatment for advanced disease or early relapse after perioperative chemotherapy. The platform used central droplet digital PCR and later added Guardant360 targeted sequencing. A positive result by either ctDNA assay could assign therapy. Cohort A used extended-dose fulvestrant for ESR1 mutations. Cohort B used neratinib, plus fulvestrant for oestrogen receptor-positive disease, for HER2 mutations. Cohort C used capivasertib plus fulvestrant for AKT1-mutant oestrogen receptor-positive disease. Cohort D used capivasertib monotherapy for selected AKT1 or PTEN alterations.

Confirmed objective response by RECIST version 1.1 was the primary endpoint. The study also measured duration of response, clinical benefit, progression-free survival, safety, mutation frequency, assay agreement, tissue concordance, cohort entry, and selected pharmacokinetics. Imaging occurred every 8 weeks through week 32 and every 12 weeks later. There was no independent central response review. Each cohort used a single-stage A’Hern design with one-sided alpha 5% and 80% power.

Results

ctDNA screening scaled well. Results were available for 1034 of 1044 screening registrations. Median turnaround was 13 days for digital PCR and 10 days for targeted sequencing. The platform found a mutation or amplification in 533 patients and a cohort-eligible target in 357. Figure 1 shows that 136 patients entered a treatment cohort through ctDNA testing. Gene-level kappa values between digital PCR and targeted sequencing ranged from 0.89 to 0.93. Concordance with tissue was strongest when the tissue biopsy and blood sample were close in time.

Cohorts B and C crossed their activity thresholds. Cohort B had 5 responses among 20 evaluable patients and median progression-free survival of 5.4 months. Cohort C had 4 responses among 18 and median progression-free survival of 10.2 months. Cohort A had 6 responses among 74 and median progression-free survival of 2.2 months. Cohort D had 2 responses among 19 and median progression-free survival of 3.4 months. The D basket result separated sharply by alteration. Two of 6 AKT1-mutant patients responded, while none of 13 patients with PTEN alterations responded.

Discussion

The authors argue that ctDNA can replace tissue sequencing for many mutation-detection tasks in advanced breast cancer. The data support the narrower statement well. Blood testing returned results in nearly every registered screening patient, the two ctDNA methods agreed closely, and contemporaneous tissue comparison showed high sensitivity. The paper also makes a sensible boundary explicit. Tissue remains useful for immunohistochemistry and copy-number assessment.

The treatment cohorts support target-specific signals rather than broad therapeutic claims. Neratinib activity in HER2-mutant disease and capivasertib activity in AKT1-mutant disease are the clearest positive findings. Extended-dose fulvestrant did not rescue ESR1-mutant disease, and capivasertib monotherapy did little in PTEN-altered disease. The authors acknowledge heavy prior treatment, lack of PIK3CA targeting, and other design limits.

Clinical validity

The assay question is answered better than the treatment question. Prospective result availability was 99%. Orthogonal ctDNA methods agreed closely. Tissue comparisons showed high sensitivity, especially for contemporaneous samples. These are practical clinical-validity data, not just analytic validation.

Treatment evidence

There is no randomized comparator, so response rates must be read against prespecified single-arm activity thresholds and prior literature. Cohorts B and C cleared those thresholds. Cohorts A and D did not. The small denominators make subgroup claims unstable. A 33% response in 6 AKT1-mutant cohort D patients is interesting, but it is still 2 responses.

Safety

The safety profile matches the drug classes and was clinically relevant. The most common grade 3-4 events were increased gamma-glutamyltransferase in cohort A, diarrhoea in cohort B, fatigue in cohort C, and rash in cohort D. Investigators reported 17 serious adverse reactions in 11 patients and one treatment-related death from grade 4 dyspnoea in cohort C.

Source-integrity findings. The abstract reports 4 of 20 grade 3-4 diarrhoea events as 25%, while the main Results and Supplementary Table S5 support 20%. The primary article also calls Guardant360 a 73-gene panel, while the supplementary methods call it a 74-gene panel. Neither discrepancy was silently reconciled.

Implementation and practice takeaways

ctDNA workflow
The trial shows that a centralized blood-based screen can return results for nearly all registered patients and can feed treatment assignment within about 2 weeks.
Timing matters
Agreement with tissue was better when tissue and blood were collected within 60 days. Older tissue is a weaker reference for an evolving metastatic genome.
Rare targets need separate evidence
HER2 mutation and AKT1 mutation showed treatment activity. ESR1 mutation did not make higher-frequency fulvestrant effective, and PTEN alteration did not predict useful capivasertib monotherapy activity here.
Keep tissue in the pathway
The article itself limits the replacement claim. Immunohistochemistry and copy-number assessment still require tissue in many cases.

The cleanest reuse of this paper is in a clinical evidence model that separates assay validity, molecular eligibility, treatment assignment, response, and safety. Do not collapse those into one claim that "liquid biopsy works." The trial supports each layer to a different degree.
