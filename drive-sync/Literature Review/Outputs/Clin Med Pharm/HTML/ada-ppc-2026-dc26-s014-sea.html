One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-implementation-value pediatric diabetes chapter with 84 recommendations, a practical new-onset treatment algorithm, and a condition-by-condition complication matrix.

The chapter deliberately separates care that applies to all children and adolescents with diabetes from type 1-specific and type 2-specific care. Cross-cutting priorities are developmentally appropriate DSMES, individualized nutrition and physical activity, psychosocial and social-determinant screening, family involvement without premature transfer of responsibility, school/daycare support, reproductive counseling, and diabetes technology access. For type 1 diabetes, the chapter emphasizes early CGM, AID/open-loop pump options, individualized A1C/CGM goals, exercise safety, and school support. For type 2 diabetes, it emphasizes risk-based screening, autoantibody testing when phenotype overlaps, chronic-care lifestyle treatment, early pharmacotherapy, metformin/insulin pathways for initial severity, GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors when goals are unmet, and selective metabolic surgery.

Complication surveillance is operationalized in Table 14.1: dyslipidemia, hypertension, nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, MASLD, sleep apnea, PCOS, cardiovascular risk, autoimmune thyroid disease, and celiac disease. The source also addresses tobacco/nicotine, alcohol, cannabis, and transition to adult care. Evidence strength is heterogeneous. The chapter itself flags limited pediatric evidence for type 2 DSMES, A1C diagnostic validation, ideal CGM time-in-range targets, direct surgery-versus-modern-medication comparisons, and transition interventions.

Section condensation

Introduction and source architecture

The ADA states that pediatric diabetes care cannot simply be derived from adult care because epidemiology, pathophysiology, development, and treatment response differ. The chapter is organized around introductory type 1/type 2 material, guidance common to pediatric diabetes, type 1-specific care, type 2-specific care, complication screening/treatment, substance use, and transition to adult care.

Type 1 diabetes remains the most common pediatric form, while youth-onset type 2 diabetes is increasing and is characterized as more aggressive than adult-onset type 2 diabetes. The TODAY follow-up is cited to underscore early microvascular burden, and the chapter emphasizes social, cultural, obesity, family-history, sex, gestational-diabetes, and social-determinant context.

Care for all children and adolescents

DSMES is framed as an ongoing family-centered process rather than a one-time educational event. Nutrition therapy should be individualized to growth, culture, resources, food access, and diabetes therapy; physical activity targets 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily with strengthening activity at least three days weekly, while insulin-treated youth require glucose monitoring and hypoglycemia prevention plans.

Psychosocial care is integrated into routine diabetes care, including screening for distress, depression, anxiety, disordered eating, family factors, food and housing insecurity, health literacy, finances, social support, school performance, and age-appropriate time alone with clinicians. The chapter reports substantial pooled burdens of distress, depression, and anxiety, strengthening the implementation case for routine screening.

Type 1 diabetes

Technology recommendations support CGM at diagnosis or as soon as possible, AID for capable users, open-loop pumps when AID cannot be used, and school support for prescribed technology. Glycemic goals are individualized: <7% A1C is appropriate for most youth, with both less stringent and more stringent goals depending on hypoglycemia, treatment burden, safety, and feasibility. CGM metrics over the recent 14 days or longer are recommended alongside or instead of A1C when possible.

Exercise management emphasizes individualized glucose targets, rapid-acting carbohydrate availability, insulin adjustment, and recognition of delayed/nocturnal hypoglycemia. The chapter also flags uncertainty in the ideal pediatric time-in-range target and notes emerging evidence that shorter CGM sampling periods can still be representative.

Type 2 diabetes

Risk-based screening begins after puberty or age 10 in youth with overweight/obesity plus risk factors, with repeat testing at least every two years if normal. Fasting glucose, OGTT, symptomatic random glucose, and A1C can be used, while pancreatic autoantibodies are recommended when type 2 diabetes is suspected in youth with overweight/obesity to exclude autoimmune type 1 diabetes. The chapter explicitly acknowledges pediatric validation limitations for A1C.

Pharmacologic care is severity-driven. Metformin is first-line for incidental/asymptomatic A1C <8.5%; marked hyperglycemia without acidosis receives long-acting insulin while metformin is initiated; DKA requires insulin until acidosis resolves; and HHS should be assessed at glucose ≥600 mg/dL. When goals are not met on metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists and/or SGLT2 inhibitors are considered, with noninsulin therapies maximized before escalating insulin when feasible. The chapter also discusses pediatric RCT evidence for GLP-1 agents, tirzepatide, and SGLT2 inhibitors and selective metabolic surgery, while noting the absence of randomized surgery comparisons with current treatment options.

Complications and associated conditions

Table 14.1 consolidates recommendations 14.43–14.79. It specifies lipid screening and treatment thresholds, blood-pressure definitions and ACE inhibitor/ARB treatment, annual or duration-based kidney screening and albuminuria treatment, retinopathy and neuropathy screening intervals, annual MASLD assessment in type 2 diabetes, sleep-apnea and PCOS evaluation, weight/CVD risk management, and autoimmune thyroid and celiac screening in type 1 diabetes.

Supporting narrative describes early atherosclerotic risk, the practicality of non-HDL cholesterol, limited pediatric lipid intervention data, kidney risk, lower early retinopathy risk, AI-enabled retinal screening, and a 7% distal peripheral neuropathy prevalence in 1,734 adolescents with type 1 diabetes. This section contains several practice-ready thresholds but also relies partly on extrapolation and lower-grade evidence.

Substance use and transition

The chapter calls for screening for tobacco/nicotine vaping, substance use, and alcohol, discourages recreational cannabis, and highlights delayed hypoglycemia after alcohol. It reports substantial cannabis exposure among adolescents with type 1 diabetes and describes hyperglycemic ketosis–cannabis hyperemesis as a diagnostic consideration in hyperglycemic emergencies.

Transition preparation should begin in early adolescence and at least one year before anticipated transfer, with coordinated pediatric/adult team support and shared decision-making. The source emphasizes that there is no age-specific cutoff for transfer and acknowledges that intervention research remains limited, despite evidence that fragmentation during transition can worsen care and outcomes.

Pancreatic autoantibodies:

Dsmes, Nutrition, Physical Activity, Exercise, School/Child Care

  • 14.1 Children and adolescents with diabetes, and their parents or caregivers (for individuals aged <18 years), should receive comprehensive, culturally sensitive, and developmentally appropriate individualized diabetes self-management education and support according to reference standards at diagnosis and routinely thereafter. B
  • 14.2 Provide individualized medical nutrition therapy for children and adolescents with prediabetes or diabetes, emphasizing key nutrition principles (i.e., more nonstarchy vegetables, whole fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds, and low-fat dairy products and less sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets, meat, refined grains, and processed or ultraprocessed foods), B as an essential component of the overall treatment plan.
  • 14.3 Monitor carbohydrate intake, whether by carbohydrate counting or experience-based estimation, as a key component to optimizing glycemic and weight management for children and adolescents with diabetes. B
  • 14.4 Educate children and adolescents and their caregivers on the potential need to adjust insulin with high-fat and high-protein meals. Meal composition affects postprandial glucose excursions. A
  • 14.5 In children and adolescents with diabetes, provide comprehensive nutrition education at diagnosis, and at least annually and ideally more frequently, by an experienced registered dietitian nutritionist to assess the eating pattern in relation to weight status, age-appropriate growth, risk for disordered eating behaviors, and cardiovascular disease risk factors. E
  • 14.6 Physical activity is recommended for all children and adolescents with diabetes with the goal of 60 min of moderate- to vigorous-intensity aerobic activity daily, with vigorous musclestrengthening and bone-strengthening activities at least 3 days per week. B
  • 14.7 Children and adolescents with diabetes using insulin therapy and their parents should monitor glucose levels before, during, and after physical activity and receive education about personalized glycemic goals according to the type and intensity of the planned physical activity. C
  • 14.8 Children and adolescents with diabetes and their parents or caregivers should be educated on strategies to prevent hypoglycemia during, after, and overnight following physical activity or exercise. Treatment for hypoglycemia should be accessible before, during, and after engaging in activity. C

Psychosocial Care, Social Determinants, Family Involvement, Adolescent Autonomy And Reproductive Counseling

  • 14.9 At diagnosis and during routine follow-up care, screen children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes, B type 2 diabetes, B and other forms of diabetes E for psychosocial concerns (e.g., diabetes distress, depressive symptoms, anxiety, disordered eating behaviors), family factors, and behavioral health concerns that could affect diabetes management with ageappropriate standardized and validated tools. Refer to a qualified behavioral health professional, preferably experienced in childhood diabetes, when indicated.
  • 14.10 Behavioral health professionals should be considered integral members of the pediatric diabetes interprofessional team. E
  • 14.11 Encourage developmentally appropriate family involvement in diabetes management tasks for children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, B and other forms of diabetes, E recognizing that premature or unsupportive transfer of diabetes care responsibility to children and adolescents can contribute to diabetes distress, lower engagement in diabetes self-management behaviors, and deterioration in glycemia.
  • 14.12 Health care professionals should screen for food insecurity, housing stability, health literacy, financial barriers, and social or community support and apply that information to treatment decisions. E
  • 14.13 Health care professionals should consider asking school-aged children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes, B type 2 diabetes, E and other forms of diabetes, E along with their parents or caregivers, about social adjustment (peer relationships) and school performance to determine whether further intervention is needed.
  • 14.14 Offer adolescents with diabetes time by themselves with their health care professional(s) at a developmentally appropriate age. E
  • 14.15 During adolescence, generally during the stage of pubertal growth and development, incorporate reproductive health and preconception counseling into routine diabetes clinic visits for all individuals of childbearing potential because of the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes in this population. A

Type 1 Diabetes Technology, School Support, Glycemic Goals, Cgm And Exercise

  • 14.16 Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) should be offered for diabetes management at diagnosis or as soon as possible in children and adolescents with diabetes who are capable of using the device safely (either by themselves or with caregivers). A The choice of device should be made based on the individual’s and family’s circumstances, desires, and needs.
  • 14.17 Offer automated insulin delivery (AID) systems for diabetes management to children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes who are capable of using the device safely (either by themselves or with caregivers). Choice of device should be made based on the individual’s and family’s circumstances, desires, and needs. A
  • 14.18 Offer open-loop insulin pump therapy for type 1 diabetes management to children and adolescents on multiple daily injections who are capable of using the device safely (either by themselves or with caregivers) if unable to use AID systems. Choice of device should be made based on the individual’s and family’s circumstances, desires, and needs. A
  • 14.19 Students with diabetes must be supported at school in the use of diabetes technology, including CGM, insulin pumps, connected insulin pens, and AID systems, as prescribed by their diabetes care team. C
  • 14.20 A1C goals must be individualized and reassessed over time. An A1C of <7% (<53 mmol/mol) is appropriate for most children and adolescents with diabetes. B
  • 14.21 Less stringent A1C goals (such as <7% [<53 mmol/mol] or <7.5% [<58 mmol/mol]) may be appropriate for children and adolescents with diabetes who cannot articulate symptoms of hypoglycemia; have hypoglycemia unawareness; cannot access advanced insulin delivery technology and/or CGM; cannot check blood glucose regularly; or have nonglycemic factors that increase A1C. B
  • 14.22 Even less stringent A1C goals may be appropriate for children and adolescents with diabetes and a history of severe hypoglycemia or limited life expectancy or where the harms of pediatric diabetes, may be warranted. Such professionals can provide individualized, evidence-based behavioral health care services, including cognitive-behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based approaches, and other interventions, to improve psychosocial functioning in children and adolescents with diabetes (59–61). The complexities of diabetes management require ongoing caregiver involvement in care throughout childhood and adolescence. Developmentally appropriate, supportive family teamwork between the growing child or adolescent and their parent(s) can help increase parent involvement in diabetes management and maintain or improve A1C in children and adolescents with A1C ≥8.0% (62). It is appropriate to inquire about diabetesspecific family relationships, including family teamwork and conflict, during visits; health care professionals can both help families negotiate a plan and refer to an appropriate behavioral health professional for more in-depth support (63). Such professionals can conduct further assessments and deliver evidence-based behavioral interventions to support developmentally appropriate, collaborative family involvement in diabetes self-management (64,65). Monitoring of social adjustment (peer relationships) and school performance can facilitate both well-being and academic achievement (66,67). Diabetes management and glycemic levels may be associated with and impact academic progress and students’ functioning in the school setting, which highlights the need for appropriate accommodations and access to diabetes-related support in school (68). Decision-making involvement with children and adolescents regarding the adoption of management plan components can improve participation in diabetes selfmanagement, technology adoption, and glycemic outcomes (69,70). Use of welldesigned decision aids can engage children and adolescents in comprehensive, unbiased conversations with their diabetes care team about treatment options (71). Creating self-care contracts (72) and integrating technology that shares glucose data with the care team may facilitate shared decision-making and enhance care (73). Importantly, health care professionals working with children and adolescents who are not yet able to provide legal consent must balance clinical oversight with promoting developmentally appropriate independence. Recommendations include Current standards for diabetes management reflect the need to minimize hyperglycemia as safely as possible. The Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) (79), which did not enroll children <13 years of age, demonstrated that near normalization of blood glucose levels was more difficult to achieve in adolescents than in adults. Nevertheless, the increased use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and AID systems, goal setting, and improved education have been associated with more children and adolescents reaching the blood glucose goals recommended by the ADA (80–82), particularly in families in which the parents or caregivers as well as the child or adolescent with diabetes participate jointly to perform the required diabetes-related tasks. TYPE 1 DIABETES IN CHILDREN AND ADOLESCENTS Lower A1C in adolescence and young adulthood is associated with a lower risk and rate of microvascular and macrovascular complications (83–85) and demonstrates the effects of metabolic memory (86–89). While an A1C goal of <7% (<53 mmol/mol) is appropriate for most children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes, as AID algorithms continue to improve and access to these systems expands, children and adolescents who can attain a goal of <6.5% (<48 mmol/mol) without adverse effects should be encouraged to do so. In addition, achieving lower A1C levels is likely facilitated by setting lower A1C goals (80,90). Lower goals may be possible during the honeymoon phase of type 1 diabetes and in those with access to CGM and AID. The honeymoon phase, also referred to as partial remission, refers to a transient period of increased endogenous insulin secretion shortly after diagnosis when initiation of insulin therapy resolves glucotoxicity and allows for improved insulin secretion from residual β-cells. Recent data with newer devices indicate that the risk of hypoglycemia at lower A1C is less than it was before (81,82,91–94). However, special consideration should be given to the risk of hypoglycemia in young children (aged <6 years) who are often unable to recognize, articulate, and/or manage hypoglycemia. Registry data indicate that lower A1C goals can be achieved in children, including those aged <6 years, without increased risk of severe hypoglycemia (91). Please refer to section 6, “Glycemic Goals, Hypoglycemia, and Hyperglycemic Crisis,” for more information on glycemic assessment. A strong relationship exists between the frequency of glucose monitoring and glycemic outcomes (95,96). Glucose levels for all children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes should be monitored multiple times daily using CGM whenever possible. For those who cannot access CGM and in case of inaccurate CGM readings, access to finger-stick blood glucose monitoring remains essential. Use of CGM soon after type 1 diabetes diagnosis is associated with improved A1C (97–99). Parents, caregivers, and children and adolescents should be offered initial and ongoing education and support for CGM use. Behavioral support may further improve ongoing CGM use (100). Metrics derived from CGM include percent time in range (TIR), time below target range (<70 mg/dL and <54 mg/dL), and time above target range (>180 mg/dL and >250 mg/dL) (101). While studies indicate a relationship between TIR and A1C (102,103), it is still uncertain what the ideal goal TIR should be for children, and further studies are needed. Though 14 days of CGM data has long been considered the gold standard, recent data indicate that 10 or more days of CGM data provides a reliable assessment of glycemia and that ≥7 days of data may be sufficient for children and adolescents attaining TIR goals (104,105). Please refer to section 7, “Diabetes Technology,” for more information on the use of blood glucose meters, CGM, and insulin pumps. More information on insulin injection technique can be found in section 9, “Pharmacologic Approaches to Glycemic Treatment.” In addition, type 1 diabetes and chronic hyperglycemia are associated with adverse effects on cognition during childhood and adolescence (7,106,107). Several factors, including young age, severe hypoglycemia at <6 years of age, DKA, and chronic hyperglycemia (106,108,109), may contribute to adverse effects on brain development and function. Attaining higher CGM TIR is associated with better structural brain development and better neurocognitive outcomes (108), further emphasizing the importance of achieving glycemic goals (7,108). Key Concepts in Setting Glycemic Goals • Glycemic goals should be individualized, and lower goals may be reasonable based on a benefit-risk assessment. • Blood glucose goals should be modified in children with frequent hypoglycemia or hypoglycemia unawareness. Exercise Needs Specific to Children and Adolescents With Type 1 Diabetes Real-world data from the Type 1 Diabetes Exercise Initiative Pediatric (T1DEXIP) study reported that glucose levels tended to drop during exercise, most particularly in those with lower A1C, shorter diabetes duration, and less fear of hypoglycemia (110). Furthermore, glucose levels tended to be lowest at about 8–16 h postexercise in those with similar characteristics, and nocturnal hypoglycemia was more frequent when activity levels were higher and longer in duration (111). These data underscore the need for individualized management strategies for children and adolescents using insulin, including common sense precautions and understanding how to make insulin dose adjustments when undertaking exercise and physical activity. Accessible rapid-acting carbohydrates and frequent blood glucose monitoring before, during, and after exercise, with or without CGM maximize safety with exercise. As hyperglycemia can occur before, during, and after physical activity, it is important to ensure the elevated glucose level is not related to insulin deficiency, as that can lead to worsening hyperglycemia with exercise and DKA risk. Intense treatment are greater than the benefits. B

Type 2 Diabetes Screening, Diagnosis, Lifestyle And Glycemic Goals

  • 14.25 Consider risk-based screening for prediabetes and/or type 2 diabetes after the onset of puberty or after 10 years of age, whichever occurs earlier, in children with overweight (BMI ≥85th to <95th percentile) or obesity (BMI ≥95th percentile) and who have one or more additional risk factor for diabetes (see Table 2.5 for evidence grading of other risk factors).
  • 14.26 If screening is normal, repeat screening at a minimum of 2-year intervals E or more frequently if BMI is increasing. C
  • 14.27 Fasting plasma glucose, 2-h plasma glucose during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test, elevated random glucose with symptoms of hyperglycemia, and/or A1C can be used to test for prediabetes or type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents. B
  • 14.28 Children and adolescents with overweight or obesity with hyperglycemia in whom the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes is being considered should have a panel of pancreatic autoantibodies tested to exclude the possibility of autoimmune type 1 diabetes. B
  • 14.29 Provide children and adolescents with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes and their families with developmentally and culturally appropriate comprehensive lifestyle programs integrated with diabetes management to achieve at least a 7–10% decrease in excess weight. B
  • 14.30 Given the necessity of longterm weight management for children and adolescents with diabetes, lifestyle intervention should be based on a chronic care model and offered in the context of diabetes care. E
  • 14.31 For children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes, glycemic status should be assessed at least every 3 months or as frequently as clinically indicated. E
  • 14.32 Consider setting an A1C goal of <6.5% (<48 mmol/mol) for most children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes who have a low risk of hypoglycemia. For those at higher risk of hypoglycemia, A1C goals should be individualized as clinically appropriate. C

Type 2 Diabetes Pharmacotherapy And Metabolic Surgery

  • 14.33 Initiate pharmacologic therapy with consideration of multiple add-on therapies early on, in addition to behavioral counseling for healthful nutrition and physical activity changes, at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. A
  • 14.34 In individuals with incidentally diagnosed type 2 diabetes (A1C <8.5% [<69 mmol/mol] and asymptomatic), metformin is the initial pharmacologic treatment of choice unless contraindicated by kidney function. A
  • 14.35 Children and adolescents with marked hyperglycemia (A1C ≥8.5% [≥69 mmol/mol]) without acidosis at diagnosis should be treated initially with long-acting insulin while metformin is initiated and titrated. B
  • 14.36 Initiate subcutaneous or intravenous insulin treatment in individuals with ketoacidosis to rapidly correct the hyperglycemia and the metabolic derangement. Once acidosis is resolved, metformin should be initiated while subcutaneous insulin therapy is continued. A
  • 14.37 In individuals presenting with severe hyperglycemia (blood glucose ≥600 mg/dL [≥33.3 mmol/L]), consider assessment and treatment as needed for hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state. A
  • 14.38 If individualized glycemic goals are not achieved or maintained with metformin (with or without long-acting insulin), glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) and/or sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) should be considered in children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes of approved ages. A
  • 14.39 For children and adolescents with type 2 diabetes not meeting individualized glycemic goals, consider maximizing noninsulin therapies (metformin, GLP-1 RA, and SGLT2i) before initiating and/or intensifying the insulin therapy plan. E
  • 14.40 In individuals with type 2 diabetes initially treated with insulin and metformin and/or other glucoselowering medications who are meeting glucose goals based on blood glucose monitoring or CGM, reducing or discontinuing insulin should be considered. B
  • 14.41 Consider metabolic surgery for the treatment of adolescents with type 2 diabetes who have class 2 obesity or higher (BMI 35 to <40 kg/m2 or 120% to <140% percentile for age and sex, whichever is lower) and who have elevated A1C and/or serious comorbidities despite lifestyle and pharmacologic intervention. A
  • 14.42 Metabolic surgery for adolescents with type 2 diabetes should be performed only by an experienced surgeon working as part of a wellorganized and engaged interprofessional team, including a surgeon, endocrinologist, registered dietitian nutritionist, behavioral health specialist, and nurse. A

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance 10/10

Rationale: Directly addresses pediatric type 1 and type 2 diabetes care across education, technology, pharmacotherapy, complications, psychosocial care, and transition.

Evidence basis: The chapter contains 84 operational recommendations spanning routine pediatric diabetes practice.

Limiting factor: Relevance is high for pediatric diabetes but narrower for adult-only practice.

Would change: Broader applicability beyond pediatric diabetes would raise cross-domain relevance; a narrower local use case would lower it.

Novelty 7/10

Rationale: The 2026 chapter incorporates newer pediatric pharmacotherapy, diabetes technology, and revised organization while retaining established pediatric care principles.

Evidence basis: Source narrative includes newer SGLT2 inhibitor trials, tirzepatide data, CGM/AID guidance, and autonomous retinal screening.

Limiting factor: Many foundational recommendations are updates of established standards rather than novel primary evidence.

Would change: Major new outcome trials or materially changed recommendations would raise novelty.

Method strength 8/10

Rationale: ADA uses an expert committee and graded recommendations, but this exact chapter does not contain the full guideline-development methods.

Evidence basis: Recommendation grades are shown throughout and underlying evidence is cited extensively.

Limiting factor: The separate Introduction and Methodology is outside the supplied source, limiting chapter-only appraisal of search/selection methods.

Would change: Direct review of the governing methodology and evidence tables would raise confidence.

Evidence strength 7/10

Rationale: Evidence is substantial but heterogeneous: some recommendations are grade A/B, while important pediatric areas rely on C/E evidence and indirect or limited data.

Evidence basis: The chapter explicitly notes gaps in DSMES evidence for type 2 diabetes, A1C diagnostic validation, ideal pediatric TIR, surgery comparisons, and transition interventions.

Limiting factor: Pediatric RCT and long-term outcome evidence remains sparse for several decisions.

Would change: More pediatric comparative-effectiveness trials and long-term safety/outcome data would raise the score.

External validity 8/10

Rationale: The guidance spans type 1 and type 2 diabetes, developmental stages, family/social context, and multiple clinical settings.

Evidence basis: Recommendations address school/daycare, marginalized populations, family resources, and transition to adult care.

Limiting factor: It is an ADA/US-oriented practice standard and some therapeutic availability/regulation is jurisdiction-dependent.

Would change: Direct validation across health systems and jurisdictions would raise external validity.

Implementation value 10/10

Rationale: The chapter provides thresholds, screening intervals, medication pathways, school/technology guidance, complication surveillance, and a new-onset treatment algorithm.

Evidence basis: Figure 14.1 and Table 14.1 translate major decisions into operational workflows and condition-specific actions.

Limiting factor: Implementation still requires local formularies, regulatory status, staffing, and individualization.

Would change: Local protocol mapping and current regulatory/formulary verification would further increase implementation readiness.

Bottom line: Use this chapter as a core pediatric diabetes practice reference and protocol-mapping source. Do not treat its publication date as proof of current regulatory status, local formulary access, or local policy alignment without separate verification.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build around developmental stage
DSMES, family responsibility, psychosocial screening, school support, and transition planning are recurring infrastructure—not add-ons.
Operationalize technology early
CGM at diagnosis/as soon as possible, AID when safe and feasible, and explicit school support are high-value implementation points.
Use severity-driven type 2 pathways
Initial A1C and metabolic decompensation determine metformin/insulin intensity; autoantibodies matter when phenotype overlaps.
Turn Table 14.1 into reminders
Screening age/duration, annual intervals, LDL/BP/UACR/eGFR thresholds, and referral triggers are suitable for CDS/checklist implementation.
Preserve evidence gaps
Do not overstate pediatric evidence where the chapter itself flags limited validation, comparative data, or long-term safety/outcomes.
Verify regulatory facts separately
The chapter’s medication-approval statements are source-date facts; current regulatory/formulary status requires a separate verification workflow.
