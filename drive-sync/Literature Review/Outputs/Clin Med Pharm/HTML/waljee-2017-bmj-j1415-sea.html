One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. The paper is a high-value corticosteroid safety and stewardship reference. The strongest use is to counter the assumption that a brief outpatient course is harmless. Do not use it as standalone causal proof or as a patient-specific NNH calculator.

The study followed 1,548,945 commercially insured adults aged 18-64 from 2012 through 2014. Of these, 327,452, or 21.1%, filled at least one short-term oral corticosteroid prescription. Typical exposure was brief but not trivial. Median use was 6 days, median prednisone-equivalent dose was 20 mg/day, and 23.4% of users received at least 40 mg/day. Upper respiratory infection, spine conditions, and allergy were the most common linked diagnoses. Family practice and internal medicine accounted for most linked prescribers.

The main safety signal came from the self-controlled case series. During days 5-30 after a prescription, adjusted incidence rate ratios were 5.30 for sepsis, 3.33 for venous thromboembolism, and 1.87 for fracture. The risks fell in days 31-90 but remained above baseline for most comparisons. Elevated 5-30 day rates were also seen in the less-than-20 mg/day stratum. Sensitivity analyses that excluded non-oral corticosteroid users, changed the prescription-date window, matched diagnoses, and stratified by respiratory or musculoskeletal indications broadly preserved the signal.

The main weakness is causal identification. Claims data capture prescriptions filled, not ingestion, and do not measure all clinical reasons or time-varying severity that can drive both treatment and an adverse event. The cohort also excludes adults older than 64 and people outside commercial insurance. The paper therefore supports caution with discretionary short courses, not a claim that every observed excess event was caused by corticosteroids.

Methods and design

Population and exposure

Clinformatics DataMart supplied deidentified private-insurance enrollment, medical claims, and pharmacy claims. The investigators started with 2,234,931 adults aged 18-64 who were continuously enrolled from 2012 through 2014 and also required 2011 enrollment to establish prior corticosteroid exposure and comorbidity history.

They excluded 293,456 people with oral corticosteroid use in 2011, 102,243 who only received non-oral corticosteroids or oral budesonide, 224,658 with malignancy or transplant, 28,540 with at least 30 cumulative days of oral corticosteroids, and 37,089 with a prior study adverse event. The final cohort was 1,548,945.

Outcome and analysis

The three acute safety outcomes were hospital admission for sepsis, venous thromboembolism, and fracture. Venous thromboembolism and fracture could be identified in inpatient or outpatient claims. The cohort analysis reported incidence per 1000 person-years and 5-90 day absolute risks after a clinic visit.

The self-controlled case series compared each patient with themselves. Risk windows were days 5-30 and 31-90 after the prescription, with days 5-180 before the prescription as reference. The primary design excluded a four-day window around the fill date, counted only the first event, excluded recent pre-prescription hospitalization, and adjusted for outcome-specific concomitant drug classes using conditional Poisson regression.

Sensitivity analyses

  • Matched-diagnosis 30-day comparison with age, sex, and race adjustment.
  • Respiratory versus musculoskeletal indication strata.
  • Exclusion of concomitant non-oral corticosteroid users.
  • Seven-day instead of four-day prescription-date exclusion window.

Results

Use patterns

Short courses were common. 327,452 adults, 21.1% of the cohort, received at least one qualifying prescription. Median duration was 6 days, 47.4% received at least 7 days, median prednisone-equivalent dose was 20 mg/day, and 23.4% received at least 40 mg/day. Methylprednisolone 6-day dose packs accounted for 46.9% of all prescriptions.

Use varied across groups and geography. The Pacific region had the lowest stated regional use at 12.4%, while East South Central had the highest at 29.4%. The most frequent linked diagnoses were upper respiratory infection, spine conditions, and allergy. Family Practice accounted for 45.3% of linked prescribers and Internal Medicine for 17.6%.

Absolute risk and incidence

During days 5-90 after a clinic visit, sepsis occurred in 0.05% of users versus 0.02% of non-users, venous thromboembolism in 0.14% versus 0.09%, and fracture in 0.51% versus 0.39%. Table 2 also shows higher crude incidence rates among users across most demographic strata.

Within-person risk

The primary 5-30 day adjusted IRRs were 5.30 for sepsis, 3.33 for venous thromboembolism, and 1.87 for fracture. During days 31-90, the respective IRRs were 2.91, 1.44, and 1.40. The lower-dose stratum, less than 20 mg/day prednisone-equivalent, still had 5-30 day IRRs of 4.02, 3.61, and 1.83.

The dose strata did not form a clean monotonic dose-response pattern. Very low doses were uncommon, and the authors specifically state that risks at those doses could not be adequately assessed. This limits a simple interpretation that a numeric dose threshold defines safety.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance9/10

Directly relevant to outpatient corticosteroid safety and medication stewardship.

Evidence basis
1.55 million insured adults, prescribing patterns, and acute safety outcomes.
Main limit
The source addresses one exposure class and three acute outcomes.
Would change the score
Broader outcome capture or contemporary replication would raise it.
Novelty8/10

The study quantified acute harms after brief courses, an exposure that was less characterized than chronic steroid use.

Evidence basis
Large claims cohort plus within-person risk windows and dose strata.
Main limit
The study is from 2012-2014 claims data and is no longer temporally new.
Would change the score
A modern replication with richer clinical data would raise current novelty.
Method strength7/10

The design combines a large cohort with a self-controlled case series and several sensitivity analyses.

Evidence basis
Within-person comparison, conditional Poisson models, drug adjustment, matched-diagnosis analysis, indication strata, and alternate windows.
Main limit
Claims-based exposure and outcome definitions cannot remove all time-varying confounding or misclassification.
Would change the score
Prospective exposure ascertainment or a design with stronger control of indication and time-varying confounding would raise it.
Evidence strength6/10

The association is consistent and large for sepsis and venous thromboembolism, but the observational design does not establish causality.

Primary 5-30 day IRRs were 5.30 for sepsis, 3.33 for venous thromboembolism, and 1.87 for fracture, with similar sensitivity analyses.

Would change the score

Would change the score
Replication in older and more diverse populations would raise it.
Implementation value8/10

The paper gives a concrete safety signal that can inform stewardship of discretionary short courses.

Evidence basis
Common indications, prescriber specialties, dose patterns, absolute risks, and risk windows are all reported.
Main limit

Would change the score

Main strengths

  • Large national cohort with over 1.5 million adults.
  • Within-person self-controlled analysis reduces confounding from stable individual characteristics.
  • Multiple sensitivity analyses address diagnosis-related health-care contact, concomitant non-oral steroid use, indication families, and risk-window definition.
  • The appendix makes exposure normalization and outcome coding reproducible.

Main limitations

  • Observational claims data cannot eliminate residual time-varying confounding or confounding by indication.
  • Filled prescriptions do not establish that patients took the drug as prescribed.
  • Commercially insured adults aged 18-64 do not represent older adults or all US populations.
  • Very low doses were uncommon, so safety at those doses remains uncertain.
  • Only three acute event categories were evaluated, and sepsis required hospitalization.
Do not overclaim. The IRRs are not randomized treatment effects. The paper does not prove a causal dose-response relation, and it does not provide a general patient-level NNH that can be transported without assumptions.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use nowUse the study as a safety signal when weighing a discretionary short steroid course whose expected benefit is small or uncertain.
MonitorThe highest relative risk period in this study was the first month after the prescription. Risk did not return immediately to baseline in the 31-90 day window.
AvoidDo not call a six-day course risk-free simply because it is brief or because the prednisone-equivalent dose is below 20 mg/day.
Pair withCurrent disease-specific guidance and patient-specific risk assessment are still needed to decide whether a corticosteroid is indicated.
Best use in CDS or stewardship: flag marginal-indication outpatient steroid courses for a benefit-risk check, rather than converting the reported IRRs into an automatic contraindication.
