One-page synthesis

Waljee and colleagues examined 1,548,945 commercially insured adults aged 18-64 years who were continuously enrolled from 2012 through 2014 after a 2011 baseline year. Short-term oral corticosteroid exposure was defined as a filled outpatient prescription with cumulative exposure under 30 days. Of the final cohort, 327,452 people (21.1%) received at least one short course. Median use was 6 days (IQR 6-12) at a median prednisone-equivalent dose of 20 mg/day (IQR 17.5-36.8); six-day methylprednisolone dosepaks accounted for 46.9% of prescriptions.

The central safety analysis used a within-person SCCS. Relative to the 5-180 days before initiation, event rates were highest 5-30 days after initiation: sepsis IRR 5.30 (95% CI 3.80-7.41), venous thromboembolism 3.33 (2.78-3.99), and fracture 1.87 (1.69-2.07). Associations attenuated but remained elevated for the full cohort at 31-90 days. Elevated 5-30 day risks were also seen below 20 mg/day prednisone equivalent: sepsis 4.02, VTE 3.61, and fracture 1.83. Diagnosis-matched comparisons, condition-stratified SCCS analyses, removal of concomitant non-oral corticosteroid users, and use of a seven-day exclusion window produced broadly consistent findings.

Methods

Cohort construction

The source used Clinformatics DataMart. Adults 18-64 years with continuous 2012-2014 enrollment and a 2011 baseline year were eligible. Exclusions removed prior 2011 oral corticosteroid users, non-oral-only corticosteroid or oral budesonide users, transplant or malignancy, cumulative oral exposure of 30 days or more, and a 2011 history of the selected adverse events. The flow diagram reduces 2,234,931 initially identified adults to 1,548,945 in the final cohort.

Exposure and outcomes

Filled pharmacy claims defined exposure; doses were normalized to prednisone equivalents. Sepsis required an inpatient primary diagnosis, while VTE and fracture used acute inpatient and outpatient claims. The appendix supplies the ICD-9-CM code sets and dose-conversion table. The first corticosteroid prescription was linked to the nearest outpatient claim within three days to infer indication and provider specialty; 85.0% of users were linked to a diagnosis and 65.9% to a prescribing physician.

Self-controlled case series

The SCCS compared 5-30 and 31-90 day post-initiation periods with a 5-180 day pre-initiation reference period, excluding a four-day window around the prescription date. Only the first event was counted, patients hospitalized within 14 days before initiation were excluded, and time-varying concomitant medication classes were modeled. Fixed conditional Poisson regression estimated IRRs. Sensitivity analyses addressed healthcare-contact bias, diagnostic misclassification, concomitant non-oral steroid exposure, and the exclusion-window definition.

Results

Use patterns

Users were slightly older and had more comorbidity than non-users. Use varied geographically: 12.4% in the Pacific region versus 29.4% in East South Central and 27.6% in West South Central. The most common linked indications were upper respiratory infection (15.8%), spine conditions (11.2%), and allergy (10.5%). Family practice (45.3%) and internal medicine (17.6%) were the two most common linked specialties.

Absolute risks

Across 5-90 days after a clinic visit, sepsis occurred in 0.05% of users versus 0.02% of non-users, VTE in 0.14% versus 0.09%, and fracture in 0.51% versus 0.39%. These between-person absolute differences are small, while the within-person relative associations were concentrated early after treatment.

Dose strata

Five-to-30-day IRRs remained elevated across the three prednisone-equivalent dose categories. For less than 20 mg/day, IRRs were 4.02 for sepsis, 3.61 for VTE, and 1.83 for fracture. For 20-39 mg/day they were 7.10, 2.83, and 1.95; for at least 40 mg/day they were 4.98, 4.15, and 1.77. The non-monotonic pattern does not support a simple dose-response inference.

Sensitivity analyses

Condition-stratified 5-30 day estimates remained elevated for respiratory and musculoskeletal presentations. Removing concomitant non-oral corticosteroid users yielded IRRs of 4.84 for sepsis, 3.29 for VTE, and 1.92 for fracture. Expanding the peri-prescription exclusion window to seven days yielded 8-30 day IRRs of 4.33, 2.94, and 1.65, respectively. Appendix Table 5 also showed elevated adjusted odds in several diagnosis-matched comparisons, although some allergy/bronchitis estimates were imprecise or not statistically significant.

Limitations and interpretation

The study does not establish that corticosteroids caused the events. The SCCS controls stable person-level factors, but indication severity, intercurrent illness, and other time-varying factors can still confound risk periods. Outcome ascertainment depends on claims codes, and the first-prescription indication/provider linkage can misclassify clinical context. The study also could not characterize very-low-dose risk well, examined only three acute adverse-event categories, and found no monotonic dose-response pattern.

External validity is limited by commercial insurance, exclusion of adults 65 years or older, and 2012-2014 US practice patterns. The signal is clinically important because exposure was common and associations were temporally strongest soon after initiation, but the study should be used as a safety signal and prescribing-caution source rather than as a current treatment guideline.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best used to understand the epidemiologic safety signal around short oral corticosteroid courses and the design strengths/limits of a claims-based SCCS. Do not use it alone to determine current indications, dosing, or monitoring.

Method strength 7/10

Rationale: The SCCS design, time-varying medication adjustment, and multiple sensitivity analyses strengthen causal interpretation without eliminating confounding.

Evidence basis: Within-person reference periods, fixed conditional Poisson models, matched-diagnosis analysis, and alternate exclusion windows.

Limiting factor: Claims-based exposure/outcome definitions and residual time-varying confounding remain.

Would raise/lower: Prospective designs, negative controls, richer clinical covariates, and validated outcomes would raise the score.

Evidence strength 7/10

Rationale: Associations were large, temporally concentrated, dose-stratum consistent, and robust to several sensitivity analyses.

Evidence basis: 5-30 day IRRs: sepsis 5.30, VTE 3.33, fracture 1.87; sensitivity analyses remained elevated.

Limiting factor: Observational association does not establish causation, and no monotonic dose-response was demonstrated.

Would raise/lower: Convergent prospective or quasi-experimental evidence would raise the score.

External validity 6/10

Rationale: The nationwide insured cohort is broad but excludes adults 65 years or older and people outside commercial insurance.

Evidence basis: Nationwide US claims with more than 1.5 million adults.

Limiting factor: Commercial insurance, age restriction, and 2012-2014 care patterns narrow generalization.

Would raise/lower: Replication in Medicare, Medicaid, uninsured, and international populations would raise the score.

Implementation value 8/10

Rationale: The findings support careful indication selection, shortest feasible exposure, and attention to serious early adverse events.

Evidence basis: Risks emerged within 5-30 days and remained elevated even below 20 mg/day prednisone equivalent.

Limiting factor: The study does not define a safe dose, treatment alternative, monitoring protocol, or current guideline recommendation.

Would raise/lower: Current guideline/label synthesis and intervention trials would raise implementation value.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use the paper for
Recognizing that short courses are not biologically or clinically risk-free and that early serious events can occur.
Do not use the paper for
Declaring a causal effect, a safe prednisone-equivalent threshold, or a current guideline recommendation.
Prescribing implication
Require a clear indication, avoid unnecessary repeat exposure, and minimize dose/duration when corticosteroids are clinically warranted.
Evidence gap
Older adults, non-commercially insured populations, very-low-dose exposure, and prospective causal evidence remain incompletely addressed by this source.
