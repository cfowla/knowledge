One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this five-year IVIG cohort, immediate adverse reactions were uncommon overall but clustered in patients receiving high-dose therapy for autoimmune disease. In the selected autoimmune/adult regression subgroup, female sex, neuromuscular disease, and higher cumulative IVIG dose were independently associated with patient-level reactions; at the infusion level, female sex, age below 60 years, and neuromuscular disease were associated, while dose per infusion was not.

The study recorded adverse reactions in 4.9% of patients and 2.5% of infusions. The strongest adjusted patient-level associations were cumulative dose ≥7 g/kg (OR 12.259, 95% CI 4.280–35.108) and neuromuscular disease (OR 6.914, 95% CI 2.485–19.237). Male versus female sex had OR 0.156, consistent with higher risk in women. Infusion-level neuromuscular disease remained associated (OR 4.635), age ≥60 years was associated with lower odds (OR 0.235), and dose ≥0.45 g/kg per infusion was not associated (OR 1.045, 95% CI 0.598–1.821).

The most common immediate reaction phenotypes were fever and headache; among neuromuscular-disease patients with reactions, headache was especially frequent. The data are useful for risk awareness, but the retrospective single-center design, heterogeneous indications and cumulative doses, selected regression subgroup, and uncertain handling of within-patient dependence limit causal interpretation and prediction. The paper should not be used alone to change IVIG dose or infusion practices.

Source integrity issue. The Results prose reports 3 of 360 low-dose patients with adverse reactions, whereas Table 3 contains four patient-level adverse-reaction events. This artifact preserves the conflict and does not choose a corrected total.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame IVIG as generally well tolerated but note that risk factors for infusion reactions are incompletely established. The study retrospectively evaluated demographic, diagnostic, dosing, infusion, and reaction data over five years.

They report reactions in 4.9% of patients and 2.5% of infusions and identify sex, neuromuscular disease, cumulative patient dose, and age-related patterns as the principal signals. The abstract concludes that neuromuscular disease emerged as a significant risk factor.

Introduction

The introduction reviews IVIG use across immunodeficiency, autoimmune, inflammatory, and other conditions and distinguishes common mild transient reactions from rare severe complications. It argues that broad-cohort risk-factor data remain limited.

The stated objective is to identify factors associated with IVIG reactions to support safer and more comfortable administration; the study is exploratory observational risk-factor work rather than a prospective prediction study.

Patients and Methods

All IVIG recipients at one Japanese university hospital from May 2014 through December 2018 were included. Infusions followed manufacturer-recommended rates, with a slow start around 0.01 mL/kg/min and escalation over one hour to a maximum prescribed rate below 0.06 mL/kg/min. Clinical information came from multiple medical-record sources using a uniform collection sheet.

An immediate adverse reaction required occurrence during infusion and physician diagnosis. Severity categories were defined by discomfort, need to interrupt or treat, sequelae, and prolonged treatment. Analyses used chi-square/Wilcoxon comparisons and logistic regression with ORs and 95% CIs. The final risk-factor models concentrated on patients aged ≥15 years with autoimmune disease.

Results — population and exposure

The cohort included 748 patients and 4,070 infusions. Autoimmune disease accounted for 377 patients and 2,856 infusions; neuromuscular diagnoses included CIDP, GBS, and myasthenia gravis. Hypogammaglobulinemia and sepsis were major non-autoimmune indications.

Exposure differed markedly by indication: average cumulative dose per patient was 3.7 g/kg in autoimmune disease versus 0.6 g/kg in hypogammaglobulinemia and 0.4 g/kg in sepsis. This dose–indication structure is central to interpreting crude adverse-reaction rates.

Results — adverse reactions and risk factors

Overall immediate reaction rates were 4.9% by patient and 2.5% by infusion. Autoimmune-disease patients had higher crude incidence than hypogammaglobulinemia or sepsis. In the high-dose cohort, women and patients aged ≥15 years had higher patient- and infusion-level incidence, and patients with reactions had much higher cumulative dose. The low-dose cohort had few events, but its patient-level total is internally inconsistent between prose and Table 3.

Within the selected subgroup of 184 patients aged ≥15 years with autoimmune disease, adjusted patient-level odds were higher with neuromuscular disease and cumulative dose ≥7 g/kg, while age ≥60 years was not significant. On 2,548 infusions, neuromuscular disease and younger age remained associated; dose ≥0.45 g/kg per infusion was not. Fever and headache were the dominant reaction phenotypes.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the data as showing clinically important heterogeneity in IVIG reaction risk. They emphasize neuromuscular disease, female sex, and cumulative exposure, while noting that a high dose on an individual infusion did not independently predict reactions. They discuss prior literature suggesting dose and infusion-rate effects but acknowledge that the mechanism behind the neuromuscular signal remains unclear.

The paper explicitly identifies retrospective analysis as a limitation and calls for more detailed patient-level investigation before stronger safety recommendations are made. The closing inference is association, not proof that altering dose, rate, or indication will prevent reactions.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a quantitative observational reference for immediate IVIG infusion-reaction risk signals. Do not use it as a stand-alone dosing, product-selection, or infusion-rate protocol.

Directly relevant to IVIG infusion safety and risk stratification.

Evidence basis: 748 patients, 4,070 infusions, indication- and dose-stratified adverse-reaction data.

Limiting factor: Observational association does not establish a management rule.

Limiting factor: Several risk factors were already suspected from prior IVIG safety literature.

The dataset is substantial, but the design is retrospective and single-center.

Evidence basis: Uniform collection forms, explicit AR definition, multivariable logistic models, odds ratios and 95% CIs.

Limiting factor: Residual confounding, subgroup selection after univariate screening, and uncertain handling of repeated infusions limit causal and inferential strength.

Generalizability is constrained by one Japanese academic center and a heterogeneous local IVIG population.

Evidence basis: Wide age range and multiple indications improve breadth within the center.

Limiting factor: Product mix, local indications, infusion practices, and population characteristics may differ elsewhere.

Would raise: Multicenter, multinational data across products and protocols would raise the score.

6/10

Implementation value

The findings can inform heightened observation and risk awareness but do not define a validated protocol change.

Evidence basis: Risk signals are clinically interpretable and the infusion protocol is described.

Limiting factor: No validated prediction threshold, intervention trial, or demonstrated benefit from altering monitoring or dosing.

Would raise: A validated bedside risk model linked to tested mitigation strategies would raise the score.

Validity

Strengths: large infusion count; explicit reaction definition; multiple indications; both patient- and infusion-level perspectives; adjusted ORs with confidence intervals; and preservation of negative findings such as non-significant dose per infusion. Principal limitations: retrospective single-center design; heterogeneous indication and exposure; residual confounding; selected higher-incidence regression subgroup; limited information on missingness and product-specific factors; and unclear statistical accommodation of repeated infusions within patients.

Outcome scope: the study captures reactions occurring during infusion and diagnosed by physicians. It should not be treated as a complete assessment of delayed IVIG toxicities such as thrombosis, renal injury, aseptic meningitis, or hemolysis.

Practice translation

The most defensible clinical use is heightened awareness that patients with neuromuscular disease, women, and those with high cumulative exposure may warrant careful observation. The data do not demonstrate that lowering cumulative dose, changing infusion rate, or applying a specific prophylaxis strategy reduces events. Current product labeling, contemporary guidance, and patient-specific factors remain necessary for management decisions.
