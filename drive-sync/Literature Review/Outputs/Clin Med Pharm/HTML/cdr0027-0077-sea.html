One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this 146-patient CCU cohort, early UFH therapy frequently failed to place patients within the local APTT target: 41.1% were subtherapeutic at 6 hours, 42.3% at 24 hours, and 46.7% at 48 hours. The authors interpreted sex, racial grouping, admission diagnosis, and admission APTT ratio as predictors of apparent heparin resistance and argued for formalized dosing and monitoring protocols.

The study was prospective and operationally focused. Patients received a fixed starting regimen of a 5,000-unit UFH bolus followed by 1,000 units/hour, with physician-directed adjustment using a local APTT target of 1.5-2.5 times the mean. APTT was collected at prespecified timepoints. Only 26.7% of patients were therapeutic at 6 hours; 32.2% were above the target. The corresponding therapeutic proportions at 24 and 48 hours were 30.0% and 33.3%.

Secondary analyses reported higher subtherapeutic rates at 6 hours in Arabs versus non-Arabs (48% vs 21%, P=0.006), women versus men (55.3% vs 34.7%, P=0.003), and unstable angina versus acute myocardial infarction (45% vs 20.9%, P=0.004). An admission APTT ratio below 1.0 was also associated with subtherapeutic APTT at 6 hours. Bleeding attributed to UFH occurred in 8 patients (5.5%); other reported events were uncommon.

Central appraisal issue. The study often labels subtherapeutic APTT under this local fixed-dose regimen as “heparin resistance,” but it did not demonstrate that patients required unusually high UFH doses or separate biological resistance from inadequate initial dosing, dose-adjustment behavior, assay variability, or patient-level confounding. Body weight and delivered dose intensity are not reported in the baseline table or outcome analyses.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the study as a real-world assessment of UFH practice in a busy Kuwaiti CCU, emphasizing apparent heparin resistance, predictors of responsiveness, and complications. It reports a prospective observational cohort of 146 patients treated according to a standard normogram, with APTT measurements on admission and at 6, 24, and 48 hours.

The headline finding is persistent subtherapeutic APTT in roughly two-fifths of patients at each timepoint. The abstract identifies race, sex, admission diagnosis, and baseline APTT below 1.0 as predictors and reports no significant difference in complications across groups. It concludes that heparin resistance is common early and recommends evidence-based administration and monitoring protocols.

Introduction

The introduction reviews UFH as an indirect anticoagulant with a narrow therapeutic window and variable dose-response. It attributes early heparin resistance to nonspecific binding, reticuloendothelial removal, and platelet factor 4 neutralization, and argues that close APTT monitoring and dose adjustment are needed.

The local rationale is practice-oriented: UFH was widely used in Kuwait and the Middle East, but the authors state that anticoagulation practice, pitfalls, and factors influencing outcomes had not been explored locally. This motivates a real-world CCU audit rather than a mechanistic heparin-resistance study.

Methods

The study prospectively collected data for 4 months at Farwaniya Hospital. Treating physicians were blinded to data collection, the institutional review board approved the study, and informed consent was obtained. Eligibility centered on CCU patients receiving UFH, mainly for myocardial infarction or unstable angina; patients later found to have noncardiac chest pain remained eligible if UFH had been given initially. Patients not receiving UFH were excluded.

The starting regimen was a 5,000-unit bolus followed by 1,000 units/hour. APTT was measured every 6 hours and the dose adjusted by treating physicians. The local therapeutic APTT ratio was 1.5-2.5; <1.5 was subtherapeutic and >2.5 over-anticoagulated. APTT was recorded at admission, 6, 24, 48, and 72 hours. The primary outcome was therapeutic APTT attainment at 6, 24, and 48 hours; secondary outcomes included candidate predictors and complications. Analyses were mainly descriptive with Pearson chi-square tests for secondary outcomes.

Results - cohort and primary endpoints

The cohort included 146 patients; mean UFH duration was 3.64 days (range 1-15). At 6 hours, 39/146 (26.7%) were therapeutic, 60/146 (41.1%) subtherapeutic, and 47/146 (32.2%) over-anticoagulated. At 24 hours, among 137 patients still receiving heparin, 41 (30.0%) were therapeutic, 58 (42.3%) subtherapeutic, and 38 (27.7%) over-anticoagulated.

At 48 hours, among 105 patients still on UFH, 35 (33.3%) were therapeutic and 49 (46.7%) subtherapeutic. Table 2 reports 21 (20%) as over-anticoagulated, whereas the adjacent prose reports 20 (20%), an internal inconsistency. Denominators fall from 146 to 137 to 105; the article does not give a detailed accounting of why patients exited UFH before later timepoints.

Results - secondary endpoints and safety

At 6 hours, subtherapeutic APTT was more common in Arabs than non-Arabs (48% vs 21%, P=0.006), women than men (55.3% vs 34.7%, P=0.003), and unstable-angina than acute-MI patients (45% vs 20.9%, P=0.004). The racial difference narrowed at 24 and 48 hours; the sex and diagnosis differences remained numerically present. On admission, 88/146 (60.3%) had APTT ratio <1.0. Table 3 reports 45/88 patients in that group versus 15/58 with admission APTT at least 1.0 as subtherapeutic at 6 hours (P=0.010).

UFH-attributed bleeding occurred in 8/146 patients (5.5%), mostly minor; one clinically significant hemoptysis episode led to discontinuation. Table 4 reports stroke in 2 patients, PE in 3, thrombus extension in 1, and death in 1. Follow-up platelet counts were not routinely ordered to monitor for HIT; only one patient is described as having a platelet count after a bleeding episode. The text says recurrent ischemia is shown in Table 4, but no such row appears in the displayed table.

Discussion

The authors interpret the high early subtherapeutic APTT rate as clinically important inadequate heparinization and discuss both UFH pharmacologic variability and practice factors such as initial dosing, dose adjustment, and physician caution. They highlight four potential predictors: racial grouping, sex, admission diagnosis, and low baseline APTT.

The discussion itself acknowledges uncertainty around some associations. The racial finding is described as needing confirmation, and the unstable-angina versus MI difference could reflect different treatment intensity or thrombolytic effects rather than diagnosis-specific resistance. The authors also emphasize poor HIT surveillance and argue that evidence-based heparin protocols can reduce APTT fluctuations.

Conclusion and disclosures

The conclusion attributes UFH pitfalls to both drug properties and physician knowledge of pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics. It recommends an approved heparin normogram, frequent APTT monitoring, and dose adjustment, while calling for broader studies to confirm populations at risk of heparin resistance.

The authors declare no conflict of interest. No dedicated funding statement appears in the retrieved article text.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High topical relevance for a heparin-responsiveness deep dive, but the study is best used as a historical real-world signal about APTT variability and protocol implementation rather than as a causal or mechanistic definition of heparin resistance.
9/10Relevance

Directly examines UFH responsiveness, APTT monitoring, apparent resistance, dosing practice, and complications in a CCU.

Evidence basis: prespecified serial APTT data and subgroup analyses. Limit: ACS-heavy single-center setting. Would rise: broader indications and richer dosing data.

5/10Novelty

Provides region-specific prospective practice data and exploratory predictor signals.

Evidence basis: local Kuwait cohort and early UFH monitoring. Limit: mostly descriptive associations. Would rise: mechanistic confirmation or externally validated predictors.

4/10Method strength

Prospective collection and predefined timepoints are strengths, but there is no randomized comparator, multivariable model, dose-intensity analysis, or confidence-interval reporting.

Evidence basis: Methods and results. Limit: confounding and implementation variation are unresolved. Would rise: adjusted analyses, protocol-adherence data, patient weight, and delivered dose.

4/10Evidence strength

The article establishes frequent off-target APTT values under the local regimen, but causal claims about “resistance” and predictors are weak.

Evidence basis: repeated APTT distributions and chi-square subgroup tests. Limit: surrogate endpoint, multiple unadjusted comparisons, sparse clinical events, source inconsistencies. Would rise: dose-response data, adjusted models, anti-Xa/biologic corroboration, and uncertainty estimates.

3/10External validity

Generalizability is limited by one hospital, a 4-month period, an ACS-dominant case mix, and locally defined practice.

Evidence basis: setting and baseline table. Limit: no external cohort. Would rise: multicenter replication across indications and dosing protocols.

6/10Implementation value

Useful for illustrating how serial anticoagulation targets can reveal protocol failure and how process gaps such as HIT monitoring can coexist with dosing variability.

Evidence basis: high early off-target APTT and documented monitoring practices. Limit: insufficient detail to reproduce or endorse the regimen. Would rise: full nomogram, adherence metrics, dose-adjustment logs, and outcome-linked implementation data.

Strengths

  • Prospective data collection in routine CCU practice.
  • Prespecified serial APTT timepoints with changing denominators reported.
  • Concrete treatment starting regimen and local target definition.
  • Directly surfaces operational safety/process issues, including lack of routine platelet surveillance.
  • Authors acknowledge uncertainty around some subgroup associations.

Major limitations

  • Construct validity: subtherapeutic APTT is treated as “heparin resistance,” but the study does not show unusually high dose requirements or distinguish biological resistance from underdosing, adherence, physician titration, or assay effects.
  • Dosing confounding: the starting dose is fixed and patient body weight is not reported, preventing dose-per-kilogram assessment.
  • Unadjusted predictor analyses: race, sex, diagnosis, and baseline APTT are assessed with simple subgroup comparisons; no multivariable adjustment is reported.
  • Changing denominators: 146 patients contribute at 6 hours, 137 at 24 hours, and 105 at 48 hours; reasons for stopping UFH are not fully characterized.
  • Clinical outcomes underpowered: safety/thrombotic events are sparse, making “no significant difference” weak evidence of equivalence or safety.
  • Safety surveillance incomplete: platelet counts were not routinely monitored for HIT.
  • Reporting inconsistencies: 48-hour over-anticoagulation count differs between table and prose; recurrence ischemia is referenced as present in Table 4 but not displayed.
  • External validity: single-center, short-duration, geographically and clinically narrow cohort.

Bottom line

Best use: historical evidence that fixed-regimen UFH in routine CCU care can yield frequent early APTT values outside a target range, and a useful case study in separating pharmacologic responsiveness from protocol implementation.

Do not use for: a definitive prevalence of biological heparin resistance, causal claims about race/sex/diagnosis, current UFH dosing recommendations, or reassurance about clinical-event safety.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the paper's internal methods/results because the full six-page source and all four main-text tables were inspected; moderate for broader practice translation because no external current-practice verification was performed.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Separate “off-target APTT” from resistance.A CDS or review framework should distinguish failure to reach a laboratory target from true high-dose biological heparin resistance.
2. Capture dose intensity and weight.Without weight and delivered dose over time, apparent responsiveness cannot be interpreted cleanly.
3. Audit protocol execution.Serial time-to-therapeutic metrics can identify implementation problems, but should be paired with nomogram adherence and dose-adjustment data.
4. Preserve changing denominators.APTT performance at later timepoints must retain the number still receiving UFH and reasons for treatment discontinuation.
5. Treat subgroup signals as exploratory.The source provides hypotheses, not validated predictors; confounding and multiple testing are substantial concerns.
6. Integrate safety surveillance.The platelet-monitoring gap shows why anticoagulation pathways should couple efficacy monitoring with explicit safety checks.
