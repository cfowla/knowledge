One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Confirm the diagnosis before committing to prolonged therapy; use oral terbinafine for most treatable cases without contraindications because it offers the strongest cure performance, reserve topical agents for milder disease or when oral therapy is unsuitable, and counsel patients about long regrowth times and substantial recurrence.

The review frames onychomycosis as a common chronic nail infection with functional and quality-of-life consequences rather than a cosmetic problem. Its diagnostic approach emphasizes the broad differential for nail dystrophy and prioritizes KOH microscopy, with fungal culture, PAS stain, or PCR as confirmatory options when KOH is negative but suspicion remains high. The review also permits empiric terbinafine when testing is cost-prohibitive. Table 3 gives a compact diagnostic-performance comparison: KOH is highly specific (95%); fungal culture is most specific (99%) but has variable sensitivity; PAS has high sensitivity (81%-91.6%); PCR is reported with 83% sensitivity and 84% specificity.

For treatment, oral agents outperform topical therapy in cure and duration. Terbinafine is positioned as first line, with baseline transaminase testing and attention to interacting medications; itraconazole and off-label fluconazole are alternatives. Topical ciclopirox, efinaconazole, and tavaborole have much lower complete-cure rates but fewer systemic adverse effects and interactions. The article preserves practical dosing, duration, recurrence-prevention strategies, and selected quantitative RCT results. Evidence for laser, plasma, photodynamic, and integrative therapies is presented as limited or investigational.

Section condensation

Epidemiology and pathogenesis

The review reports North American point prevalence up to 13.8% in adults and 0.44% in people younger than 18. Age over 60 is highlighted as a major risk factor, with recurrent nail trauma, tobacco use, diabetes, obesity, psoriasis, malignancy, HIV, peripheral vascular disease, and immunocompromise also listed.

Dermatophytes are reported to cause about 70% of U.S. infections, with molds and yeasts responsible for the remainder. A cited study reported mixed fungal infections in 39%, which the authors use to explain diagnostic and treatment complexity.

Diagnosis: presentation, severity, and testing

Because fungi account for only about half of nail dystrophies, the article emphasizes differential diagnosis. Typical findings include discoloration, deformity, hypertrophy/hyperkeratosis, subungual debris, onycholysis, brittleness/crumbling, and odor. Severity is graded with the Onychomycosis Severity Index using extent of involvement, matrix proximity, and dermatophytoma or subungual hyperkeratosis >2 mm.

KOH microscopy is the preferred first test because it is rapid, inexpensive, and highly specific. If KOH is negative but suspicion remains high, fungal culture, PAS stain, or PCR may be used. The review notes that samples should come from the proximal area of onycholysis. Diagnostic testing is generally recommended before therapy, but empiric terbinafine is described as an option when testing is cost-prohibitive.

Treatment selection and oral therapy

Treatment choice is tied to disease extent, matrix involvement, number of nails, comorbidities, drug interactions, adverse effects, cost, duration, and patient preference. Oral therapy is described as most effective at any severity. Terbinafine is the preferred first-line oral agent. Chronic or active liver disease is the principal contraindication cited; baseline transaminases are recommended, whereas routine follow-up laboratory monitoring is stated to be unnecessary in immunocompetent patients. The review flags potential interactions with tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, tamoxifen, atypical antipsychotics, and beta blockers.

Itraconazole has continuous and pulse regimens depending on nail site. In a five-year prospective severe-disease study (n=144), the review reports higher mycologic relapse with itraconazole than terbinafine (53% vs 23%) and higher clinical relapse (48% vs 21%), both P<.001. A 22-trial network meta-analysis (n=4,205) is summarized as favoring terbinafine over itraconazole for complete cure. Fluconazole 150 mg weekly for at least six months is presented as an off-label alternative; griseofulvin is deemphasized.

Topical therapy

Topical therapy is positioned for superficial disease, early distal lateral subungual disease, limited nail involvement, prevention of recurrence, pediatric situations, or when systemic therapy is undesirable. Recommended duration is 24 weeks for fingernails and 48 weeks for toenails. Local reactions such as exfoliation, erythema, burning, and dermatitis are the principal adverse effects described.

Complete-cure effects are modest. Ciclopirox RCTs reported 5.5% vs 0.9% (P=.059) and 8.5% vs 0% (P=.001). Two efinaconazole phase 3 studies reported 17.8% vs 3.3% (NNT 7) and 15.2% vs 5.5% (NNT 10), both P<.001. Two tavaborole phase 3 studies reported 6.5% vs 0.5% (NNT 17) and 9.1% vs 1.5% (NNT 13), both P<.001.

Surgical, device-based, integrative, and emerging therapy

Trimming and debridement are recommended as adjuncts to pharmacologic treatment, with nail removal reserved for severe or treatment-refractory disease. Laser modalities are described as FDA-approved only for temporary cosmetic improvement and supported by low-level evidence/small RCTs. Combining laser with topical therapy is reported to increase overall effectiveness.

Essential oils, vinegar soaks, and mentholated ointment have only small-scale supportive studies. A 19-person uncontrolled plasma pilot reported 53.8% clinical cure. An 80-person photodynamic-therapy trial reported 90% response versus 40% with weekly fluconazole 300 mg at 24 weeks (P=.002; NNT 2), but the study was not blinded. The review calls for larger randomized trials before these newer modalities are adopted.

Prognosis and prevention

The review reports relapse in 20%-25% of successfully treated cases, commonly within two years. Older age (>70), nail trauma, and diabetes are listed as poor prognostic features. Preventive advice is largely expert-opinion based: avoid barefoot exposure in public spaces, disinfect footwear and socks, keep feet cool/dry, and recognize recurrence early.

Treating tinea pedis promptly is recommended because infected skin may act as a reservoir. A cited study reported that twice-weekly topical antifungal prophylaxis after terbinafine cure reduced recurrence to 33% compared with 76% without prophylaxis (P<.001).

Data sources

The review reports a PubMed Clinical Queries search using “onychomycosis,” “tinea unguium,” and “nail fungus,” including meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, clinical trials, and reviews. Cochrane, DynaMed, and Essential Evidence Plus were also searched. The stated search dates were November 13, 2020 and August 15, 2021.

However, the article does not present a reproducible systematic-review selection flow, formal risk-of-bias assessment, certainty-of-evidence framework across all outcomes, or explicit data-extraction methods. Its evidence-rating layer is the AAFP SORT system for selected practice recommendations.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a concise 2021 primary-care synthesis with practical diagnostic and treatment detail. It should not be treated as a current stand-alone guideline or as primary evidence for the cited trials.
8/10

Relevance

Directly useful for diagnosis, pharmacotherapy selection, counseling, and recurrence prevention in routine clinical care.

Evidence basis: Clear clinical questions, dosing, cure rates, interactions, and treatment-selection criteria.

Principal limit: Focused on one condition and framed for primary care.

Would change with: Higher if the user is actively managing onychomycosis or building a dermatology/pharmacotherapy reference.

4/10

Novelty

The article is a synthesis rather than a novel study and largely consolidates established diagnostic and treatment approaches.

Evidence basis: Rapid review structure and cited prior trials/reviews.

Principal limit: No new dataset, intervention, or original analysis.

Would change with: Higher only if the synthesis introduced a new decision framework or materially updated prior guidance.

5/10

Method strength

The search sources, terms, eligible evidence types, and dates are reported, but systematic-review methods are incomplete.

Evidence basis: PubMed Clinical Queries plus Cochrane, DynaMed, Essential Evidence Plus; stated search dates.

Principal limit: No PRISMA-style selection flow, formal risk-of-bias assessment, reproducible inclusion/exclusion protocol, or explicit dual extraction.

Would change with: Higher with prespecified selection methods, transparent study flow, risk-of-bias assessment, and certainty grading.

6/10

Evidence strength

Several recommendations draw on RCTs, systematic reviews, and a network meta-analysis, but some practice recommendations are explicitly expert-opinion/SORT C or limited-quality/SORT B.

Evidence basis: Quantitative RCT summaries, network meta-analysis, prospective follow-up, SORT recommendation table.

Principal limit: The review does not independently reanalyze evidence or consistently grade certainty across each claim.

Would change with: Higher with direct appraisal of primary studies, consistent certainty grading, and updated evidence verification.

7/10

External validity

The condition and interventions are broadly applicable to primary care, but the review reflects the literature and U.S. regulatory/pricing context available through 2021.

Evidence basis: Common outpatient condition; multiple treatment classes and age groups discussed.

Principal limit: Currency and local availability/cost may differ; some cited evidence is old or narrowly selected.

Would change with: Higher with contemporary multinational data and explicit subgroup/applicability analysis.

8/10

Implementation value

The review gives practical test selection, treatment indications, dosing, durations, cure rates, monitoring points, and recurrence strategies.

Evidence basis: Tables 3-5, Choosing Wisely recommendation, SORT table, explicit counseling points.

Principal limit: Some recommendations depend on evidence the review characterizes as low level or expert opinion.

Would change with: Higher with a formal decision algorithm, current formulary/cost data, and updated guideline/regulatory cross-checking.

Main limitations: secondary review; not systematic-review-grade methodology; mixed evidence maturity; older evidence base; regulatory and price statements are time-sensitive; diagnostic and treatment conclusions depend on underlying studies that were not independently checked in this run.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use
As a compact reference for test selection, disease-severity framing, oral-vs-topical treatment selection, patient counseling, and a starting bibliography.
Do not overclaim
Do not treat the review's summaries of cited trials as if the primary publications were directly inspected or atomized.
High-value next read
Primary trials and systematic reviews supporting terbinafine comparative effectiveness, efinaconazole/tavaborole RCTs, diagnostic accuracy, and recurrence prophylaxis.
Currency caveat
Any 2021 FDA-status, laboratory-monitoring, cost, or preferred-therapy statement should be verified before current clinical implementation.
