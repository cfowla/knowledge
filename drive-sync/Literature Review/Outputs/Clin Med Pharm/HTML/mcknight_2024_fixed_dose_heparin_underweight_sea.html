One-page synthesis

Bottom line: Fixed 5,000-unit titration boluses produced the same reported rate of supratherapeutic HCV after the first bolus in underweight and non-obese controls (13.9% in each group), and no study-defined clinically significant bleeding events occurred. However, the underweight group received substantially more heparin per kilogram (108.6 vs 72.1 units/kg), heparin holds for supratherapeutic HCV were numerically more frequent, >40% of both groups reportedly never achieved stable HCV, and the study was too small and protocol-specific to establish clinical safety.
Why it matters

A fixed 5,000-unit correction bolus can exceed an underweight patient’s initial weight-based loading dose. The paper directly tests whether that institution-specific approach appears to overcorrect anticoagulation or cause bleeding.

Best interpretation

This is a protocol-safety signal study, not proof of equivalence or noninferiority. The most defensible use is to justify local review of fixed bolus strategies in low-weight patients and to motivate better-powered, patient-centered evaluation.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame fixed 5,000-unit titration boluses as a potential safety problem in underweight VTE patients because the bolus may exceed the initial recommended weight-based loading dose. They compare underweight patients with BMI 18.5–29.9 kg/m² controls, focusing on post-bolus HCV overshoot, heparin holds, time to stable HCV, and bleeding.

The abstract reports 158 analyzed patients, identical 13.9% post-bolus supratherapeutic HCV rates, more heparin holds in underweight patients, prolonged time to stable HCV, and no difference in clinically significant bleeding. Its statement that “all patients” required >48 hours to stable HCV is not fully consistent with Table 3’s distributions and with the fact that >40% reportedly never reached stable HCV.

1. Introduction

The rationale is pharmacologic and operational: UFH has complex PK/PD, institutions use variable titration protocols, and evidence in underweight patients is sparse. UNC’s HCV-based nomogram uses a fixed 5,000-unit bolus for HCV <0.3; for a 45-kg patient, this exceeds the 3,600-unit 80-units/kg loading dose cited from older ACCP guidance.

The study question is therefore narrower than “how should UFH be dosed in underweight patients?” It asks whether this specific fixed correction bolus appears safe relative to the same nomogram in non-obese controls.

2. Materials and methods

This was a retrospective EMR review spanning more than eight years. From 101 underweight and 1,014 control candidates, 79 patients were ultimately included in each group; control records were randomly sampled to create a 1:1 comparison. Baseline demographics, anticoagulation labs, imaging confirmation, and loading-dose administration were collected.

Analyses used chi-square tests for categorical variables, independent-samples t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U tests for nonparametric variables, with p<0.05. No power calculation, multivariable adjustment, propensity method, repeated-measures model, or time-to-event method is described.

3. Results

The groups differed as expected in weight and BMI, and also in sex. The mean fixed bolus exposure was 108.6±17.7 units/kg in underweight patients versus 72.1±13.8 units/kg in controls (p<0.001). The reported primary outcome was identical: 13.9% in each group had a supratherapeutic HCV after the first titration bolus.

Among patients who reached stable HCV, median time was 48.3 hours (IQR 34.4–81.3) versus 58.5 hours (30.4–69.1), p=0.62; the paper states >40% of each group never achieved stable HCV. Protocol-directed holds were numerically more common in underweight patients but not statistically significant. No clinically significant bleeding occurred. Length of stay was shorter in the underweight group (15.2 vs 24.8 days; p=0.04), a secondary finding with unclear causal relation to bolus safety.

4. Discussion

The authors interpret the equal first-bolus HCV overshoot rate and absence of observed bleeding as reassuring but stop short of declaring the fixed-dose approach safe. Their concern persists because underweight patients received materially higher units/kg and required more holds later in therapy. They hypothesize that large boluses may overcorrect HCV, force infusion pauses, and delay stable anticoagulation.

They acknowledge institution-specific HCV monitoring, BMI-based rather than weight-based underweight classification, a wide underweight weight range, and small sample size. Additional limitations apparent from the design include selection of only patients who required a bolus, no adjustment for baseline imbalance/confounding, a surrogate primary endpoint, and limited ability to evaluate rare bleeding outcomes.

5. Conclusions

The authors conclude that the first-bolus supratherapeutic HCV rate did not differ by BMI group, while maintaining concern that underweight patients may be more susceptible to over-anticoagulation. They call for additional studies before the safety of fixed-dose titration boluses can be established.

Source consistency and quantitative QA

Control heparin-hold percentage: Table 3 reports 10 patients as 10.1% of n=79. Those values are not arithmetically consistent (10/79 ≈ 12.7%). The SEA preserves the table exactly and does not silently repair it.
Loading-dose percentage: Table 2 reports 42 control patients as 56% of n=79. Those values are also not arithmetically consistent (42/79 ≈ 53.2%).
“All patients took over 48 h”: The abstract’s statement conflicts with Table 3 IQR lower bounds below 48 h and with the text stating that >40% of each group never achieved stable HCV. The more precise table/prose should be favored when interpreting this outcome.
Stable-HCV endpoint handling: Results are reported “for patients who reached a stable HCV,” while >40% reportedly never did. No censoring/time-to-event analysis is described, so the median time-to-stability values do not characterize the entire cohort.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for UFH protocol design; do not use as stand-alone practice-changing evidence.
The question is directly relevant to anticoagulation medication-use systems, but the study is small, single-center, retrospective, surrogate-heavy, and underpowered for bleeding safety.

Major strengths

  • Specific, operationally meaningful medication-safety question.
  • Exact institutional nomogram is reported, allowing replication of the exposure definition.
  • Groups are similar on many measured baseline variables, with key differences shown rather than hidden.
  • Authors avoid claiming definitive safety and explicitly call for more evidence.

Major limitations / bias risks

  • Selection: Only patients who required at least one titration bolus were eligible; results do not represent all UFH-treated underweight patients.
  • Confounding: No multivariable adjustment despite sex imbalance and potentially unmeasured differences in illness severity, VTE phenotype, concomitant therapies, or bleeding risk.
  • Surrogate primary endpoint: Supratherapeutic HCV after one bolus is not itself a patient-centered bleeding outcome.
  • Safety power: Zero observed bleeds in 79 patients per group cannot establish low or equivalent bleeding risk. As a simple model-based approximation, the “rule of three” gives an upper 95% event-rate bound of roughly 3.8% per group when zero events are observed.
  • Incomplete stability: >40% reportedly never achieved stable HCV, complicating interpretation of median time-to-stability.
  • Generalizability: HCV monitoring and a fixed 5,000-unit correction bolus are local practices.
  • Data/reporting consistency: At least two count/percentage pairs are internally inconsistent.

Practice translation

Use now?

Maybe as a local safety signal; no as a stand-alone dosing change. The paper is sufficient to justify auditing low-weight patients exposed to fixed 5,000-unit correction boluses, but not to prove that fixed dosing is unsafe or to specify a replacement dose.

Most defensible action

For institutions with similar protocols, evaluate weight-normalized bolus exposure, post-bolus anticoagulation trajectories, protocol holds, time in therapeutic range, and bleeding by low-weight strata before deciding whether a capped or weight-based correction rule is warranted.

What this paper supports

  • Underweight patients receive materially larger units/kg exposure from the same fixed bolus.
  • First-bolus HCV overshoot was not higher in this sample.
  • The protocol may have efficiency concerns because therapeutic stabilization was slow/incomplete in both groups.
  • Additional data are needed before declaring the fixed bolus clinically safe.

What it does not support

  • That fixed 5,000-unit boluses are equivalent or noninferior to weight-based boluses.
  • That bleeding risk is absent in underweight patients.
  • That a specific lower bolus or cap should replace the protocol.
  • That findings transfer directly to anti-Xa- or aPTT-based nomograms.

Currency verification — external context

External verification, separate from the source: The paper’s background cites 2012 ACCP guidance. CHEST’s VTE guideline has since been updated; the 2021 treatment-phase recommendation, carried in the current CHEST guideline presentation, favors DOACs over VKA for many patients with acute VTE. That broader evolution in VTE therapy does not answer this paper’s specific question about UFH correction boluses in underweight patients. The study should therefore be interpreted as a narrow UFH protocol-safety study rather than a general VTE-treatment study.
