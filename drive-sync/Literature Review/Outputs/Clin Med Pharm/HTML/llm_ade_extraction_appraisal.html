Core thesis

A carefully prompted, two-pass reasoning LLM can convert deidentified clinical notes into structured adverse drug event records with high precision on a diverse within-institution sample. The most valuable contribution is not just ADE extraction, but extraction of attributes that matter for pharmacovigilance triage: seriousness, labeled/unlabeled status, present-illness status, failure of efficacy, MedDRA LLT, medication changes, and causality evidence.

Most reusable finding

The false-positive taxonomy is directly actionable for system design: block guesses around redacted text, require specific drug names, enforce temporal plausibility, separate hypothetical patient-instruction language from experienced events, validate MedDRA terms deterministically, and route independent LLM-inferred ADEs to a different review pathway from clinician-attributed ADEs.

Primary limitation

The study validates extraction correctness in one deidentified UCSF dataset; it does not validate external generalization, longitudinal regulatory signal generation, outcome impact, prospective deployment, repeated-run stability, or performance after model retirement/version changes. The recall estimate is not based on full manual annotation of all 372 notes.

Section condensation

Abstract

The article presents a two-pass OpenAI o1 pipeline for extracting adverse drug events from full EHR clinical notes. Pass 1 screens notes for drug-event pairs with a 'reasonable possibility' of causation; Pass 2 adds structured ADE attributes such as seriousness, label status, MedDRA coding, present illness status, and failure of efficacy.

In 372 deidentified UCSF notes, the model extracted 191 ADEs. Expert review judged 180 true positives, yielding 94.2% precision, 84.1% estimated recall, and F1 88.9%. The abstract also reports 100% correctness for seriousness, 93.9% label-status correctness, 92.5% valid MedDRA LLT coding, 12.2% serious ADEs, 15.0% unlabeled ADEs, 8.9% failure of efficacy, and vendor cost of $0.18 per note / $0.35 per validated ADE.

Introduction and prior work

The authors argue that pharmacovigilance databases miss many ADE signals because voluntary reporting is incomplete and structured EHR data often omit causal reasoning documented in notes. The central framing is FDA-style 'reasonable possibility': evidence suggesting a causal relationship between a drug/biologic and an adverse event.

The literature review contrasts older sentence-level or rule-based systems with BERT-family and GPT-era ADE extraction work. Prior systems often used pre-annotated pairs, sentence-level contexts, case reports, vaccine reports, social media posts, or narrower clinical tasks; the authors position their study as full-note, broad-domain, causality-aware extraction.

Objectives

The study aims to determine whether a state-of-the-art LLM can extract ADEs from full-length clinical notes, whether extracted events meet a reasonable-possibility causality threshold, whether precision is high enough for aggregate statistical signal detection, and whether the model can infer regulatory-useful attributes such as serious, unlabeled, present illness, and failure of efficacy.

This is framed as feasibility for regulator/manufacturer signal generation rather than direct clinical decision support or automated individual case reporting.

Methods: source notes and sampling

All notes came from the UCSF deidentified clinical notes database with encounter date after 2023-09-05, at least 256 characters, authored by physicians or clinical pharmacists, and representing patient-care or procedure notes rather than lab/pathology/radiology notes. The final validation set included 372 notes: 12 notes from each of 31 classes defined by provider specialty group, inpatient/outpatient encounter type, and patient-care/procedure note class.

This design improves within-institution diversity, but it is not a prevalence-weighted sample and not an external validation set. Notes from patients used in earlier testing were excluded, reducing direct reuse but not eliminating broader prompt/model tuning risks.

Methods: LLM pipeline

The model was OpenAI o1-2024-12-17. Pass 1 extracted ADEs with a reasonable possibility of causation, including event, medication, rationale, and verbatim supporting phrases. Pass 2 ran only when Pass 1 found ADEs and added structured fields for regulatory/use-case filtering and quality checks.

The two-pass design is operationally important because 84.9% of notes had no extracted ADEs, avoiding pass 2 cost for most notes. The article references online supplements for exact prompts and SQL, which were not contained in the uploaded PDF; this limits prompt-level reproducibility from the main article alone.

Methods: evaluation

Precision was first assessed by Reviewer 1 for all 191 LLM-generated ADEs. The authors then added a 100-note gold-standard subset: all notes where the model found at least one ADE plus 42 randomly sampled notes where the model found no ADEs. Reviewer 2 annotated the 100 notes while blinded to LLM output; Reviewer 3 adjudicated LLM ADEs validated by Reviewer 1 but absent from Reviewer 2's gold standard.

This evaluation is stronger than a single-reviewer precision check but still constrained. Precision is close to full LLM-output precision because all LLM-positive notes were included; recall is estimated from the 100-note subset and could miss false negatives in the unsampled LLM-negative notes.

Results: extraction validity and performance

The model extracted 191 ADEs from 372 notes. Final adjudication yielded 180 true positives and 11 false positives, with 34 false negatives and 42 constructed true negatives in the 100-note evaluation framework. Reported final performance was precision 94.2%, recall 84.1%, accuracy 82.8%, and F1 88.9%.

False positives were clinically informative: most came from guessing redacted content, uncertain chronology, class-only drug references, unclear medication/event targets, unlikely causality, or hypothetical events. These are addressable with output constraints, dictionary checks, chronology requirements, and human-review triggers.

Results: ADE attributes

Among valid ADEs, 22/180 (12.2%) were serious, 27/180 (15.0%) were estimated as unlabeled after correction assumptions, and 16/180 (8.9%) were failure of efficacy. Label-status validity was 93.9%; present-illness classification was wrong in only one case; MedDRA LLT generation produced real terms 92.5% of the time.

The MedDRA result is a key implementation warning: 7.5% of LLTs were plausible but non-existent, and one valid term was clinically mismapped due to BUN/Cr abbreviation interpretation. Any production pipeline requires deterministic dictionary validation and likely human coding review for high-impact outputs.

Results: independent LLM inference and unlabeled ADEs

Most extracted ADEs reflected clinician documentation: 63.9% were discussed as the cause and 23.9% as a potential cause. Only 22 ADEs (12.2%) were not mentioned by the physician as causal; reviewers identified at least five plausible cases where the model inferred causality from drug-event knowledge.

The paper also found seven non-efficacy unlabeled ADEs. All were suggested by the note author, not independently discovered by the model, which matters for interpreting the LLM's novelty in signal discovery.

Discussion and contributions

The authors conclude that o1 can precisely extract most ADEs from full clinical notes and that note-derived ADEs could expand real-world pharmacovigilance beyond structured sources used by Sentinel or Darwin EU. They emphasize full-note reasoning, causal possibility filtering, seriousness/unlabeled/present-illness attributes, broad within-institution note diversity, and cost feasibility.

The stronger supported claim is feasibility of high-precision ADE extraction from UCSF deidentified notes. The weaker claim is readiness for regulatory signal detection: the study did not test disproportionality analysis, signal prioritization, regulator adjudication, prospective monitoring, or patient-level outcomes.

Limitations and future work

The authors acknowledge single-institution data, deidentification artifacts, LLM hallucination, non-existent MedDRA terms, output-length limits, model retirement, prompt non-universality, lack of a final gold standard independent of post hoc model-result validation, stochastic variability, proprietary model cost, and privacy risks of third-party LLM use.

Future directions include validation against benchmark clinical notes such as n2c2, testing open-source local models, and adding structured data such as diagnoses, medications, and labs to improve chronology. These are necessary before operational deployment.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that current LLMs can extract ADEs from clinical notes with high enough precision to potentially support pharmacovigilance signal generation when analyzed in aggregate. They argue that open-source/local models could reduce privacy and cost barriers.

The conclusion should be read as a research and workflow-design claim, not a clinical-practice recommendation. No medication-use practice should change based on this paper alone.

Evaluation and appraisal

Overall appraisal: Read first if building or evaluating AI-assisted ADE extraction, pharmacovigilance NLP, or note-to-structured-safety pipelines. Use as a feasibility and design paper, not as deployment-grade evidence.

Main strengths

  • Clinically meaningful task definition centered on reasonable possibility of causation rather than mere co-occurrence.
  • Full-note extraction instead of sentence-only classification.
  • Reviewer workflow that identified both LLM false positives and human gold-standard misses.
  • Regulatory-useful attributes: seriousness, unlabeled status, present illness, failure of efficacy, MedDRA LLT, medication changes, and verbatim evidence.
  • Operationally relevant cost, no-ADE triage rate, and error taxonomy.

Main limitations

  • Single-institution, deidentified UCSF notes; no external validation.
  • Recall is an estimate from a 100-note subset, not full manual annotation of all 372 notes.
  • No direct baseline comparison on the same dataset.
  • Reviewer 1 was involved in prompting/scripting and validation, which can introduce bias.
  • Accuracy is less interpretable because true negatives were constructed as one TN per note with no ADE from LLM or Reviewer 2.
  • Model version is proprietary and described by the authors as retiring, limiting future replication.
  • Structured terminology and label-status outputs require deterministic verification; the model produced non-existent MedDRA LLTs.
  • The study did not test whether extracted ADEs improve actual signal detection, regulatory action, or patient safety outcomes.
Do not overclaim. The paper does not show that an LLM can autonomously discover rare unlabeled ADEs at regulatory scale. It shows that, in a small single-site validation, an LLM can extract many ADEs with high precision, including clinician-suspected unlabeled cases and some plausible inferred labeled ADEs.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Best use nowUse as a design reference for a human-reviewed ADE extraction prototype, error taxonomy, schema planning, and validation study design.
Do not use forAutomated individual case reporting, regulatory action, clinical medication decisions, label updates, or safety alerts without independent validation.
Workflow implicationUse a two-pass architecture: broad note screening first, structured enrichment only for candidate ADEs.
Human reviewRequire expert review for unlabeled ADEs, serious ADEs, LLM-inferred causality, ambiguous chronology, redacted text, and failure-of-efficacy signals.
Controlled terminologyValidate MedDRA LLTs against the official dictionary before storage, analysis, or reporting. Never accept plausible generated LLTs as authoritative.
Label statusUse retrieval from current FDA labeling or a frozen label corpus; do not rely on model memory for labeled/unlabeled classification.
PrivacyPrefer local/open-source models or secure institutional gateways for notes. Third-party API use needs privacy, legal, and data-governance review.
Validation designBefore deployment, run multi-site external validation, repeated-run stability testing, baseline comparison, adjudicated full gold standards, and downstream signal-analysis evaluation.

Clinical practice bottom line

No direct medication-use practice change. The paper concerns extraction of ADE signals from notes, not comparative drug safety, diagnosis, prevention, dosing, or treatment. Evidence is insufficient for patient-level clinical action.

Build-useful bottom line

Build a constrained, audited prototype rather than a free-running extractor. The highest-value implementation is a note-to-ADE candidate generator that preserves verbatim evidence, validates terminology, verifies label status through retrieval, separates event subclasses, and queues high-risk outputs for expert review.
