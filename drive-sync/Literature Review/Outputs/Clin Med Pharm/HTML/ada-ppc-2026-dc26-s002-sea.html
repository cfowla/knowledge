One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Diabetes diagnosis should use standardized A1C or plasma-glucose criteria with confirmation when hyperglycemia is not unequivocal, followed by etiologic classification because test choice, monitoring, prognosis, genetic evaluation, and treatment pathways depend on diabetes type and clinical context.

The chapter is unusually implementation-dense. It provides explicit nonpregnant diagnostic thresholds, prediabetes criteria, type 1 diabetes staging, adult and pediatric screening criteria, special monitoring for oncology therapies, CFRD and PTDM pathways, genetic testing triggers for neonatal diabetes/MODY, and both accepted GDM diagnostic strategies. The highest-value nuance is not a single threshold but the chapter’s insistence on context: A1C can mislead when red-cell biology changes; adult type 1 classification may require antibodies/C-peptide/genetics; early posttransplant hyperglycemia is not automatically PTDM; CFRD screening differs from usual diabetes screening; and GDM one-step versus two-step strategies trade greater case identification against uncertain incremental outcome benefit.

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-utility reference for diagnostic thresholds and classification workflows. Keep it available at the point of clinical decision-making, but use the reference queue when a chapter-level secondary claim needs independent verification.

Section condensation

Diagnostic Tests for Diabetes

The chapter permits diagnosis with A1C or plasma glucose. In nonpregnant individuals, Table 2.1 gives four routes: A1C ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL during a 75-g OGTT, or random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL in the setting of classic hyperglycemic symptoms or hyperglycemic crisis. Without unequivocal hyperglycemia, confirmation is required. The chapter notes that the 2-hour OGTT identifies more people with prediabetes and diabetes than fasting glucose or A1C, while practical choice depends on access, convenience, variability, and analytic limitations.

A1C must use NGSP-certified methods traceable to DCCT, and point-of-care diagnostic use is restricted to appropriately certified settings and approved devices. The chapter emphasizes discordance as a signal to investigate assay or physiologic interference and recommends plasma glucose when A1C–glycemia relationships are altered, including some hemoglobin variants, pregnancy, G6PD deficiency, HIV, and altered red-cell turnover.

Classification

The chapter retains etiologic categories and emphasizes classification because management, education, technology, and prognosis differ by diabetes type. Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune or idiopathic; type 2 diabetes is the most common category; other specific types include monogenic diabetes and disease of the exocrine pancreas; and gestational diabetes is diagnosed in pregnancy when overt preexisting diabetes is not established.

Classification may be difficult at presentation, especially in adults. Figure 2.1 provides a practical pathway for suspected adult type 1 diabetes using autoantibodies, age, monogenic clues, and C-peptide. The figure explicitly warns that no single clinical feature confirms type 1 diabetes and that the algorithm is based on White European populations, limiting uncritical extrapolation.

Type 1 Diabetes

The chapter frames type 1 diabetes as a staged autoimmune disease. Stage 1 combines at least two islet autoantibodies with normoglycemia; stage 2 adds dysglycemia without overt diabetes; stage 3 is clinical diabetes. It reports approximately 44% 5-year progression from stage 1 and approximately 60% 2-year and 75% 5-year progression from stage 2. Autoantibodies include insulin, GAD, IA-2, and ZnT8.

Screening is recommended for people with familial or genetic risk, followed by metabolic evaluation and staging when antibodies are detected. Multiple autoantibodies warrant specialized-center referral and consideration of prevention trials or approved therapy such as teplizumab. A single IA-2 antibody is treated as higher risk; the chapter also gives repeat-testing intervals for isolated autoantibody positivity and recommends standardized autoantibody testing in adults with overlapping type 1/type 2 phenotypes.

Prediabetes and Type 2 Diabetes

Prediabetes is defined by A1C 5.7–6.4%, fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL, or 2-hour OGTT glucose 140–199 mg/dL. The chapter cites strong graded risk: in a systematic review of 44,203 people, A1C 5.5–6.0% corresponded to 5-year diabetes incidence of 9–25%, while A1C 6.0–6.5% corresponded to 25–50% and approximately 20-fold higher risk than A1C 5.0%. Type 2 diabetes is reported to account for 90–95% of diabetes.

Adult screening is risk-based before age 35 and universal starting at 35, with at least 3-year repeat intervals when results are normal. Pediatric risk-based screening begins after puberty or at age 10, whichever comes first, when overweight/obesity coexists with an additional risk factor. The chapter also addresses medication-associated risk, HIV, second-generation antipsychotics, and glucocorticoids, emphasizing that screening strategy should be matched to the clinical setting.

Diabetes Induced by Systemic Anti-Cancer Therapy

The chapter highlights hyperglycemia and diabetes with immune checkpoint inhibitors, PI3K-alpha inhibitors, and mTOR inhibitors. It requires symptom/emergency education for all three categories, including diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state.

Monitoring differs by therapy: glucose at baseline and each visit for immune checkpoint inhibitors; baseline glucose/A1C with early weekly then four-weekly glucose monitoring for PI3K-alpha inhibitors; and baseline plus each-visit glucose monitoring for mTOR inhibitors. A1C every 3 months is considered for PI3K-alpha and mTOR inhibitor therapy.

Pancreatic Diabetes and CFRD

Diabetes of the exocrine pancreas includes pancreatitis-related diabetes and cystic fibrosis-related diabetes. The chapter recommends diabetes screening 3–6 months after acute pancreatitis and annually thereafter, and annual screening in chronic pancreatitis. CFRD is reported in about 20% of adolescents and 50% of adults with cystic fibrosis and is associated with worse nutrition, morbidity, and mortality.

Annual OGTT beginning by age 10 is preferred for CFRD. When OGTT is not feasible, the chapter supports a two-step A1C approach: A1C 5.5–6.4% prompts OGTT within 3 months, and A1C ≥6.5% is consistent with CFRD. It cites diagnostic-performance data for A1C but explicitly does not endorse CGM for CFRD screening because validated diagnostic thresholds are lacking.

Posttransplantation Diabetes Mellitus

Early posttransplant hyperglycemia is common; the chapter reports approximately 90% of kidney allograft recipients have hyperglycemia in the first few weeks. Formal PTDM diagnosis should wait until maintenance immunosuppression is stable, usually around 3 months, and acute infection is absent.

OGTT is the preferred diagnostic test. The chapter stresses that graft- and patient-optimal immunosuppression should not be compromised solely to reduce PTDM risk. Evidence for specific glucose-lowering therapies in PTDM is limited because few randomized trials have addressed this population.

Monogenic Diabetes Syndromes

Monogenic diabetes represents less than 5% of diabetes but has disproportionate diagnostic value because correct molecular diagnosis can change therapy and family counseling. Diabetes diagnosed before 6 months strongly suggests a monogenic cause; the chapter reports that about 80–85% of such cases have an identifiable monogenic etiology.

All people diagnosed before 6 months should have neonatal-diabetes genetic testing, regardless of current age. MODY testing is recommended for young people with atypical type 1/type 2 features and autosomal-dominant family patterns. The chapter summarizes HNF1A-, GCK-, HNF4A-, and HNF1B-MODY and neonatal causes such as KCNJ11, ABCC8, INS, FOXP3, and 6q24 abnormalities, emphasizing gene-specific treatment implications.

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

The chapter addresses early pregnancy detection of overt diabetes and abnormal glucose metabolism, routine GDM screening at 24–28 weeks, postpartum OGTT at 4–12 weeks, and lifelong 1–3-year screening after GDM. Table 2.8 presents both accepted diagnostic pathways. The one-step 75-g OGTT diagnoses GDM with any one of fasting 92, 1-hour 180, or 2-hour 153 mg/dL. The two-step strategy uses a 50-g screen followed by a 100-g OGTT with Carpenter-Coustan thresholds.

Evidence does not establish a single clearly superior population strategy. The one-step approach detects substantially more GDM; HAPO and follow-up studies support continuous risk relationships and later metabolic risk, but a randomized one-step versus two-step screening trial found more diagnoses without improved pregnancy/perinatal outcomes. A 2021 USPSTF review similarly reported 11.5% versus 4.9% GDM diagnosis without improved health outcomes. The chapter therefore presents the choice as a balance of outcome-based criteria, intervention evidence, infrastructure, cost, and implementation consequences.

Figures, tables, and workflow

Every main-text visual is reconciled below as a structured representation; no screenshot was required because the load-bearing content can be reconstructed faithfully in text.

Table 2.1 — Diabetes diagnostic criteria

A1C ≥6.5%; FPG ≥126 mg/dL; 2-h PG ≥200 mg/dL after 75-g OGTT; or random glucose ≥200 mg/dL with classic symptoms/hyperglycemic crisis. Confirm with a second abnormal result unless hyperglycemia is unequivocal.

Table 2.2 — Prediabetes criteria

A1C 5.7–6.4%; FPG 100–125 mg/dL; 2-h PG 140–199 mg/dL after 75-g OGTT.

Table 2.3 — Glucose versus A1C

Glucose is lower cost and directly measures glycemia but is less preanalytically stable and more variable; A1C is convenient, stable, nonfasting, and chronic but can be less available and affected by nonglycemic factors.

Figure 2.1 — Suspected adult type 1 diabetes

Start with islet autoantibodies. If positive, type 1 diabetes is supported. If negative, integrate age, monogenic features, and C-peptide; consider genetic testing when indicated. No single clinical feature confirms type 1 diabetes. Algorithm derived from White European populations.

Table 2.4 — Type 1 diabetes staging

Stage 1: multiple autoantibodies + normoglycemia. Stage 2: autoantibodies + dysglycemia, still presymptomatic. Stage 3: overt diabetes/hyperglycemia, often symptomatic.

Table 2.5 — Adult screening

Screen adults with overweight/obesity plus risk factors; screen all others from age 35; repeat at least every 3 years if normal; continue lifelong screening after GDM; consider medication-associated risk.

Table 2.6 — Youth screening

Overweight/obesity plus at least one risk factor; begin after puberty or age 10, whichever occurs earlier; repeat at least every 3 years when normal.

Table 2.7 — Monogenic diabetes

Highlights HNF1A-, GCK-, HNF4A-, HNF1B-MODY and neonatal causes KCNJ11, ABCC8, INS, FOXP3, and 6q24, with treatment/syndromic clues.

Table 2.8 — GDM diagnosis

One-step: 75-g OGTT, any one value ≥92 fasting, ≥180 at 1 h, or ≥153 at 2 h. Two-step: 50-g 1-h screen ≥130/135/140 followed by 100-g OGTT; Carpenter-Coustan thresholds 95/180/155/140 mg/dL fasting/1/2/3 h, generally ≥2 abnormal values; ACOG notes one elevated value can be used.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance — 10/10

Rationale: Foundational diagnostic and classification guidance for routine diabetes practice.

Evidence basis: Explicit diagnostic thresholds and testing pathways across common and special populations.

Main limiter: It is one chapter and does not contain treatment details from other Standards sections.

Would change with: Would fall if the user’s task were unrelated to diagnosis/classification.

Novelty — 6/10

Rationale: An annual standards update consolidating current diagnostic evidence rather than a single novel study.

Evidence basis: Updated 2026 recommendations, special-population monitoring, and current GDM evidence synthesis.

Main limiter: Much of the diagnostic framework is established and iterative.

Would change with: Would rise with clearly identified major departures from prior guidance, which were not assessed here.

Method strength — 7/10

Rationale: Recommendations are explicitly graded and evidence-linked, but this chapter does not contain the full ADA development methodology.

Evidence basis: Recommendation grades A/B/C/E, cited evidence, structured diagnostic criteria, and committee authorship.

Main limiter: Full search, evidence-selection, conflict-management, and grading methods are delegated to a separate methodology source.

Would change with: Would rise after direct appraisal of the ADA methodology and evidence tables.

Evidence strength — 8/10

Rationale: Core diagnostic thresholds are mature, but evidence varies by topic and some areas rely on consensus or incomplete comparative trials.

Evidence basis: Strong diagnostic evidence plus staged T1D, risk cohorts, and pregnancy evidence; several A/B recommendations.

Main limiter: GDM strategy comparisons, PTDM therapies, CFRD CGM thresholds, and some screening intervals have important gaps or expert-opinion grades.

Would change with: Would rise with direct primary-source appraisal and stronger comparative trials in identified gaps.

External validity — 8/10

Rationale: Broad clinical coverage with explicit attention to multiple ages and special populations.

Evidence basis: Adult, pediatric, pregnancy, transplant, cancer-therapy, cystic-fibrosis, and monogenic pathways are addressed.

Main limiter: Figure 2.1 is based on White European populations; assay access and pregnancy screening practices vary.

Would change with: Would rise with more diverse validation of classification algorithms and implementation studies.

Implementation value — 10/10

Rationale: The chapter is highly actionable because it specifies thresholds, intervals, trigger conditions, and preferred tests.

Evidence basis: Eight tables, a classification flowchart, and numbered recommendations directly support clinical workflows.

Main limiter: Local laboratory, obstetric, transplant, oncology, and genetics pathways still require operational adaptation.

Would change with: Would fall only where local policy or resource constraints prevent implementation.

Strengths

  • Explicit, actionable thresholds and intervals across multiple diagnostic contexts.
  • Clear distinction between screening, diagnosis, confirmation, etiologic classification, and longitudinal risk.
  • Recommendation grades are presented at point of use.
  • Special-population guidance addresses common sources of diagnostic error.

Limitations and uncertainty

  • The full guideline-development methodology is not contained in this chapter, limiting independent appraisal of search completeness and grading process.
  • Several recommendations are grade C or E, and comparative evidence is weak in some areas such as PTDM therapies and GDM screening strategy.
  • Figure 2.1 is explicitly based on White European populations.
  • Chapter-level summaries of cited studies are secondary evidence and should not be treated as primary-study atoms.

Do not overclaim: the chapter does not prove that one GDM screening strategy is universally superior, validate CGM as a CFRD diagnostic test, or support changing graft-optimal immunosuppression solely to reduce PTDM risk.
