Scope warning: This study describes pediatric invasive MRSA epidemiology and trends from 2005-2010. It does not test treatment strategies or establish the effectiveness of prevention interventions, and its surveillance window is historical for current practice.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Pediatric invasive MRSA trends differed from contemporaneous adult patterns. Community-associated invasive MRSA increased among children older than 3 months, while health care-associated categories did not show significant reductions; young infants and Black children had the highest estimated incidence.

The investigators analyzed laboratory-confirmed invasive MRSA cases captured by CDC Active Bacterial Core Surveillance in selected counties across nine US metropolitan areas. Cases were categorized as hospital-onset, health care-associated community-onset, or community-associated according to culture timing and documented health care exposures. Trend models used Poisson or negative binomial regression, with continuous-year effects and demographic adjustment; national 2010 burden estimates were projected using Census population distributions.

Across 876 cases in 834 patients, 42% of classified cases were community-associated, 35% hospital-onset, and 23% HACO. Among children aged 3 months-17 years, community-associated incidence increased 10.2% per year (95% CI 2.7%-18.3%), whereas HACO and hospital-onset trends were not statistically significant. Late-onset hospital-onset infection among infants aged 3-89 days declined 11.3% per year (95% CI -19.6% to -2.0%). For 2010, estimated national incidence was 2.6 per 100,000 overall, 43.9 per 100,000 in infants aged 3-89 days, and 6.7 per 100,000 in Black children.

Section condensation — Methods

Surveillance population and case definitions

ABCs used active, population-based laboratory surveillance in selected counties across nine metropolitan areas, covering about 4.4 million people younger than 18 years. An invasive MRSA case required MRSA from a normally sterile site in a surveillance-area resident; qualifying repeat cultures were separated by at least 30 days. Medical records supplied demographics, outcomes, and health care risk factors.

HO was defined by culture on or after hospital day 4. HACO required outpatient or early-in-hospital culture plus an established health care risk factor; CA used the same culture-timing window without a documented health care risk factor. Infants younger than 3 days were considered early-onset and infants 3-89 days late-onset. These categories are explicit and reproducible, but they remain vulnerable to incomplete documentation of prior health care exposure.

Laboratory and statistical methods

A convenience subset of isolates underwent CDC confirmation, susceptibility testing, and molecular characterization. Beginning in 2008, submitted isolates could be assigned PFGE type directly or by inference from microbiologic characteristics. Incidence denominators came from bridged-race Census estimates; unknown race was imputed within surveillance areas, and the population younger than 90 days was approximated as one-quarter of the population younger than one year.

Time trends were estimated using Poisson regression or negative binomial models when overdispersion was present. Trend analyses were restricted to areas reporting continuously across the study period, and models adjusted for age, gender, and race as applicable. National 2010 counts were projected by applying surveillance incidence to the US age/race distribution; uncertainty used gamma-based confidence intervals.

Section condensation — Results

Cases and clinical phenotype

The surveillance system identified 876 invasive MRSA cases among 834 children. Ninety-one percent of cases were hospitalized and 6.1% were fatal. Of 857 cases with epidemiologic classification, 42% were CA, 35% HO, and 23% HACO. Infants aged 3-89 days were dominated by HO infection (151/190), while the highest CA proportion occurred in children aged 5-10 years (92/130).

Bloodstream infection occurred in 79% of cases. Bone/joint infection, skin/soft-tissue infection, and pneumonia/empyema were also common. Outcomes differed sharply by epidemiologic category: tabled case fatality was 14.4% for HO, 2.0% for HACO, and 1.7% for CA; median hospital stay was 60, 10, and 9 days, respectively. These comparisons are descriptive and reflect differing patient complexity across categories rather than adjusted causal contrasts.

Isolate characteristics

Only 257 cases (29%) had isolates submitted for CDC characterization. USA300 accounted for 72% and USA100 for 18% of submitted isolates. USA300 was frequent across categories, including 84.3% of CA, 62.5% of HACO, and 54.5% of HO isolates.

The authors cautioned that the submitted isolate subset was a convenience sample and should not be treated as representative of all pediatric cases. The molecular epidemiology results therefore support descriptive strain-pattern observations rather than population-wide strain prevalence estimates.

Incidence trends and national burden

Late-onset invasive MRSA among infants aged 3-89 days declined 9.8% annually, and the HO subset declined 11.3% annually. Among children aged 3 months-17 years, CA incidence increased 10.2% annually, while HACO (-2.6%) and HO (-8.7%) estimates had confidence intervals crossing zero. Increases in CA incidence were reported across seven of nine surveillance sites.

For 2010, the authors estimated 1,895 invasive MRSA infections nationally in children younger than 18 years, with an overall adjusted incidence of 2.6 per 100,000. Estimated incidence was highest in infants aged 3-89 days (43.9 per 100,000), higher in Black than White children (6.7 vs 1.6 per 100,000), and higher in males than females (3.0 vs 2.1 per 100,000).

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. Best used as a historically important pediatric MRSA surveillance study and as a model for case definitions/trend analysis. Do not use it alone to infer current 2026 incidence, current strain distribution, treatment decisions, or the effectiveness of prevention interventions.
Relevance7/10

High relevance to pediatric invasive MRSA epidemiology and surveillance methods, but limited direct treatment guidance.

Evidence basis: Population-based active surveillance, age/race risk patterns, epidemiologic categories, and national burden estimation.

Limiting factor: The source addresses epidemiology rather than pharmacotherapy or current bedside management.

Would change with: Higher score if paired with current pediatric MRSA prevention/treatment guidance.

Novelty8/10

At publication, the study provided pediatric-specific incidence trends and national burden estimates that were less established than adult trends.

Evidence basis: Six years of ABCs surveillance with age-specific and epidemiologic-category trend analyses.

Limiting factor: Novelty is historical and diminished by the 2005-2010 observation window.

Would change with: Contemporary multi-year pediatric surveillance extending the same methods.

Method strength7/10

Active population-based surveillance and explicit case definitions support internal consistency.

Evidence basis: Laboratory-confirmed sterile-site cases, continuous-area trend restriction, adjusted regression models, and predefined epidemiologic categories.

Limiting factor: Catchment areas changed, some denominators were approximated, and isolate typing used a convenience sample.

Would change with: Nationally representative denominators, complete isolate sampling, and prespecified sensitivity analyses.

Evidence strength7/10

The data support descriptive incidence and trend claims with reported confidence intervals and population denominators.

Evidence basis: 876 cases, modeled annual changes with 95% CIs, and national estimates with uncertainty.

Limiting factor: Observational surveillance cannot establish prevention-effect causality, and some subgroup estimates are imprecise.

Would change with: Independent replication with current data and stronger sensitivity analyses.

External validity6/10

Nine geographically diverse metropolitan areas improve breadth, but national representativeness was not established.

Evidence basis: Large ABCs catchment and national standardization using Census distributions.

Limiting factor: The authors explicitly state that surveillance populations may not represent the US nationally.

Would change with: Broader or nationally representative surveillance with stable catchment coverage.

Implementation value4/10

Useful for identifying risk groups and surveillance priorities, but insufficient alone to change current clinical practice.

Evidence basis: Clear case definitions, epidemiologic categories, and prevention-priority discussion.

Limiting factor: The data end in 2010 and do not test treatment or prevention interventions.

Would change with: Current surveillance plus intervention-effectiveness evidence and guideline linkage.

Main strengths

  • Active, laboratory-confirmed, population-based surveillance with explicit clinical classification rules.
  • Large multicenter pediatric dataset with adjusted temporal models and uncertainty intervals.
  • Separate analysis of infants, epidemiologic categories, age, race, and national burden.
  • Trend analyses restricted to continuously reporting areas to reduce denominator/catchment instability.

Main limitations

  • National representativeness of the nine metropolitan-area surveillance populations was unknown.
  • Only 29% of cases contributed isolates for molecular characterization, using a convenience sample.
  • Infant denominators under 90 days were approximated from annual infant population estimates.
  • Early-onset neonatal trend analysis was underpowered.
  • Medical-record abstraction could miss health care exposures and misclassify epidemiologic category.
  • The surveillance period ends in 2010, limiting current practice translation.
Claim boundary: The discussion speculates that NICU infection-prevention progress may partly explain the infant HO decline, but the study did not test that causal mechanism.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Reuse the explicit HO/HACO/CA case definitions and surveillance-analysis structure when comparing historical pediatric MRSA epidemiology.
Monitor
Risk concentration in very young infants and substantial differences by race are important surveillance signals, but require contemporary confirmation before operational decisions.
Avoid overclaiming
Do not interpret category-specific mortality or length of stay as causal effects of acquisition setting; underlying illness differs markedly across groups.
