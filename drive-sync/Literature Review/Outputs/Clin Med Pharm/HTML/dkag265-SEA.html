One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a large cohort of patients carrying a coded G6PD-deficiency diagnosis, outpatient oral quinolone dispensations were not associated with a statistically detectable major increase in 30-day haemolysis-related diagnosis codes compared with oral penicillins. The primary aOR was 1.29 (95% CI 0.92–1.77), with absolute event rates below 1 per 1000 episodes in both groups.

The study analyzed 465,083 eligible dispensations among 64,861 patients: 65,906 quinolone episodes and 399,177 penicillin episodes. The active comparator is a major strength because infection itself can precipitate haemolysis and can confound comparisons with unexposed patients. The investigators also performed within-patient, paediatric, and agent-specific analyses and triangulated coded outcomes with bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocyte, and haemoglobin data.

The main caution is that “no detectable major increase” is not equivalent to “no risk.” G6PD deficiency was identified from diagnosis codes rather than quantitative enzyme activity or genotype; laboratory testing was selective; pharmacy dispensation did not establish ingestion or dose; infection severity and concurrent oxidant exposures were unavailable; and events were sparse in children and for agents other than ciprofloxacin/ofloxacin. Jaundice coding was more frequent after quinolones, but the broader laboratory pattern was discordant rather than consistent with clinically important oxidant haemolysis.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction

G6PD deficiency can predispose to haemolysis during oxidative stress, while quinolone warnings have historically rested heavily on case reports. The authors frame an unresolved prescribing question: whether quinolones meaningfully increase haemolysis risk relative to another antibiotic class in G6PD-deficient patients, given infection itself can trigger haemolysis and confound drug attribution.

The study therefore compares oral quinolones with oral penicillins rather than with non-use, adds laboratory markers to diagnosis-code outcomes, and prespecifies secondary analyses in children, within-patient antibiotic-class exposure, and individual agents.

Patients and methods

Clalit Health Services longitudinal EHR data were used for a 2010–2023 retrospective active-comparator cohort. One qualifying D55.0 or D75.A code was sufficient for G6PD ascertainment; laboratory confirmation, enzyme activity, genotype, and variant severity were not required. Exposure was an outpatient community dispensation of oral quinolone or penicillin; episodes within 30 days of each other were constrained, and windows containing both classes were excluded. Dose, duration, indication, adherence, and IV exposures were unavailable or excluded.

The primary outcome was a haemolysis-related diagnosis code within 30 days. Jaundice and laboratory markers were secondary. Primary models adjusted for age, sex, and CCI. Laboratory analyses were complete-case; secondary outcomes were exploratory without multiplicity correction. Repeated exposures were addressed with GEE/mixed-effects approaches. Paediatric analysis used age ≤18 years. Ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin were analyzed individually; levofloxacin and moxifloxacin were too rare for stable comparative models.

Results — primary cohort

The cohort contained 465,083 dispensations among 64,861 patients. Quinolone recipients were older, more often female, and had higher comorbidity burden. Haemolysis-related diagnosis codes occurred after 52/65,906 quinolone episodes (0.079%) versus 198/399,177 penicillin episodes (0.050%); aOR 1.29 (95% CI 0.92–1.77; P=0.12). The source reports an absolute difference of 0.029%, approximately 3 additional coded events per 10,000 dispensations if causal.

Jaundice coding was higher after quinolones (aOR 3.07), but laboratory outcomes were inconsistent with a coherent haemolytic syndrome: bilirubin and reticulocytosis were not significantly increased in the main cohort, LDH elevation was slightly less frequent, haptoglobin did not differ materially, and the adjusted haemoglobin-decline difference was small (0.068 g/dL).

Results — within-patient sensitivity analysis

Among 22,247 patients exposed to both antibiotic classes, 59,889 quinolone and 173,983 penicillin episodes were analyzed. Haemolysis-related codes occurred after 0.065% versus 0.061% of episodes; adjusted OR 0.949 (95% CI 0.647–1.366; P=0.785). A complementary patient-level analysis likewise did not show increased risk after quinolones (aOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.12–1.30).

Jaundice codes and bilirubin >1.2 mg/dL remained somewhat more frequent after quinolones, but LDH was less frequent and haptoglobin/reticulocyte differences were not material. Haemoglobin decline did not support a larger decrement with quinolones.

Results — paediatric subgroup

The paediatric analysis included 169,288 episodes among 27,572 patients aged ≤18 years. Haemolysis-related codes were almost identical at the episode level: 5/8865 quinolone episodes (0.056%) versus 91/160,423 penicillin episodes (0.057%); aOR 1.017 (95% CI 0.358–2.257; P=0.970). The patient-level estimate was imprecise (aOR 1.7, 95% CI 0.56–4.21).

Laboratory findings again did not form a haemolysis signal. However, only five coded events followed quinolone exposure, so the analysis cannot exclude uncommon risk in neonates, very young children, or severe enzymatic deficiency.

Results — individual quinolones

Agent-level information was available for 65,903/65,906 quinolone episodes: ciprofloxacin 55.6%, ofloxacin 44.3%, levofloxacin 0.13%, and moxifloxacin 0.01%. No norfloxacin or nalidixic acid episodes were identified.

In within-patient comparisons, ciprofloxacin was not associated with increased coded haemolysis versus penicillins (aOR 0.45, 95% CI 0.13–1.10; P=0.23). The ofloxacin estimate was extremely unstable because of sparse events (aOR 0.001, 95% CI 0.0001–19.01), so it should not be interpreted as a reliable protective effect.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the primary, within-patient, paediatric, and ciprofloxacin findings as absence of a detectable major population-level signal rather than proof of complete individual safety. They argue the discordance between jaundice coding and the broader laboratory profile weakens a causal haemolysis interpretation. They also caution that the study should not justify broader quinolone use or supersede antimicrobial-stewardship considerations and class-specific adverse effects.

Major limitations are coded rather than laboratory-confirmed G6PD deficiency; absent genotype/enzyme severity; possible exposure misclassification from dispensations; residual confounding from infection severity and concurrent oxidants; selective laboratory testing and surveillance bias; missing transfusion/time-to-event data; exploratory secondary/subgroup analyses without multiplicity adjustment; and very limited data for levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, older quinolones, neonates, and severe deficiency.

Conclusion

The source concludes that oral quinolone dispensations were not associated with a detectable major increase in coded haemolysis compared with oral penicillins. It supports individualized prescribing and vigilance rather than automatic class-wide avoidance based solely on a G6PD diagnosis.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High practical relevance for antimicrobial prescribing in G6PD deficiency and a stronger comparative design than case-report-based warnings, but not strong enough to establish class-wide safety or override patient-specific risk factors.
Relevance8/10

Directly addresses a common medication-avoidance question with stewardship implications.

Evidence basis: large outpatient cohort, clinically recognizable comparator, paediatric and agent analyses. Limit: coded phenotype and outcome. Would rise: prospective or laboratory-confirmed high-risk cohorts.

Novelty7/10

Adds active-comparator and within-patient epidemiologic evidence to a literature historically dominated by case reports and avoidance lists.

Limit: same healthcare system as prior related work. Would rise: independent external replication with genotype/enzyme severity.

Method strength7/10

Active comparator, large sample, episode windows, repeated-measures methods, and laboratory triangulation improve causal interpretability.

Limit: retrospective EHR design, major baseline differences, residual confounding, exposure/outcome misclassification. Would rise: richer indication/severity and oxidant-covariate capture.

Evidence strength6/10

Primary result excludes a very large population-level association but remains compatible with smaller effects; rare-event subgroups are imprecise.

Limit: wide CIs in key subgroups, selective laboratory testing, exploratory secondary endpoints. Would rise: more confirmed haemolysis events with adjudication.

External validity5/10

Nationwide Israeli data are broad, but phenotype severity is unknown and nearly all quinolone exposure was ciprofloxacin/ofloxacin.

Limit: uncertain transferability to severe G6PD variants, neonates, different prescribing systems, and other quinolones. Would rise: multi-country replication.

Implementation value7/10

Useful for reframing “automatic avoidance” into individualized assessment when a quinolone is otherwise appropriate.

Limit: does not specify a prospective monitoring protocol or define safe use by genotype/severity. Would rise: validated risk-stratified implementation guidance.

Validity and bias

  • Confounding: quinolone recipients were older and more comorbid; adjustment and within-patient analysis reduce but do not eliminate confounding.
  • Exposure measurement: dispensation is a proxy for treatment initiation and does not establish ingestion, dose, duration, or adherence.
  • Outcome ascertainment: diagnosis codes can reflect prevalent/recurrent disease and are not adjudicated acute drug-induced haemolysis.
  • Phenotype ascertainment: no quantitative G6PD activity or genotype; severe deficiency may be underrepresented or indistinguishable.
  • Laboratory selection: complete-case laboratory analyses use non-randomly tested subsets and are vulnerable to surveillance bias.
  • Multiplicity: secondary/subgroup analyses were exploratory without multiplicity correction; isolated significant findings should not be overinterpreted.
Do not overclaim: this study does not prove quinolones are universally safe in G6PD deficiency, does not establish safety of levofloxacin/moxifloxacin or older quinolones, and does not adequately exclude rare severe events in neonates or patients with severe enzyme deficiency.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What this supportsA coded G6PD diagnosis alone may be insufficient reason for automatic class-wide quinolone avoidance when the drug is otherwise clinically appropriate; individualized risk assessment is more consistent with these data.
What still mattersPrior haemolytic crises, known severe G6PD deficiency, very young age, active infection-related oxidative stress, baseline anaemia, renal/hepatic function, and concurrent oxidant medications remain relevant cautions.
Stewardship boundaryThe paper explicitly does not justify broader quinolone prescribing or override indication, resistance patterns, and quinolone class adverse effects.
Best next evidenceExternal cohorts with laboratory-confirmed G6PD status, quantitative enzyme activity/genotype, adjudicated haemolysis, infection severity, medication dose/adherence, and sufficient exposure to individual agents.
Bottom line: Useful pharmacovigilance evidence against a large common haemolysis signal from outpatient oral quinolones in coded G6PD deficiency. Most defensible use is to weaken categorical avoidance—not to infer zero risk.
