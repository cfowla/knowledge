Scope warning. This paper evaluates an institutional UFH dosing change using aPTT-based process outcomes in a historical, predominantly male VA cohort. It does not establish current guideline-concordant dosing, nor does it demonstrate differences in bleeding, recurrent thrombosis, mortality, or other patient-centered outcomes.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: At this VA facility, lowering the initial high-dose UFH infusion from 18 to 14 U/kg/h was associated with faster achievement of the local therapeutic aPTT range and fewer early supratherapeutic aPTT values, although the latter comparison did not reach conventional statistical significance.

The investigators retrospectively reviewed 129 hospitalized veterans treated from January 2009 through August 2011 under a high-dose weight-adjusted UFH protocol. The facility changed the initial infusion rate from 18 to 14 U/kg/h after January 2011. Sixty-one patients received 14 U/kg/h and 68 received 18 U/kg/h. The therapeutic aPTT range was 58–97 seconds, corresponding locally to anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 U/mL. The primary outcome was time to first therapeutic aPTT; secondary analyses included day-1/day-2 aPTT categories and the day-2 infusion rate among patients at goal.

Unadjusted mean time to therapeutic aPTT was 18.8 h with 14 U/kg/h versus 25.7 h with 18 U/kg/h (p=0.04). In a gamma log-link model adjusted for age, BMI, and initial bolus receipt, the 18 U/kg/h start had a coefficient of 0.34 (95% CI 0.11–0.57; p<0.01), which the authors interpreted as 1.41 times longer expected time to therapeutic aPTT. Day-1 supratherapeutic aPTT occurred in 39.3% versus 55.9% (p=0.06), while day-1 goal aPTT occurred in 42.6% versus 29.4% (p=0.12), for 14 versus 18 U/kg/h. Among patients therapeutic on day 2, mean maintenance rates were 12.1 versus 15.1 U/kg/h (p<0.01).

Load-bearing limitations: groups were nonconcurrent and nonrandomized; the 14 U/kg/h group received a higher median loading dose; documentation quality changed over time; only 4 women were included and all were in the 18 U/kg/h group; BMI ≥40 kg/m² was excluded; indications were heterogeneous; patients who never reached therapeutic aPTT were excluded from the primary model; and patient-centered efficacy/safety outcomes were not comprehensively measured.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames UFH infusion dosing as variable in practice and notes that the conventional 18 U/kg/h weight-based start can produce supratherapeutic aPTT. The stated objective was to determine whether 14 U/kg/h under the facility's high-dose protocol achieved therapeutic anticoagulation faster than 18 U/kg/h. The retrospective cohort included 129 patients.

The abstract reports initially elevated aPTTs with 18 U/kg/h and an adjusted 1.41-fold longer time to therapeutic aPTT than with 14 U/kg/h. It concludes that 14 U/kg/h may be beneficial and might reduce monitoring/titration workload. This is an author interpretation based on process outcomes, not a demonstrated reduction in clinical events.

Introduction

The introduction reviews VTE burden, reasons UFH remains useful, and the variability of individual anticoagulant response. It traces the 18 U/kg/h dose to prior weight-based nomogram evidence and ACCP adoption, while citing studies suggesting lower rates around 13–16 U/kg/h may better approximate actual infusion requirements and reduce supratherapeutic aPTT responses.

The local facility observed frequent supratherapeutic aPTTs with 18 U/kg/h and changed its high-dose protocol to 14 U/kg/h after multidisciplinary review. The study was designed as a validation of that local protocol change, with time to therapeutic aPTT as the principal outcome.

Methods — sample and study design

This was a single-site VA retrospective chart review covering January 2009–August 2011. Eligible patients were 18–89 years old, received the high-dose weight-adjusted IV UFH protocol at 14 or 18 U/kg/h for at least 12 h, and had at least two aPTT measurements. Coagulation disorders, liver failure, and BMI ≥40 kg/m² were excluded. Only the first qualifying course was used for repeat patients.

The key design feature is temporal rather than randomized assignment: the initial rate was lowered from 18 to 14 U/kg/h after January 2011. This creates a historical-control comparison and leaves secular changes in documentation, monitoring, clinical practice, and case mix as possible confounders.

Methods — measures and analysis

The exposure was initial infusion rate. The primary outcome was hours to aPTT 58–97 seconds, a range linked at that facility to anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 U/mL. Day-1 and day-2 morning aPTTs were categorized as subtherapeutic, at goal, or supratherapeutic. The day-2 infusion rate was recorded only among patients at goal.

Bivariate comparisons used chi-square/Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and t test or Mann–Whitney for continuous measures. A gamma-distribution generalized linear model with log link assessed time to therapeutic aPTT, adjusting for age (per decade), BMI, and initial bolus receipt. Gender was not included because only four women were present. McNemar testing evaluated day-1/day-2 marginal category changes.

Results

The cohort included 61 patients at 14 U/kg/h and 68 at 18 U/kg/h. Mean age was 64.4 years and mean BMI 27.8 kg/m². Only 4 patients were women, all in the 18 U/kg/h group. Indications were mixed rather than restricted to acute VTE. A bolus was given to 82.2% overall; median loading dose was higher in the 14 U/kg/h group (6,418 vs 5,000 U; p=0.03).

Of 129 patients, 120 reached therapeutic aPTT. Mean time to goal was 18.8 versus 25.7 h for 14 versus 18 U/kg/h (p=0.04). In the adjusted model, 18 versus 14 U/kg/h had coefficient 0.34 (95% CI 0.11–0.57; p<0.01), interpreted as 1.41 times longer expected time. BMI was independently associated with longer time (coefficient 0.04 per kg/m²; 95% CI 0.02–0.06; p<0.01). Day-1 supratherapeutic aPTT was more common with 18 U/kg/h (55.9% vs 39.3%; p=0.06), but between-group aPTT-category differences were not statistically significant. By day 2, 56.3% of those with measurements were at goal and 24.3% were supratherapeutic.

Discussion

The authors interpret the faster time to target with 14 U/kg/h as support for the facility's protocol revision. They argue that the difference appears driven by early overshooting with 18 U/kg/h. Among patients therapeutic on day 2, the mean maintenance rate was 13.6 U/kg/h overall, 12.1 U/kg/h after a 14 U/kg/h start, and 15.1 U/kg/h after an 18 U/kg/h start.

They also discuss potential workflow benefits from fewer titrations and note BMI as a predictor of slower time to goal. However, the discussion appropriately stops short of recommending an even lower universal start, because further reduction might increase subtherapeutic anticoagulation.

Limitations

The source explicitly identifies single-site retrospective design, an older predominantly male VA population, lack of generalizability to women, imperfect historical documentation, possible misclassification, unmeasured patient factors, and limited clinical outcome collection. The cohort also mixed acute VTE with other anticoagulation indications.

A particularly important analytical limitation is that patients who did not reach therapeutic aPTT were excluded from the generalized linear model; 8.8% of the 18 U/kg/h group versus 4.9% of the 14 U/kg/h group did not reach goal. The primary endpoint also captures only time to first therapeutic aPTT and not subsequent time in range or later excursions.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that 14 U/kg/h achieved therapeutic anticoagulation faster than 18 U/kg/h and that 18 U/kg/h produced initially elevated aPTTs. They suggest potential benefit from 14 U/kg/h and call for further study of lower initial rates and BMI-related adjustments.

For interpretation, the conclusion is supported for the measured surrogate/process outcome within this cohort, but it is not evidence that 14 U/kg/h improves bleeding, recurrent thrombosis, mortality, or net clinical benefit.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High topical relevance and an unusually explicit dosing/titration protocol make the paper useful for a heparin deep dive, but its nonrandom historical-control design and surrogate/process endpoints substantially limit causal and practice-changing inference.
Relevance
9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses initial IV UFH infusion rate and early anticoagulation control.

Evidence basis: head-to-head 14 vs 18 U/kg/h institutional protocol comparison with explicit aPTT target and titration table.

Limiting factor: historical and single-site.

Would rise: contemporary multicenter evidence with clinical outcomes.

Novelty
6/10

Rationale: Provides a direct comparison of 14 vs 18 U/kg/h in non-morbidly obese patients.

Evidence basis: local protocol change created the comparison.

Limiting factor: observational validation of an institutional change rather than a new mechanistic or randomized design.

Would rise: prospective dose-finding or adaptive randomized study.

Method strength
4/10

Rationale: Reasonable regression choice and transparent exclusions, but causal inference is weak.

Evidence basis: gamma log-link GLM adjusted for age, BMI, and bolus; N=120.

Limiting factor: nonconcurrent, nonrandom groups; residual confounding; differential documentation; outcome-based exclusion from model.

Would rise: concurrent randomization, prespecified missing-data strategy, fuller covariate control.

Evidence strength
4/10

Rationale: The aPTT process signal is internally coherent, but it is not a patient-centered benefit signal.

Evidence basis: adjusted coefficient 0.34 (95% CI 0.11–0.57); early supratherapeutic trend with 18 U/kg/h.

Limiting factor: no robust bleeding, recurrence, mortality, or time-in-therapeutic-range outcome.

Would rise: prospective clinical outcomes with uncertainty and safety assessment.

External validity
3/10

Rationale: Applicability is narrow.

Evidence basis: single VA site, mean age ~64, only 4 women, BMI ≥40 excluded, heterogeneous indications.

Limiting factor: sex, setting, obesity, indication, and local aPTT calibration.

Would rise: multisite validation across sex, body size, indications, and monitoring systems.

Implementation value
6/10

Rationale: The protocol is explicit enough to reconstruct and compare with local dosing workflows.

Evidence basis: complete loading/titration table and reported maintenance rates.

Limiting factor: evidence does not justify transplanting the protocol without local validation and contemporary review.

Would rise: current, externally validated protocol linked to bleeding/VTE outcomes and anti-Xa/aPTT performance.

Validity assessment

  • Selection/temporal bias: 18 U/kg/h was the earlier protocol and 14 U/kg/h the later protocol; secular changes could influence results.
  • Baseline imbalance: median loading dose differed (6,418 vs 5,000 U; p=0.03), and all women were in the 18 U/kg/h group.
  • Measurement bias: the authors report imperfect historical documentation and improved reliability after the protocol change, which could differentially affect groups.
  • Outcome conditioning: the main time model excluded patients who never reached therapeutic aPTT; failure-to-reach rates differed by group.
  • Surrogate/process outcome: aPTT target attainment is clinically relevant to UFH management but does not establish net clinical benefit.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Useful now

Use as historical evidence that a conventional 18 U/kg/h start can overshoot local aPTT targets in some populations and that institutional starting-rate optimization can be studied quantitatively.

Do not overclaim

Do not treat the study as proof that 14 U/kg/h is a universally superior starting dose or that it reduces bleeding or recurrent VTE.

Protocol-design lesson

Separate the initial rate, loading strategy, laboratory target calibration, titration increments, obesity handling, and patient indication when comparing UFH protocols; these components can confound one another.

Best follow-up question

Look for prospective or multicenter studies comparing lower versus 18 U/kg/h starts with anti-Xa/aPTT exposure, time-in-range, bleeding, recurrent thrombosis, and obesity/sex subgroups.
