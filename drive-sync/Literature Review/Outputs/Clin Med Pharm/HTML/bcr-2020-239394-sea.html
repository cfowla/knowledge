One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The case is worth retaining as a diagnostic caution: severe diarrhoea during dasatinib therapy should not automatically be attributed to the drug, and CDI may occur even without a recent antibiotic history. The report is not strong enough to support a causal dasatinib-CDI association or to guide modern CDI treatment.

The patient developed severe persistent diarrhoea roughly 2 months after starting dasatinib 140 mg/day, with 20-25 bowel movements per day and crampy abdominal pain. He reported no antibiotic or corticosteroid exposure and no other medication in the preceding 2 months. Endoscopy showed confluent erythema, ulceration and pseudomembrane formation; biopsy demonstrated inflammatory colitis; stool enzyme immunoassays were positive for C. difficile toxins A and B. Dasatinib was stopped and oral metronidazole 500 mg three times daily was started with supportive care.

Symptoms resolved by 2 weeks, repeat sigmoidoscopy at 14 days showed mucosal resolution, and metronidazole was continued for 3 weeks. Dasatinib was restarted after a month and no diarrhoea recurred over the next 2 years. That non-recurrence after rechallenge is an important counterweight to the paper's etiologic hypothesis: the case is persuasive for CDI diagnosis but not for dasatinib causality.

Section condensation

Summary / Background

The authors frame dasatinib as a tyrosine kinase inhibitor with recognized gastrointestinal adverse effects and state that CDI without antecedent antibiotic exposure had not previously been reported in this context. This novelty claim is author framing rather than independently demonstrated evidence.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old man with Philadelphia chromosome-positive accelerated-phase CML began dasatinib 140 mg/day in September 2016. After about 2 months he developed severe persistent diarrhoea (20-25 times/24 h) and crampy abdominal pain. He had no antibiotic or corticosteroid use and no other medication apart from dasatinib during the 2 months before symptom onset. Examination showed dehydration, tachycardia (108/min), blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg, no fever and no abdominal tenderness.

Investigations

Haemoglobin was 91 g/L. The article visibly prints the white blood cell count as −6.85×10^9/L; this source-printed sign is preserved here rather than silently normalized. Differential leucocyte count showed neutrophils 74%, lymphocytes 22%, monocytes 3% and eosinophils 1%; platelets were 237×10^9/L. CRP was 25 mg/L (reference <10). Liver and renal function tests and electrolytes were within reference ranges. Sigmoidoscopy showed confluent erythema, ulceration and pseudomembrane formation in the sigmoid colon and rectum. Biopsy showed crypt-architecture disruption, neutrophil-rich inflammation, fibrin and cryptitis, without leukaemic infiltration, dysplasia or organisms. Stool toxin A/B enzyme immunoassays were positive.

Source-integrity note: page 1 visibly prints the WBC value with a negative sign (−6.85×10^9/L). The prior SEA version silently rendered this as positive 6.85×10^9/L. This repair preserves the source as printed and treats the sign as an unresolved source-level anomaly.

Differential diagnosis

Because of immunosuppression and inflammatory markers, the authors considered common enteric pathogens, opportunistic infections including CMV, and neutropenic enterocolitis. CDI remained in the differential despite lack of prolonged antibiotic exposure; endoscopic pseudomembrane plus positive toxin testing led to the diagnosis.

Treatment

Dasatinib was discontinued. The patient received oral metronidazole 500 mg three times daily, oral rehydration solution and probiotics, with serial urea, creatinine and electrolyte monitoring.

Outcome and follow-up

Diarrhoea resolved by 2 weeks; repeat sigmoidoscopy after 14 days showed resolution of ulceration and normal-looking mucosa. Metronidazole was continued for 3 weeks. Dasatinib was restarted after a 1-month interruption, and no diarrhoea recurred during 2 years of follow-up.

Discussion / learning points

The authors propose that dasatinib may predispose to CDI through off-target kinase effects, impaired NK/T-regulatory-cell function, and reduced local intestinal immunity. Those mechanisms are literature-based hypotheses, not tested in this patient. The most defensible learning point is diagnostic rather than causal: in a patient on dasatinib with substantial diarrhoea, CDI remains possible even without recent antibiotics.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance7/10

Clinically useful signal for evaluating severe diarrhoea during dasatinib therapy and for keeping CDI in the differential even without recent antibiotics.

Evidence basis: Directly documented CDI case during dasatinib exposure.

Limiting factor: Narrow single-patient context.

Would change with: Additional cohort/pharmacovigilance evidence linking dasatinib to CDI would raise the score.

Novelty6/10

The authors present the case as a previously unreported association, but that novelty claim is not independently established by the case itself.

Evidence basis: Unusual CDI presentation without antecedent antibiotics.

Limiting factor: Priority/first-report claim depends on literature completeness.

Would change with: Independent systematic confirmation of first-report status would raise the score.

Method strength2/10

The diagnostic workup is reasonably documented, but a single uncontrolled case cannot establish drug causality.

Evidence basis: Clinical history, stool toxin testing, endoscopy, histology, dechallenge and long follow-up are reported.

Limiting factor: No comparator, formal causality tool, pharmacologic mechanism test, or controlled rechallenge.

Would change with: A case series or analytic study with exposure controls and causality assessment would raise the score.

Evidence strength3/10

Evidence is strong for CDI in this patient but weak for the claim that dasatinib caused or materially predisposed to it.

Evidence basis: Positive toxin A/B immunoassays and compatible pseudomembranous colitis; clinical/endoscopic resolution with CDI therapy.

Limiting factor: Dasatinib causality remains speculative, and lack of recurrence after restart weakens a simple causal interpretation.

Would change with: Consistent recurrence on rechallenge, mechanistic evidence, or epidemiologic association would raise the score.

External validity2/10

A single 36-year-old man with accelerated-phase CML provides very limited generalizability.

Evidence basis: Detailed individual case phenotype.

Limiting factor: No denominator or population sampling.

Would change with: Replicated cases across settings and larger cohorts would raise the score.

Implementation value5/10

The main practical value is diagnostic vigilance; the specific metronidazole regimen should not be copied as contemporary preferred therapy.

Evidence basis: Case demonstrates CDI can coexist with TKI-associated diarrhoea and no antibiotic history.

Limiting factor: Treatment guidance is not the purpose of the report and current CDI management has evolved.

Would change with: Concordance with current guidelines plus prospective diagnostic pathways would raise the score.

Principal validity problem: the report conflates temporal association with possible causation. Positive CDI testing and response to treatment prove the infectious episode; they do not show that dasatinib was the precipitating cause. The absence of recurrent diarrhoea after dasatinib restart further limits causal inference.

Main strength: the clinical phenotype is unusually well documented for a short case report, with medication history, laboratory values, endoscopic images, histology, toxin testing, treatment response, and 2-year follow-up.

Source-integrity issue: the article prints a negative WBC value (−6.85×10^9/L). Because the source itself contains this anomaly, the value is not corrected in the evidence record. Any positive-value interpretation would be an external repair, not reported data.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Keep CDI in the differential for severe diarrhoea in patients receiving dasatinib or other cancer therapies even when recent antibiotic exposure is absent.
Do not overclaim
Do not use this case to label dasatinib as a proven CDI cause, estimate incidence, or infer class-wide risk.
Treatment caveat
The source used metronidazole. Current IDSA/SHEA guidance available in the 2021 focused update prefers fidaxomicin for an initial adult CDI episode, with oral vancomycin an acceptable alternative; metronidazole is an alternative only for nonsevere CDI when preferred agents are unavailable.
