One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-utility dose-level synthesis for short-term knee/hip osteoarthritis pharmacotherapy, particularly when comparing NSAID efficacy against adverse-event discontinuation.

Core thesis. Across 192 randomized trials (102,829 participants) and 90 active regimens, several oral NSAIDs produced clinically important short-term pain reductions, whereas opioid regimens generally offered smaller clinical benefit with substantially greater adverse-event burden. Diclofenac 150 mg/day and etoricoxib 60 mg/day were highlighted by the authors as practical high-efficacy oral options; topical diclofenac 70–81 mg/day combined high probability of clinically important pain benefit with a more favorable short-term safety profile.

What the evidence actually supports. The article supports comparative six-week pain efficacy and short-term tolerability more strongly than long-term safety. Five oral regimens had ≥99% probability of exceeding the prespecified pain MID. Every opioid regimen had ≤53% probability of exceeding it, while 15/18 opioid regimens showed increased dropout due to adverse events. Only oxycodone ≥48 mg/day showed a statistically credible increase in serious adverse events, but serious-event estimates were generally imprecise.

Primary constraint: median follow-up was 8.6 weeks, multimorbid/frail patients were underrepresented, and cause-specific GI/CV harms were too sparse for dose-specific network estimates. The online web appendices referenced by the article were not included in the retrieved PDF.

Section condensation

Abstract / Introduction

The authors sought a clinically granular comparison of specific NSAID, opioid, and paracetamol preparations and doses rather than class-level averages. The motivating problem was that prior syntheses often pooled doses, limiting translation to lowest-effective-dose prescribing.

The paper frames knee and hip osteoarthritis as common, painful, function-limiting conditions for which pharmacotherapy must balance symptomatic benefit against gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, renal, and opioid-related harms.

Methods

Randomized English-language trials with an average of at least 100 participants per randomized arm were eligible. Searches covered CENTRAL, Medline, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO and company registries, regulatory/FDA documents, and reference lists through June 2021. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted, and assessed seven risk-of-bias domains.

Pain was primary; physical function and safety were secondary. The main time point was six weeks, with one- and 12-week sensitivity analyses. Bayesian random-effects network models estimated standardized mean differences for efficacy and odds ratios for safety. A -0.37 SD threshold (about 9 mm on a 100 mm VAS) defined the between-group MID.

Results — population and network

The review included 192 trials in 181 publications with 102,829 participants. Median trial size was 466; median follow-up was 8.6 weeks. The network contained 68 NSAID regimens, 19 opioid regimens, and three paracetamol regimens. Trial populations were generally middle-aged to older adults, but frailty and multimorbidity were incompletely represented.

Risk-of-bias concerns centered on missing outcomes and allocation concealment. Only 15% of trials were low risk for allocation concealment, while only 25–26% were low risk for incomplete pain/function data. Commercial funding was common (80%).

Results — pain and function

At six weeks, 38/51 oral NSAID, 3/9 topical NSAID, 4/18 opioid, and 1/3 paracetamol regimens were statistically superior to oral placebo for pain. Diclofenac 150, etoricoxib 60/90, and rofecoxib 25/50 mg/day each had ≥99% probability of exceeding the MID. Topical diclofenac 70–81 and 140–160 mg/day had ≥92.3% probability; all opioid regimens were ≤53%.

Physical-function results broadly tracked pain. Treatment effects were similar from one through 12 weeks. Dose-response signals were identified for celecoxib, etoricoxib, naproxcinod, and tramadol, although higher dose did not uniformly translate to an acceptable benefit-harm profile.

Results — safety

Increased dropout due to adverse events was supported for 10/54 oral NSAID, 0/8 topical NSAID, 15/18 opioid, and 1/3 paracetamol regimens. Increased risk of any adverse event was supported for 14/47 oral NSAID, 0/9 topical NSAID, 17/19 opioid regimens, and paracetamol 3900–4000 mg/day.

Oxycodone ≥48 mg/day was the only regimen with evidence of increased serious adverse events (OR 2.40, 95% CrI 1.09–5.60). Oxymorphone 80 mg/day had the highest modeled probabilities of adverse-event dropout (51%) and any adverse event (88%).

Discussion / Conclusion

The authors prioritize diclofenac 150 mg/day and etoricoxib 60 mg/day among oral options, while emphasizing that comorbidities and longer-term exposure make high-dose oral NSAID use less attractive. Topical diclofenac 70–81 mg/day is proposed as first-line pharmacologic treatment for knee OA because its pain benefit is clinically meaningful with lower systemic exposure.

The authors conclude that opioid benefits do not outweigh harms across evaluated preparations and doses. They also explicitly constrain inference by short follow-up, sparse cause-specific serious harms, missing-data bias, and limited representation of frail/multimorbid patients.

Evaluation and appraisal

9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly informs comparative, dose-specific pharmacotherapy choices for knee/hip osteoarthritis.

Evidence: 192 RCTs across 90 active regimens with pain, function, and safety outcomes.

Limiting factor: Does not address all modern non-pharmacologic or interventional options.

Would change score: Would rise with newer comparative evidence and broader patient-centered outcomes.

8/10

Novelty

Rationale: Dose- and preparation-level network estimates add more clinical granularity than class-level syntheses.

Evidence: Separate nodes for 68 NSAID, 19 opioid, and three paracetamol regimens.

Limiting factor: Some regimens are withdrawn or no longer commonly used.

Would change score: Would rise with formulation-specific and contemporary agent comparisons.

8/10

Method strength

Rationale: Large Bayesian network meta-analysis with duplicate review and longitudinal modeling is methodologically strong.

Evidence: 192 randomized trials, prespecified protocol/PROSPERO, Bayesian random-effects NMA, risk-of-bias analyses.

Limiting factor: English-only inclusion, ≥100/arm threshold, and substantial attrition-bias concerns.

Would change score: Would rise with IPD, stronger missing-data handling, and broader language inclusion.

7/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Short-term pain estimates are comparatively strong, while safety and serious-harm evidence is less certain.

Evidence: 170 trials contributed pain data; 168 contributed dropout-due-AE data; credible intervals reported.

Limiting factor: Short follow-up and sparse cause-specific serious harms constrain safety conclusions.

Would change score: Would rise with long-term randomized or high-quality linked safety evidence.

6/10

External validity

Rationale: Findings apply best to trial-eligible adults receiving short-term therapy.

Evidence: Large total sample and broad regimen coverage.

Limiting factor: Frailty/multimorbidity underrepresented; topical evidence was knee-only; median follow-up 8.6 weeks.

Would change score: Would rise with pragmatic trials in older multimorbid populations and hip-specific topical data.

8/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The benefit-harm framing and dose-specific estimates are directly reusable for shared decision support.

Evidence: MID probabilities and adverse-event dropout estimates support explicit trade-offs.

Limiting factor: Current prescribing still requires comorbidity, regulatory, availability, and guideline checks.

Would change score: Would rise with current guideline integration and patient-specific absolute risk models.

Main strengths

  • Large randomized evidence base and dose-specific nodes.
  • Prespecified protocol/registration and duplicate review.
  • Explicit MID probability improves clinical interpretability beyond statistical significance alone.
  • Benefit and adverse-event discontinuation are presented together.

Main limitations

  • Short treatment duration limits long-term safety inference.
  • Multimorbid/frail patients were underrepresented.
  • Attrition bias was common; allocation concealment often unclear/high risk.
  • Cause-specific GI/CV harms and formulation-specific topical effects could not be resolved.
  • Web appendices referenced by the article were not available in the retrieved source.

Practice translation and currency check

Current external verification (NICE NG226, published 19 October 2022): later NICE guidance is directionally consistent with the article’s practical interpretation: use pharmacologic treatment alongside non-pharmacologic care, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time; offer a topical NSAID for knee OA; consider oral NSAID only when topical therapy is ineffective/unsuitable with GI/renal/liver/CV risk assessment and gastroprotection; do not routinely use paracetamol or weak opioids, and do not offer strong opioids.

Best use now: comparative dose-level evidence for short-term efficacy, discussion of topical versus oral NSAID trade-offs, and evidence against routine opioid escalation.

Do not use alone for: long-term NSAID safety, individualized cardiovascular/renal/GI risk, current drug availability/regulatory status, or definitive choice in frail multimorbid patients.

External verification source: NICE. Osteoarthritis in over 16s: diagnosis and management, NG226, pharmacological management, published 19 October 2022. https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226
