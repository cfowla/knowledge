One-page synthesis

Core thesis. LLM-based rare-disease diagnostic systems show moderate average top-1 accuracy in the published benchmark literature, with higher pooled accuracy for augmented systems, but extreme between-study heterogeneity, benchmark composition effects, and pervasive leakage risk make the pooled estimate unsuitable as a prediction of real-world clinical performance.

The review identified 15 eligible studies contributing 19 system-dataset entries. Pooled Recall@1 was 43.3% (95% CI 35.1-51.6), but heterogeneity was extreme (I² 99.6%) and the 95% prediction interval spanned 11.6-78.3%. Augmented systems (agentic, retrieval-augmented, or fine-tuned) had pooled R@1 of 52.5% versus 35.4% for standalone prompting (p=0.004), while both subgroups retained I² above 98%. Benchmark family explained substantial variation: RareBench pooled R@1 was 52.0% versus 21.7% for Phenopacket Store in the meta-analysis subset.

The strongest signal is not a single model architecture but the fragility of benchmark-derived performance. All 19 entries were judged high overall risk of bias, 18/19 for possible data leakage, and no study provided independent external-group replication, prospective clinical validation, or real-world outcome evaluation. The post-hoc prevalence analysis suggested lower standalone accuracy as the proportion of ultra-rare diseases increased, but prevalence was explicitly treated as an imperfect proxy and benchmark mapping was incomplete. The manuscript is therefore most useful for evaluation design, contamination-aware benchmarking, and research prioritization, not for estimating bedside diagnostic accuracy.

Section-by-section synthesis

Abstract

The authors frame LLM rare-disease diagnosis as a fragmented evidence base and report a six-database systematic review/meta-analysis. Fifteen studies yielded 19 entries and 39,529 cases. The primary pooled R@1 was 43.3% with extreme heterogeneity and a very wide prediction interval.

Augmented systems had higher pooled R@1 than standalone systems, while benchmark composition—especially the Phenopacket Store versus RareBench contrast—was associated with performance. The abstract appropriately tempers the positive augmentation finding with leakage risk and calls for prospective, contamination-aware evaluation.

Introduction

The introduction describes the diagnostic burden of rare disease, the shift from sequencing availability toward interpretation and knowledge synthesis, and limitations of structured HPO-dependent tools. LLMs are positioned as potentially able to operate over unstructured clinical narratives and evolving biomedical knowledge.

The stated evidence gap is the lack of a cross-study synthesis of diagnostic accuracy and heterogeneity. The three objectives are pooled accuracy, sources of variability, and methodological/clinical-readiness appraisal.

Results — study selection and characteristics

From 902 records, 338 duplicates were removed, 564 records screened, 78 full texts assessed, and 15 studies included. The 19 system-dataset entries spanned GPT-4/4o, Llama-family, Claude, o1-preview, DeepSeek-V3, and MedGemma-27B backbones across RareBench, Phenopacket Store, MIMIC-IV-Rare, and institutional datasets.

Four papers contributed matched baseline-versus-augmented comparisons. Seventeen of 19 entries were from 2025 or later, emphasizing the recency—and potential instability—of the evidence base.

Results — pooled accuracy and sensitivity

The DerSimonian-Laird pooled R@1 was 43.3% (95% CI 35.1-51.6), I² 99.6%, τ² 0.134, with prediction interval 11.6-78.3%. REML and Hartung-Knapp estimates were essentially unchanged, and leave-one-out estimates ranged 41.1-44.7%.

Peer-reviewed-only analyses lowered pooled R@1 to 38.0%; excluding small studies yielded 40.0%; excluding overlapping Phenopacket Store pools raised it to 46.0%. These analyses support numerical stability of the central summary but do not solve the underlying heterogeneity or bias.

Results — benchmark variation

RareBench pooled R@1 was 52.0% versus 21.7% for Phenopacket Store (Qbetween 26.53, p<0.001). Across broader available entries, RareBench R@1 ranged 14.6-65.8% and Phenopacket Store 15.4-41.4%.

The authors mapped benchmark disease prevalence and found substantial composition differences. Phenopacket Store had 52.8% ultra-rare diseases versus 29.3% for aggregate RareBench and 1.7% for the Chinese Rare Disease List. The post-hoc standalone trend was R² 0.55 and -5.8 R@1 percentage points per 10% higher ultra-rare proportion, but prevalence is acknowledged as an imperfect proxy.

Results — augmentation, modality, genetic data

Augmented systems pooled 52.5% R@1 versus 35.4% for standalone systems (p=0.004), but both subgroups remained extremely heterogeneous. Four matched within-paper comparisons all favored augmentation, with improvements of 4.5-36 percentage points.

Generalization signals were mixed: MINT fell from 47.6% on training-seen diseases to 7.0% on unseen diseases, and RareSeek from 68.4% on institutional fine-tuning-associated data to 39.2% on RareBench. Input modality was not a significant moderator. Two studies incorporating genetic data reported higher or measurable accuracy, but their designs were too heterogeneous for pooling.

Results — risk of bias

Every entry was rated high overall risk of bias, and 18/19 had a high data-leakage concern. No study supplied independent external-group replication, prospective clinical validation, or real-world outcomes such as time to diagnosis.

This is the central constraint on interpretation: a pooled benchmark accuracy estimate can be statistically precise around its mean yet remain clinically non-transportable because the underlying evaluations may not be independent of model training, fine-tuning, or retrieval corpora.

Discussion

The authors emphasize benchmark dependence, augmentation-associated gains, and contamination risk. They interpret prevalence as one proxy for information availability, not a causal explanation, and explicitly use the MAC Medline versus RareBench contrast to show that case structure, source overlap, and input differences also matter.

The discussion is strongest when it distinguishes retrospective benchmark potential from clinical utility. It proposes disease-level holdouts, temporal separation, prospective contamination audits, and clinically meaningful outcomes for future evaluation. Claims that augmentation or genetics improve diagnosis remain associations within heterogeneous benchmark settings, not validated clinical effects.

Methods

The review followed PRISMA-DTA and was prospectively registered on OSF. Six databases were searched without language restriction; full texts and preprints were eligible. Dual-review procedures were used, with 92.0% agreement at title/abstract screening and 76.9% at full text.

The meta-analysis used strict R@1, pre-specified entry-selection rules, a modified QUADAS-3 tool, Freeman-Tukey proportions, DerSimonian-Laird random effects, prediction intervals, subgroup Q tests, Egger testing, and sensitivity models. Benchmark prevalence mapping used Orphanet, but mapping completeness varied substantially.

Availability and disclosures

The authors report NIH and Potocsnak Center support and state funders had no role in study design, analysis, publication decision, or manuscript preparation.

Meta-analysis data, benchmark mapping files, computation scripts, forest-plot code, and the manuscript build pipeline are promised for a public GitHub repository upon publication; the article-in-press text does not provide the final repository location.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. High-value synthesis for AI/clinical evaluation design; do not use the pooled 43.3% as a clinical performance expectation and do not use this review alone to justify diagnostic deployment.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses clinical AI diagnostic evaluation and rare-disease LLM systems.

Evidence basis: Large cross-study synthesis with benchmark, augmentation, contamination, and translation analyses.

Limiting factor: Does not directly evaluate pharmacy practice or deployed care.

Would raise/lower: Prospective clinical decision-support evidence would raise the score further.

Novelty
8/10

The paper claims the first systematic review/meta-analysis of LLM rare-disease diagnostic accuracy and adds benchmark-prevalence analysis.

Evidence basis: Cross-study pooling plus benchmark-composition and contamination framing.

Limiting factor: Some moderator analyses are post-hoc and the field is rapidly moving.

Would raise/lower: Independent replication of the benchmark-composition findings would raise confidence.

Method strength
7/10

Prospective registration, six-database search, dual review, risk-of-bias assessment, prediction intervals, and sensitivity models are substantial strengths.

Evidence basis: PRISMA-DTA workflow, explicit eligibility, sensitivity analyses, and modified QUADAS-3.

Limiting factor: DerSimonian-Laird does not model within-paper correlation; supplementary details are unavailable here.

Would raise/lower: Multilevel modeling plus complete supplement access and reproducible code/data would strengthen the assessment.

Evidence strength
4/10

The synthesis is internally informative but rests almost entirely on retrospective benchmark evidence at high risk of bias.

Evidence basis: All 19 entries high overall risk; 18/19 leakage concern; extreme heterogeneity; no prospective outcomes.

Limiting factor: Contamination and benchmark dependence undermine causal and clinical interpretation.

Would raise/lower: Prospective contamination-aware multicenter evaluations with patient-centered outcomes would materially raise this score.

External validity
3/10

Reported accuracy varies dramatically across benchmarks and no prospective real-world validation is available.

Evidence basis: Prediction interval 11.6-78.3%; RareBench versus Phenopacket Store contrast; generalization drops after fine-tuning.

Limiting factor: Curated benchmark distributions and overlap with training/retrieval corpora.

Would raise/lower: External clinical cohorts, temporal holdouts, and rare long-tail disease sampling would raise the score.

Implementation value
6/10

The paper provides concrete lessons for designing evaluations and deciding what not to trust, but not enough evidence to implement diagnostic use.

Evidence basis: Contamination-aware design, disease-level holdouts, benchmark-composition reporting, and clinically meaningful endpoint recommendations.

Limiting factor: No validated deployment workflow, monitoring standard, or prospective utility estimate.

Would raise/lower: Prospective workflow studies with human factors, downstream testing, safety, and governance would raise the score.

Main strengths

  • Prospectively registered diagnostic-accuracy review with broad database coverage and no language restriction.
  • Prediction intervals and multiple sensitivity analyses appropriately expose the instability hidden by a single pooled mean.
  • Risk-of-bias framework explicitly incorporates LLM-specific data leakage and reproducibility concerns.
  • Benchmark-composition analysis and within-study generalization examples make the source useful for evaluation design.

Main limitations

  • All included entries were high risk overall; 18/19 had a data-leakage concern.
  • I² remained above 95% in major subgroups, so pooled and subgroup means are descriptive.
  • Some evaluations used constrained candidate lists, and four meta-analysis values were approximate figure extractions.
  • Prevalence mapping was incomplete (49-91% mapping) and the prevalence-accuracy relationship was post-hoc.
  • The primary random-effects model did not account for within-paper correlation.
  • No independent replication, prospective clinical validation, or patient-centered outcome evidence was found.
  • The source supplement was not provided for this task, limiting verification of detailed search, prompt, bias, and synonym-handling procedures.

Confidence in appraisal: High for the main-text synthesis and limitations; moderate for supplement-dependent methodological details because the supplementary material was unavailable.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use nowUse the paper to design or audit LLM diagnostic benchmarks: report disease composition, enforce temporal/disease-level holdouts, test contamination, separate benchmark accuracy from clinical utility, and report prediction intervals/heterogeneity.
Do not use nowDo not interpret 43.3% as expected bedside accuracy, assume augmentation causes better real-world diagnosis, or infer that public-benchmark success validates a clinical decision-support system.
Build/evaluatePrefer prospective or temporally isolated cases, independent external groups, open-ended diagnostic generation, long-tail/ultra-rare representation, explicit corpus-access auditing, and clinically meaningful endpoints such as downstream testing and time to diagnosis.
Watch nextProspective multicenter trials; contamination-resistant benchmark releases after model training cutoffs; external validation of retrieval/agentic systems; human-AI workflow studies; performance stratified by disease rarity and data availability.
Bottom line. The source is more actionable as a warning about evaluation validity than as evidence of clinical diagnostic readiness. Its most reusable contribution is the combination of extreme heterogeneity, benchmark-composition effects, and explicit leakage pathways.
