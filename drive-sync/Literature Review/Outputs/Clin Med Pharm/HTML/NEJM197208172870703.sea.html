Scope warning: This is a 1972 single-study evaluation of continuous IV heparin monitoring. It is useful for understanding the evidentiary origin of the APTT therapeutic-range concept, but it does not by itself validate a current dosing protocol or contemporary laboratory range.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: recurrent venous thromboembolism during IV heparin therapy clustered in periods of relatively low APTT, whereas bleeding was not clearly related to higher heparin dose or APTT in this cohort. The authors therefore recommended maintaining APTT at 1.5-2.5 times control.

The investigators prospectively followed 234 patients receiving continuous IV heparin. Among 162 treated for venous thromboembolism, five developed recurrent thromboembolism. Before recurrence, their mean APTT was 49 seconds versus 66 seconds in 157 patients without recurrence (p<0.005), despite similar mean heparin doses. APTT was below 50 seconds on 71% of observed treatment days before recurrence versus 28% of treatment days among patients without recurrence (p<0.0001). The authors emphasized persistence of low APTT: reported recurrence risk was 7% after two consecutive days below 50 seconds and 21% after three consecutive days below 50 seconds.

Bleeding occurred in 19 of 234 patients (8%). Postoperative bleeding was more frequent than bleeding among medical patients (13.4% vs 6% as separately reported), and postoperative bleeders were older. Within postoperative and medical subgroups, however, mean heparin dose and mean APTT were similar in patients with and without bleeding. The study's strongest contribution is prospective, temporally anchored observation of APTT before recurrence; its major constraints are only five recurrent events, imperfect clinical diagnosis of venous thrombosis, variable adherence to the dose-adjustment recommendation, and the nonrandomized design.

Section condensation

Abstract / Introduction

The paper frames a practical uncertainty: heparin was already used for venous thromboembolism, but the clinical value of adjusting dose to a defined coagulation-test range had not been tested prospectively. The authors studied recurrence and bleeding while measuring APTT and assessing complications independently of knowledge of the APTT or heparin dose.

The intended therapeutic range was APTT 1.5-2.5 times control. The central hypothesis was that complications, especially recurrent thrombosis, would be less frequent when coagulation test values were maintained within that range.

Patients and Methods

Two hundred thirty-four patients receiving continuous IV heparin were followed: 162 for venous thromboembolic disease and 72 for other indications. The VTE cohort included pulmonary embolism (74), venous thrombosis (52), or both (36). Initial treatment was a 5,000-U IV bolus followed by 24,000 U/24 h by continuous infusion. APTT was measured one to three times daily; physicians were advised to adjust heparin to 1.5-2.5 times a 40-second control, but adherence to this recommendation varied.

Recurrent venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism were defined clinically with radiographic or scan support where applicable. Bleeding was assessed daily and classified as major when transfusion was required and minor when treatment was not needed. APTT used citrated platelet-poor plasma, kaolin, a three-minute incubation, recalcification, and phospholipid; pooled normal plasma measured 39-41 seconds.

Results — heparin exposure and recurrence

In the VTE cohort, mean heparin dose was 30,200 U/24 h, mean treatment duration 8.6 days, and mean APTT 66 seconds. Five patients had recurrent venous thromboembolism. Before recurrence they received a mean 34,100 U/24 h, not significantly different from the 29,900 U/24 h in patients without recurrence, but mean APTT was substantially lower (49 vs 66 seconds; p<0.005). After recurrence, mean heparin dose rose to 47,300 U/24 h (p<0.01 vs no recurrence) and mean APTT rose to 67 seconds.

Low APTT was temporally concentrated before recurrence. APTT was <50 seconds on 20/28 treatment days (71%) before recurrence versus 378/1,337 days (28%) without recurrence (p<0.0001). All recurrence patients with sufficient prior observations had at least two consecutive days below 50 seconds, and 4/4 evaluable recurrence patients had three consecutive days below 50 seconds. The paper reports recurrence risks of 5/72 (7%) after two consecutive low-APTT days and 4/19 (21%) after three. A 60-second threshold did not discriminate recurrence groups.

Results — bleeding

Nineteen of 234 patients bled (8%). Nine of 67 postoperative patients bled (13.4%); six required transfusion. Postoperative bleeders were older (mean 69 vs 54 years, p<0.01), but mean heparin dose (30,100 vs 31,600 U/24 h) and APTT (64 vs 65 seconds) did not differ significantly by bleeding status.

Ten of 167 medical patients bled (6%); seven episodes were spontaneous (4%), including hematuria, abdominal-wall hematoma, and hip hemorrhage, and three followed avoidable trauma. In medical patients, age, heparin dose, and APTT did not differ significantly between bleeding and nonbleeding groups. No patient died from bleeding or pulmonary embolism during heparin treatment.

Discussion / Conclusion

The authors interpreted the recurrence pattern as evidence that patients with venous thromboembolism should receive enough heparin to keep APTT between 1.5 and 2.5 times control at all times. They regarded recurrence as rare when APTT was maintained at or above 1.5 times control and emphasized that recurrence occurred despite similar heparin dose when APTT response was lower.

They also acknowledged important uncertainty. Clinical diagnosis of venous thrombosis was imprecise and could misclassify events; silent emboli might have been missed. Most importantly, the study did not prove that the five recurrences would have been prevented by maintaining APTT in range. Bleeding remained possible even within the recommended APTT range, particularly after surgery.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: understand the historical clinical evidence supporting APTT-guided unfractionated heparin monitoring and the origin of the 1.5-2.5-times-control target. Do not use this paper alone to define a current laboratory-specific therapeutic range or modern dosing protocol.

Evidence basis: Prospective heparin-treated cohort with explicit APTT exposure and clinical outcomes.

Novelty

Rationale: The study prospectively links serial APTT measurements to recurrence and uses temporal exposure immediately before events.

Evidence basis: Serial daily APTT, event timing, and comparison with nonrecurrence patients.

Principal limiting factor: The underlying therapeutic-range concept and heparin use predated the study.

What would change the score: A randomized target comparison or more rigorous mechanistic validation would raise it.

Method strength

Rationale: Prospective follow-up and outcome assessment without knowledge of APTT/dose are meaningful strengths, but treatment was not randomized and protocol adherence varied.

Evidence basis: Prospective design; stated blinded assessment of recurrence/bleeding; explicit laboratory method.

Principal limiting factor: Nonrandomized exposure, variable dose-adjustment adherence, and partly clinical VTE diagnosis.

What would change the score: Controlled target assignment, standardized imaging, and prespecified analytic methods would raise it.

5/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: The low-APTT association before recurrence is large and temporally coherent, but it rests on only five recurrence events and cannot establish preventability.

Evidence basis: 49 vs 66 sec mean APTT before recurrence; 71% vs 28% treatment days below 50 sec; p values reported.

Principal limiting factor: Only five recurrent events and no causal intervention test.

What would change the score: More events, confidence intervals, and randomized maintenance of different APTT targets would raise it.

5/10

External validity

Rationale: The cohort includes multiple inpatient heparin indications, but the evidence comes from one historical setting using 1972 diagnostic and APTT methods.

Evidence basis: 234 heparin-treated patients with 162 VTE cases and postoperative/medical subgroups.

Principal limiting factor: Single-era laboratory method, clinical diagnostic limitations, and no external replication in the source.

What would change the score: Replication across centers and standardized objective VTE ascertainment would raise it.

7/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper provides a clear monitoring target and concrete recurrence/bleeding observations that explain why APTT-guided adjustment became clinically compelling.

Evidence basis: Explicit 1.5-2.5-times-control recommendation and threshold analyses around APTT <50 sec.

Principal limiting factor: The study does not establish a contemporary laboratory calibration or dosing nomogram.

What would change the score: Modern validation of the target against standardized assays and clinical outcomes would raise it.

Main strengths

  • Prospective serial monitoring with APTT measured during the period preceding recurrent events.
  • Outcome assessment was stated to be performed without knowledge of APTT or heparin dose.
  • Reports dose, APTT, event timing, bleeding, subgroup details, and threshold analyses rather than only an overall association.

Main limitations

  • Only five recurrent venous thromboembolic events.
  • No random allocation to APTT targets; the study supports association more strongly than causation.
  • Heparin-adjustment recommendations were followed variably.
  • Clinical diagnosis of venous thrombosis was acknowledged to be imprecise; silent emboli could be missed.
  • Table 2 contains an internal numerical inconsistency in one row.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Preserve

The most reusable evidence is the temporal association between low APTT and recurrence, especially the repeated days below 50 seconds before events.

Do not overclaim

The study did not prove that maintaining APTT in range would have prevented the five recurrences.

Safety signal

Bleeding occurred despite APTT values that were not higher than those of nonbleeding patients; postoperative bleeding was prominent and associated with older age.

Literature-review role

Use as a foundational historical source, then pair with later validation studies that define therapeutic ranges, nomograms, and outcome-linked monitoring strategies.
