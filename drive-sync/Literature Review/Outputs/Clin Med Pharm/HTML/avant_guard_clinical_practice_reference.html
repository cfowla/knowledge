Clinical question and PICO

Clinical question: In adults with symptomatic, previously untreated persistent atrial fibrillation who are candidates for rhythm control, should first-line pulsed field ablation using a pentaspline FARAWAVE/FARAPULSE catheter strategy be preferred over initial class I or III antiarrhythmic-drug therapy?

One-page synthesis

Core thesis

In selected patients with symptomatic, previously untreated persistent AF, initial PFA reduced composite rhythm-treatment failure compared with initial antiarrhythmic-drug therapy over 12 months. Treatment success was 56% vs 30%, absolute difference 26 percentage points, HR for composite treatment failure 0.46 (95% CI 0.33-0.65; P<0.001).

Most practice-relevant caution

The trial had a clinically important neurologic safety signal: six procedure-related strokes occurred before a trial pause; after workflow changes, no stroke/TIA was observed in the post-pause PFA cohort, but only 74 post-pause PFA-treated patients contributed to that comparison.

Approximate NNT

~4

Based on the 26 percentage-point absolute difference in composite treatment success. Interpret cautiously because the primary endpoint was Kaplan-Meier estimated and composite.

Primary safety event

5.1%

13 of 257 in the combined PFA safety population; met prespecified performance goal upper confidence limit below 12%.

Hard outcomes

Not established

No basis to change anticoagulation eligibility, stroke-prevention strategy, mortality expectations, or admission/discharge criteria.

Explicit insufficient-evidence warning: This single 12-month trial is insufficient for automatic first-line PFA adoption across all persistent AF patients, for stopping oral anticoagulation after ablation, for choosing PFA in patients with CHA2DS2-VASc score 4 or higher, for comparing all PFA systems against all AAD strategies, or for replacing local EP credentialing and procedural safety pathways.

Practice verdict

Verdict: Read first; conditional practice influence.

Best use now: Support earlier EP referral and shared decision-making for carefully selected symptomatic persistent AF patients who desire rhythm control, especially when local PFA expertise, uninterrupted anticoagulation, left atrial thrombus screening, high-ACT procedural workflow, and neurologic-event surveillance are reliable.

Do not use for: automatic formulary/protocol displacement of AADs, anticoagulation de-escalation, stroke-risk modification, unselected high-risk persistent AF, long-standing persistent AF, or non-FARAPULSE PFA assumptions.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The authors tested whether PFA could be used as an initial rhythm-control strategy for persistent AF, where guidelines have historically often placed AADs before catheter ablation. Patients with previously untreated persistent AF were randomized 2:1 to PFA or AAD therapy; an additional PFA-assigned group contributed only to the safety endpoint.

The trial found higher 12-month treatment success with PFA than AAD therapy and a lower hazard of composite treatment failure. Primary safety events occurred in 5.1% of the combined PFA safety group, and serious adverse events at 12 months were 25% vs 21% in PFA vs AAD randomized as-treated groups.

Introduction / rationale

Persistent AF is clinically important because it is associated with greater adverse cardiovascular risk and health care use than paroxysmal AF. Prior evidence supports ablation after AAD failure and first-line ablation in selected paroxysmal AF, but persistent AF has more atrial remodeling and worse procedural outcomes with historical thermal approaches.

The trial addresses the specific uncertainty of first-line PFA for persistent AF, not ablation in general and not PFA after failed AADs. This makes the article directly relevant to early rhythm-control pathway decisions.

Methods

AVANT-GUARD was an international, multicenter, randomized trial sponsored by Boston Scientific. Randomized patients were assigned 2:1 to PFA or AAD therapy, and all received an insertable cardiac monitor. PFA used pulmonary-vein isolation plus posterior-wall ablation with pentaspline FARAWAVE catheter; AAD therapy followed local guideline-directed practice and excluded amiodarone as first-line therapy.

The primary effectiveness endpoint combined short-term procedural/no-ablation success and long-term freedom from arrhythmia recurrence, repeat ablation, cardioversion-related failures, class I/III AAD need in PFA patients, and amiodarone use. A 90-day blanking period was used. A trial pause after six neurologic events led to added thrombus screening, uninterrupted anticoagulation, ACT threshold, and exclusion of CHA2DS2-VASc score 4 or higher.

Results

A total of 310 patients were randomized: 207 to PFA and 103 to AAD. Another 100 were assigned to PFA for safety evaluation. The primary effectiveness endpoint favored PFA: 56% vs 30% 12-month treatment success, with HR 0.46 for composite treatment failure.

The result was mainly driven by lower asymptomatic atrial arrhythmia recurrence and lower arrhythmia burden on continuous monitoring. Symptomatic recurrence was uncommon and similar. PFA procedures averaged 85 minutes, with left atrial dwell time 51 minutes, and pulmonary-vein isolation was confirmed in all treated PFA patients.

Discussion and limitations

The authors conclude that first-line PFA reduced atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence compared with AAD therapy in untreated persistent AF. They emphasize that persistent AF should not be assumed to behave like paroxysmal AF and that the observed population is closer to clinical practice than lower-risk ablation cohorts.

Limitations include 12-month follow-up, single ablation system and lesion set, no baseline pre-treatment burden measurement from the inserted monitor, AAD-group crossover to ablation after failure, performance-goal rather than randomized safety testing, and inability to determine whether improved safety after pause was due to patient selection or procedure changes.

Clinical translation and implementation constraints

Patient selection

Best candidates mirror the trial: symptomatic persistent AF lasting 7-365 days, early rhythm-control decision point, no meaningful prior class I/III AAD exposure, acceptable anticoagulation status, no left atrial thrombus, and thromboembolic risk similar to represented patients.

Required infrastructure

EP operators trained on the specific PFA system, procedural imaging or TEE access, anticoagulation management, ACT monitoring, neurologic-event recognition, vascular-access complication readiness, and longitudinal rhythm monitoring.

Shared decision-making

Discuss expected rhythm benefit, early procedural risk, stroke signal, possibility of recurrence, need for ongoing anticoagulation based on risk, and lack of long-term hard-outcome evidence.

Decision guardrail

Consider first-line PFA discussion only if all are true:
1. Patient has symptomatic persistent AF, duration 7-365 days.
2. Rhythm-control strategy is desired after guideline-informed discussion.
3. No prior substantial class I/III AAD exposure or prior AF ablation.
4. Patient can maintain uninterrupted anticoagulation and undergo left atrial thrombus screening.
5. Local EP team can reproduce the post-pause safety workflow: thrombus imaging within 24 h, at least 4 weeks uninterrupted anticoagulation, ACT at least 350 sec before ablation, air-embolism prevention, and post-procedure neurologic surveillance.
6. Patient understands that evidence supports lower 12-month arrhythmia recurrence, not lower stroke, mortality, or medication-free status.

What should or should not change in practice

Reasonable changes now

  • Add AVANT-GUARD to the evidence base for early EP referral in selected symptomatic persistent AF.
  • Use the trial in shared decision-making when patients prefer procedural rhythm control over chronic AAD exposure.
  • Make continuous monitoring and asymptomatic AF burden part of the discussion; symptoms alone under-detect recurrence.
  • If adopting or expanding PFA, build the post-pause safety workflow into local procedure checklists.
  • Document that the expected benefit is rhythm-control success and burden reduction at 12 months.

Do not change without further evidence

  • Do not stop anticoagulation because PFA was performed or because monitoring appears favorable.
  • Do not make first-line PFA an automatic default for all persistent AF.
  • Do not extrapolate to CHA2DS2-VASc score 4 or higher, long-standing persistent AF, prior failed AADs, prior ablation, or asymptomatic AF.
  • Do not treat these data as proof that all PFA systems or thermal ablation strategies have the same benefit-risk profile.
  • Do not revise AAD dosing, renal adjustment, hepatic adjustment, or interaction screening based on this trial.
