One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. The paper is useful for recognizing a possible class-level psoriasis signal across common antihypertensives, but the evidence is not strong enough to support routine antihypertensive substitution solely on this basis.

The review pooled 13 observational studies encompassing 6.38 million people. Pairwise meta-analysis found higher psoriasis incidence with ACE inhibitors (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.31-2.13), beta-blockers (1.40, 1.20-1.63), calcium-channel blockers (1.53, 1.23-1.89), and diuretics (1.70, 1.40-2.06). These signals persisted in leave-one-out analyses. In hypertensive-only subgroup analyses, ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, and diuretics remained statistically significant, while the CCB estimate did not (OR 1.27, 95% CI 0.97-1.68).

Bayesian network meta-analysis of 8 studies/5.62 million people similarly favored higher psoriasis risk versus nonuse for ACE inhibitors (OR 2.09, 1.39-3.18), CCBs (1.53, 1.07-2.24), and thiazide diuretics (1.80, 1.23-2.66); the beta-blocker estimate was not statistically significant (1.35, 0.99-1.91). The main credibility problem is extreme pairwise heterogeneity (I² 96-98%) combined with observational confounding. The authors also could not adjust for drug latency, dose, or combination therapy. A further source-level issue is the network ranking sentence: it prints beta-blockers at 59.9% while ordering them after diuretics (45.8%) and CCBs (44.2%), suggesting the text may conflate rank probabilities with SUCRA.

Section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame psoriasis as a chronic inflammatory disease influenced by genetic and external factors, including drug exposure. Antihypertensive classes are highlighted because prior reports suggested cutaneous adverse effects and possible psoriasis associations, but findings across studies were inconsistent.

The stated question is class-level: whether ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, CCBs, and diuretics are associated with incident psoriasis. ARBs were initially in the search scope but later lacked enough studies for meta-analysis.

Methods

Two reviewers independently searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase on 8 January 2021 with no publication-date restriction, screened titles/abstracts then full texts, and resolved disagreement by discussion. Eligible designs included RCTs, case-control, and cohort studies with diagnosed psoriasis and enough information to derive OR/HR estimates; non-original publications, case reports/series, inadequate data, uncertain exposure timing, and duplicate populations were excluded. Study quality was assessed independently using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.

Pairwise meta-analysis used ORs/95% CIs, I² and Q testing, fixed effects at I² ≤50% and DerSimonian-Laird random effects otherwise, plus leave-one-out sensitivity and publication-bias assessments. Bayesian network meta-analysis used Gibbs-sampling MCMC with 50,000 burn-in and 100,000 estimation iterations and ranking probabilities. The paper states PRISMA adherence. The inspected article/supplement does not report protocol registration, GRADE certainty, or explicit network transitivity/inconsistency/convergence diagnostics.

Results

From 3,025 records, 549 duplicates were removed; 2,476 records were screened; 159 full texts were reviewed; 146 were excluded; 13 studies/6,378,116 individuals were pooled. The evidence base comprised 5 cohort and 8 case-control studies from Europe, the USA, and Asia; NOS scores were 6-9. ARBs were excluded from pooling because only three studies were available.

Pairwise pooled ORs were 1.67 for ACE inhibitors, 1.40 for beta-blockers, 1.53 for CCBs, and 1.70 for diuretics. However, heterogeneity was extreme (I² 96-98%). Publication-bias tests were non-significant, and leave-one-out results stayed statistically significant. Hypertensive-only subgroup estimates were 1.44, 1.46, 1.27, and 1.47 respectively, with the CCB CI crossing 1.0. Network analysis found ORs versus nonuse of 2.09 (ACE inhibitors), 1.35 (beta-blockers), 1.53 (CCBs), and 1.80 (thiazide diuretics); the beta-blocker CI crossed 1.0.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors discuss plausible class-specific mechanisms: bradykinin/arachidonic acid and inflammatory mediators for ACE inhibitors; beta-adrenergic/cAMP effects for beta-blockers; calcium-regulated keratinocyte differentiation for CCBs; and photosensitivity/hyperuricaemia hypotheses for thiazides. These are mechanistic rationales rather than proof that the pooled epidemiologic associations are causal.

The authors acknowledge inability to adjust for latency, dose, and combination therapy; considerable heterogeneity; and small subgroup study counts. They conclude that antihypertensive users should be monitored for possible psoriasis/cutaneous adverse events, while explicitly noting beta-blockers were not significant in the network meta-analysis and further studies are needed. No funding and no conflicts of interest were reported.

Evaluation & appraisal

Relevance 8.5/10

Rationale: Directly relevant to medication adverse-effect assessment and antihypertensive pharmacotherapy.

Evidence basis: Large synthesis across four common classes and psoriasis incidence.

Limiting factor: Association does not establish causality or a specific prescribing action.

Would change score: Higher if replicated with exposure timing/dose and clinically actionable absolute risks.

Novelty 7.0/10

Rationale: At publication, the authors positioned this as the first combined pairwise and network meta-analysis of these antihypertensive classes and psoriasis.

Evidence basis: Network comparison and rank-probability analysis add comparative framing.

Limiting factor: The underlying evidence is observational and several mechanistic claims are background hypotheses.

Would change score: Higher with richer network diagnostics and class/drug-specific dose-response analyses.

Method strength 6.0/10

Rationale: The review used dual searching, explicit eligibility criteria, NOS appraisal, sensitivity analyses, and pairwise plus Bayesian network methods.

Evidence basis: Three databases, PRISMA statement, defined heterogeneity model choice, leave-one-out analyses, 50k burn-in plus 100k MCMC estimation iterations.

Limiting factor: No protocol/registration was reported; NOS is limited; no GRADE; explicit network transitivity, inconsistency, and convergence diagnostics were not reported in the inspected materials.

Would change score: Higher with prespecified registration, contemporary risk-of-bias methods, certainty grading, and full network diagnostics.

Evidence strength 5.5/10

Rationale: Pooled associations are consistent in direction, but causal confidence is constrained by observational designs and extreme heterogeneity.

Evidence basis: 13 studies/6.38 million participants; pooled ORs 1.40-1.70; sensitivity analyses remained significant.

Limiting factor: I² 96-98%; residual confounding; drug exposure timing/dose/combination could not be adjusted; network BB estimate crossed 1.0.

Would change score: Higher with low-heterogeneity prospective cohorts or designs better addressing confounding and temporality.

External validity 6.5/10

Rationale: The evidence spans Europe, the USA, and Asia with very large aggregate populations.

Evidence basis: 13 studies across multiple regions and settings.

Limiting factor: Definitions of psoriasis, populations, exposure ascertainment, and comorbid burden varied; class-level pooling masks drug-specific differences.

Would change score: Higher with harmonized definitions and contemporary multi-country analyses with absolute risk estimates.

Implementation value 5.5/10

Rationale: The findings support vigilance for psoriasis in antihypertensive users but do not justify routine switching of effective therapy on their own.

Evidence basis: Class-level relative-risk signals plus authors' monitoring recommendation.

Limiting factor: No absolute risk, dose-response, latency, or treatment-switch benefit; causality is unresolved.

Would change score: Higher with absolute incidence, attributable risk, dechallenge/rechallenge, and comparative treatment-outcome data.

Main strengths

  • Very large aggregate sample and multi-region observational evidence base.
  • Explicit study-selection criteria, dual searching, NOS appraisal, sensitivity analysis, and publication-bias checks.
  • Pairwise and network estimates allow both exposure-vs-nonuse and comparative framing.
  • Supplement provides search strategy, quality scoring, bias tests, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup plots.

Main limitations

  • All included evidence was observational despite RCT eligibility; causal confounding remains substantial.
  • Extreme heterogeneity (I² 96-98%) weakens confidence in single pooled class effects.
  • No adjustment was possible for key exposure dimensions including latency, dose, and combination therapy.
  • Class-level pooling may obscure within-class drug differences and indication/comorbidity effects.
  • No reported protocol registration or certainty-of-evidence framework in the inspected material.
  • Network meta-analysis reporting lacks explicit transitivity/inconsistency/convergence diagnostics in the article/supplement inspected here.
  • Figure 3/text contains a ranking-probability inconsistency, and broad “all classes” conclusion language overstates the non-significant beta-blocker network estimate.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Consider antihypertensive exposure in medication review when new or worsening psoriasis occurs, particularly when temporality is plausible.
Do not overclaim
Do not interpret these pooled ORs as proof of causality or as a mandate to avoid entire antihypertensive classes.
Missing for prescribing decisions
Absolute risk, dose-response, latency, individual-drug estimates, dechallenge/rechallenge evidence, and outcomes after switching.
Best next evidence
Contemporary pharmacoepidemiologic studies with active comparators, time-varying exposure, confounding control, and absolute-risk reporting.

Clinical bottom line: The paper supports a signal worth recognizing and monitoring, especially for ACE inhibitors, CCBs, and diuretics, but the high heterogeneity and residual confounding make it unsuitable as standalone evidence for changing a well-indicated antihypertensive regimen.
