One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In euvolemic critically ill adults with hemoglobin <=9 g/dL, a restrictive red-cell transfusion strategy (threshold <7 g/dL; target 7-9 g/dL) produced a large reduction in red-cell exposure and did not show worse 30-day mortality than a liberal strategy (threshold <10 g/dL; target 10-12 g/dL). Several secondary outcomes favored restriction, but the formal equivalence claim is weakened by early termination and imprecision.

Key result. Thirty-day mortality was 18.7% (78/418) with restriction versus 23.3% (98/420) with liberal transfusion; the absolute difference was 4.7 percentage points in favor of restriction, but the 95% confidence interval ranged from -0.84 to 10.2 and P=0.11. Hospital mortality was 22.2% versus 28.1% (P=0.05), while 60-day and ICU mortality differences were not statistically significant. Restriction reduced mean red-cell use from 5.6 to 2.6 units per patient (reported 54% reduction), and 33% of restrictively managed patients received no red cells after randomization versus 0% in the liberal group.

Clinical signal and caution. Cardiac complications were lower with restriction (13.2% vs 21.0%; P<0.01), including myocardial infarction (0.7% vs 2.9%; P=0.02) and pulmonary edema (5.3% vs 10.7%; P<0.01). Favorable mortality signals in patients age <55 years and APACHE II <=20 were subgroup findings without multiplicity adjustment; the APACHE II cutoff was changed from 15 to 20 in the final analysis. Cardiac-disease patients had no significant mortality difference, and selection around severe cardiac disease constrains extrapolation to active ischemia.

Most important validity issue: The study was planned as an equivalence trial, recalculated to need 1620 patients, but stopped at 838 because enrollment declined. The observed 30-day mortality confidence interval does not tightly exclude clinically meaningful harm in either direction; therefore, “formal equivalence demonstrated” is stronger than the precision supports.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the trial as a comparison of restrictive and liberal red-cell transfusion strategies in critically ill patients, using 30-day all-cause mortality and organ dysfunction as principal efficacy measures. It reports 838 randomized patients: 418 restrictive and 420 liberal.

The abstract reports similar overall 30-day mortality (18.7% vs 23.3%; P=0.11), lower hospital mortality with restriction (22.2% vs 28.1%; P=0.05), and favorable subgroup results in lower-APACHE-II and younger patients. Its conclusion states that restriction is at least as effective and possibly superior, except potentially in acute myocardial infarction and unstable angina.

Introduction / background

The rationale is the competing concern that anemia may impair oxygen delivery while transfusion may introduce immunologic and microcirculatory harms. Routine practice often used hemoglobin targets above 10 g/dL, but the optimal threshold in critically ill patients was uncertain.

The trial therefore tested whether maintaining hemoglobin at 7-9 g/dL with transfusion below 7 g/dL could achieve outcomes comparable with a liberal 10-12 g/dL target and transfusion below 10 g/dL in patients judged euvolemic after initial treatment.

Methods

Patients were recruited from 25 Canadian ICUs between November 1994 and November 1997. Eligibility required expected ICU stay >24 h, hemoglobin <=9 g/dL within 72 h of ICU admission, and euvolemia after initial treatment. Important exclusions included active bleeding, chronic anemia, pregnancy, imminent death, inability to receive blood, and routine cardiac surgery. Allocation used center/severity stratification, permuted blocks, and sequential sealed opaque envelopes; clinician blinding was not feasible.

Restriction used a 7 g/dL trigger and 7-9 g/dL target; liberal transfusion used a 10 g/dL trigger and 10-12 g/dL target. The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Analyses were intention-to-treat. The trial was designed for equivalence, with sample size recalculated to 1620, but it stopped at 838 because enrollment declined. Subgroup analyses included age, cardiac disease, and illness severity; no multiple-comparison adjustment was used, and the APACHE II cutoff was changed from 15 to 20 in the final analysis.

Results — study population and treatment separation

Of 6451 assessed patients, 3206 were eligible, 2039 were screened for consent, and 838 consented (41%) and were randomized. Baseline characteristics were similar between treatment groups. The correction changes the enrollment comparison: 20% of enrolled patients had cardiac disease versus 26% of those not enrolled (P<0.01), not the reverse as printed in the article.

Treatment separation was substantial: mean daily hemoglobin was 8.5+/-0.7 versus 10.7+/-0.7 g/dL (P<0.01), and mean red-cell use was 2.6+/-4.1 versus 5.6+/-5.3 units per patient (P<0.01), a reported 54% reduction. One-third of restrictive-strategy patients received no post-randomization red cells versus none in the liberal group. The correction also changes cointervention wording to P>0.15 and P>0.58, supporting similarity without claiming exact equality.

Results — outcomes and subgroups

The primary 30-day mortality outcome was 18.7% versus 23.3% (absolute difference 4.7 points; 95% CI -0.84 to 10.2; P=0.11). Hospital mortality was 22.2% versus 28.1% (P=0.05), while 60-day mortality (22.7% vs 26.5%; P=0.23) and ICU mortality (13.4% vs 16.2%; P=0.29) were not significantly different. Death-adjusted organ-dysfunction analyses favored restriction, whereas unadjusted organ dysfunction and crude >3-organ failure did not differ significantly.

Cardiac complications were lower under restriction (13.2% vs 21.0%; P<0.01). Thirty-day mortality favored restriction in the APACHE II <=20 subgroup (8.7% vs 16.1%; P=0.03) and age <55 subgroup (5.7% vs 13.0%; P=0.02), but not in cardiac disease (20.5% vs 22.9%; P=0.69), severe infection/septic shock (22.8% vs 29.7%; P=0.36), or trauma (10.0% vs 8.8%; P=0.81).

Discussion / conclusion

The authors interpret the results as showing that a 7 g/dL transfusion threshold with a 7-9 g/dL target is at least as effective as and possibly superior to the liberal approach. They emphasize the marked reduction in red-cell exposure and the direction of several mortality, cardiac-complication, and organ-dysfunction outcomes.

They recommend restriction for most critically ill patients while cautioning about acute myocardial infarction and unstable angina. They also discuss enrollment/refusal patterns as a generalizability concern, particularly for severe cardiac disease. Funding came from the Medical Research Council of Canada and an unrestricted grant from Bayer.

Restrictive: if hemoglobin <7 g/dL -> transfuse 1 unit -> remeasure hemoglobin -> maintain 7-9 g/dL.
Liberal: if hemoglobin <10 g/dL -> transfuse -> maintain 10-12 g/dL.
Protocol applied during ICU stay; other clinical management remained at physician discretion.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a landmark randomized trial with high conceptual and implementation relevance, but the strength of the formal equivalence claim is limited by early termination, imprecision, subgroup multiplicity, and selection around cardiac disease.
Relevance10/10

Directly addresses a core ICU transfusion-threshold decision with mortality, organ dysfunction, complications, and blood utilization.

Evidence basis: Randomized comparison of 7 vs 10 g/dL transfusion triggers in 838 critically ill patients.

Principal limiting factor: Source is from 1994-1997 practice and current guideline alignment was not verified.

Would raise/lower: Would remain high unless the use case excludes adult critical-care transfusion decisions.

Novelty9/10

At publication, the trial directly tested a substantially lower transfusion threshold in a broad ICU population and challenged a common liberal practice pattern.

Evidence basis: Large multicenter threshold trial with marked treatment separation and clinically important outcomes.

Principal limiting factor: Novelty is historical rather than current.

Would raise/lower: Would be lower if judged only against contemporary evidence rather than its 1999 contribution.

Method strength8/10

Randomization, allocation procedures, intention-to-treat analysis, strong treatment separation, and multicenter enrollment support internal validity.

Evidence basis: 418 vs 420 randomized; clear thresholds; baseline balance; objective primary outcome.

Principal limiting factor: Open-label care, early termination at roughly half the recalculated target, changed APACHE subgroup cutoff, and no multiplicity adjustment.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with completion to target sample, prespecified fixed subgroup thresholds, multiplicity plan, and blinded outcome processes where feasible.

Evidence strength7/10

The direction of mortality and several secondary outcomes favors restriction, but the primary 30-day mortality comparison was imprecise and not statistically significant.

Evidence basis: 30-day mortality difference 4.7 points, 95% CI -0.84 to 10.2; P=0.11; adjusted OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.50-1.07.

Principal limiting factor: Formal equivalence was not tightly established after early stopping; several favorable findings are secondary/subgroup results.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with a fully powered equivalence/noninferiority design and confirmatory replication focused on patient-centered outcomes.

External validity6/10

The sample spanned 25 Canadian ICUs and multiple diagnoses, but consent/refusal patterns and cardiac-disease selection constrain generalization.

Evidence basis: Broad ICU enrollment; 41% consent rate; corrected comparison shows cardiac disease in 20% enrolled vs 26% not enrolled.

Principal limiting factor: Severe cardiac disease was disproportionately declined by physicians; routine cardiac surgery excluded; historical non-leukodepleted blood products.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with broader enrollment, contemporary blood-processing context, and dedicated active-coronary-ischemia evidence.

Implementation value9/10

The intervention is simple, explicit, auditable, and produced a 54% reduction in mean red-cell use.

Evidence basis: Threshold/target protocol is operationally clear and adherence was high with low crossover.

Principal limiting factor: Current-practice adoption requires contemporary guideline and local-policy verification, which is outside this source-only appraisal.

Would raise/lower: Would rise to 10 with current guidance reconciliation and explicit exceptions/monitoring criteria.

Validity analysis

Strengths: randomized multicenter design; concealed sequential allocation via opaque envelopes; intention-to-treat analysis; objective primary endpoint; large separation in achieved hemoglobin and transfusion exposure; low crossover; extensive outcome and complication reporting.
Primary limitations: early termination for slow enrollment; open-label treatment; the primary endpoint did not meet conventional significance and the equivalence objective remained imprecise; multiple subgroup analyses without multiplicity correction; APACHE II subgroup cutoff changed in final analysis; consent/refusal selection, especially around cardiac disease; red cells were stored without leukodepletion in the study era.

PICO

PCritically ill ICU patients, age >=16, expected ICU stay >24 h, hemoglobin <=9 g/dL within 72 h, euvolemic after initial treatment; important exclusions included active bleeding, chronic anemia, imminent death, routine cardiac surgery.
IRestrictive red-cell strategy: transfuse below 7 g/dL; target 7-9 g/dL.
CLiberal red-cell strategy: transfuse below 10 g/dL; target 10-12 g/dL.
OPrimary: 30-day all-cause mortality. Secondary: 60-day/ICU/hospital mortality, organ dysfunction/failure, complications, survival, length of stay, transfusion exposure.

Claim vs appraisal

Source claim: restriction was at least as effective as and possibly superior to liberal transfusion for most critically ill patients, except possibly acute myocardial infarction/unstable angina.

Appraisal inference: the trial supports a strong case that routine liberal transfusion above 7 g/dL was not clearly beneficial in this population and substantially increased red-cell exposure. However, because the equivalence trial stopped early and the 30-day mortality interval remained wide, the data are better characterized as showing no demonstrated mortality advantage to the liberal strategy than as proving tight statistical equivalence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What this source supports

A restrictive ICU transfusion protocol can substantially reduce red-cell exposure without a demonstrated overall 30-day mortality penalty in the studied population.

What not to overclaim

Do not treat the trial as definitive proof of formal equivalence, as evidence for active coronary ischemia, or as a substitute for current guideline/local-policy review.

Operationally reusable

The intervention boundary is explicit: trigger below 7 g/dL, one-unit transfusion with reassessment, maintain 7-9 g/dL during ICU protocol exposure.

Key monitoring concept

Track achieved hemoglobin, units transfused, protocol deviations/crossover, mortality, cardiac complications, organ dysfunction, and subgroup exceptions.

Correction-sensitive interpretation: Use 20% cardiac disease among enrolled patients versus 26% among non-enrolled patients, and use P>0.15 / P>0.58 for the cointervention-similarity statements. The uncorrected article text reverses the cardiac-disease enrollment comparison and prints equality signs for those P values.
