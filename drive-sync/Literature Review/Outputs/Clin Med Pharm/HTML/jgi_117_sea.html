One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a stratified random sample of 21 Pennsylvania hospitals, anticoagulation care for older patients with DVT or pulmonary embolism often met some—but not all—then-current consensus process targets, with substantial hospital-to-hospital variation. The largest operational gaps involved timely therapeutic PTT, platelet monitoring, adequate heparin-warfarin overlap, and early warfarin initiation.

The final sample contained 270 Medicare beneficiaries treated with IV heparin. Within 24 hours, 179/270 achieved the study-defined therapeutic PTT (66%; 95% CI 59–72). Heparin lasted at least 5 days in 226/270 (84%; 95% CI 79–87). Platelets were checked within 3 days in 148/270 (55%; 95% CI 46–62) and within 7 days in 177/270 (66%; 95% CI 58–74). Among 266 patients discharged on warfarin, 193 (72%; 95% CI 63–80) remained on heparin until PT/INR was therapeutic and 155 (59%; 95% CI 51–67) started warfarin within 2 days.

The study's strongest contribution is implementation-oriented rather than therapeutic: it demonstrates that care processes varied sharply by hospital even within one state. Early warfarin initiation was associated with a shorter geometric mean length of stay (8.2 vs 9.7 days; p=0.003), but this was observational. The authors also acknowledge that the 24-hour therapeutic-PTT target lacked randomized-trial support and that the traditional “1.5 times control” PTT threshold varied by reagent and laboratory.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors set out to measure adherence to consensus anticoagulation recommendations in a representative multihospital sample. Of 357 selected Medicare discharges, 270 remained in the final sample after unavailable charts, miscoding, lack of IV heparin, and other exclusions.

The abstract reports 66% therapeutic anticoagulation within 24 hours, 66% platelet monitoring during the first week, 84% receiving at least 5 days of heparin, and 72% of warfarin-treated patients remaining on heparin until PT/INR was therapeutic. Early warfarin initiation was associated with shorter length of stay.

Introduction / background

The paper frames IV heparin followed by warfarin as standard treatment for DVT/PE and describes serial NHLBI/ACCP consensus conferences as the benchmark for anticoagulation care. The relevant process targets included early therapeutic anticoagulation, platelet monitoring, at least 5 days of heparin, adequate heparin-warfarin overlap, and the acceptability of starting warfarin concurrently with heparin.

Prior studies had focused mainly on achieving therapeutic PTT within 24 hours and were usually limited to one or a few hospitals. Nomogram-based dosing appeared to improve timely anticoagulation in those reports. The current study was designed to assess broader adherence across 21 randomly selected hospitals.

Methods — Patients

The investigators identified 1992 Pennsylvania hospitalizations with DVT or pulmonary embolism in the principal diagnosis position, selected each patient’s first qualifying admission, and excluded deaths, transfers, vena cava filter placement, and several treatment situations that would make IV heparin management atypical. Hospitals were randomly selected after stratification by size, geography, and urban/rural location.

Up to 18 qualifying discharges per facility were randomly sampled, targeting 15 eligible patients per hospital. The final sample represented 21 hospitals and 270 patients.

Methods — Data collection

A trained abstractor confirmed that the attending physician treated the patient for DVT or PE; objective diagnostic confirmation was not required. The study therefore measures treatment practice conditional on the clinician’s working diagnosis rather than diagnostic accuracy.

Collected variables included initial heparin dose, heparin duration, timing/results of the first six PTTs in 72 hours, up to seven platelet counts, timing of warfarin initiation, and the PT ratio or INR closest to heparin discontinuation.

Methods — Analysis

Five process endpoints were defined: time to therapeutic PTT; platelet monitoring within 3 and 7 days; at least 120 hours of IV heparin; time to warfarin initiation; and continuation of heparin until therapeutic warfarin effect. Therapeutic PTT required the first of two consecutive values above 1.5 times control, excluding tests within 3 hours of the initial bolus. Therapeutic warfarin effect was PT ratio ≥1.3 or INR ≥2.0.

GEE was used for population estimates and confidence intervals while accounting for hospital clustering. Time to therapeutic PTT used survival methods. Length of stay was log-transformed, and the early-versus-late warfarin comparison was observational.

Results

The 21 hospitals included 6 rural sites; among 15 urban hospitals, 6 were small. Initial heparin practice was commonly a 5,000-unit bolus (172/270, 64%) and 1,000 units/hour infusion (146/270, 54%). Median time to first PTT check was 6.25 hours; after a subtherapeutic PTT, the geometric mean time to repeat testing was 10.2 hours (95% CI 8.9–11.7).

Process adherence ranged from 55% for platelet monitoring within 3 days to 84% for at least 5 days of heparin. Hospital significantly predicted both time to therapeutic PTT (hospital median 6.5–40 h; p=0.009) and heparin duration (hospital median 5.8–8.7 d; p<0.001). Early warfarin was associated with shorter geometric mean stay (8.2 vs 9.7 d; p=0.003). Eleven hospitals still used PT ratios in 1992; by 1995 all had adopted INR reporting.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the 66% 24-hour therapeutic-PTT rate as better than several prior usual-care reports but below the 77–99% reported in many nomogram settings. They argue that hospital variation and prior nomogram experience suggest that relatively simple interventions could improve performance. Early warfarin initiation is highlighted as a potential cost-saving opportunity, but the reported length-of-stay association is not randomized.

The discussion also provides important self-critique: the 24-hour PTT target was not supported by a randomized controlled trial; some researchers questioned its connection to recurrence; the traditional PTT threshold was laboratory-dependent; and 100% process compliance was not a realistic expectation. The paper concludes that 1992 practice often failed to achieve then-current ACCP process objectives despite reasonably broad statewide sampling.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon / historical implementation evidence. The paper is valuable for a heparin deep dive because it shows how anticoagulation recommendations fail operationally across hospitals and illustrates the rationale for protocolized dosing/monitoring. It should not be used as a current therapeutic protocol.
8/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly relevant to unfractionated heparin monitoring, heparin-warfarin overlap, and quality-of-care implementation in VTE.

Evidence basis: Multihospital data on therapeutic PTT timing, platelet monitoring, duration, and warfarin initiation.

Principal limit: Historical care and historical consensus targets limit direct present-day practice use.

What would change the score: Would increase with linkage to contemporary UFH protocols or current anticoagulation quality metrics.

5/10

Novelty

Rationale: For its era, the study broadened prior single/few-hospital work to a stratified random sample and examined several anticoagulation process measures.

Evidence basis: Authors describe it as the first study to demonstrate practice gaps across multiple additional consensus recommendations.

Principal limit: The contribution is mainly descriptive implementation evidence rather than a new therapeutic strategy.

What would change the score: Would increase with prospective intervention testing or stronger mechanistic/causal analysis.

6/10

Method strength

Rationale: Sampling and clustered analyses are appropriate for a statewide practice survey, but the design is retrospective and nonrandomized with respect to treatment processes.

Evidence basis: 21 hospitals were randomly selected after stratification; GEE accounted for clustering; time-to-PTT used survival methods.

Principal limit: Diagnosis was not independently confirmed, causal comparisons were not randomized, and several process thresholds were imperfectly calibrated.

What would change the score: Would increase with prospective data collection, validated VTE ascertainment, prespecified causal contrasts, and standardized laboratory calibration.

6/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Descriptive adherence estimates are reasonably supported, but the paper cannot establish that improving each process measure improves patient outcomes.

Evidence basis: Counts, percentages, confidence intervals, and hospital variation are reported for the main process endpoints.

Principal limit: Primary endpoints are surrogate/process measures; the authors acknowledge limited randomized support for the 24-hour PTT target; 90-day readmission data were not shown.

What would change the score: Would increase with patient-centered outcomes, complete outcome reporting, and prospective intervention evidence.

5/10

External validity

Rationale: The randomized hospital sampling supports representativeness for older Pennsylvania inpatients in the early 1990s, but generalization beyond that setting is constrained.

Evidence basis: Hospitals were stratified by size, geography, and urban/rural status, and the sample included 21 sites.

Principal limit: Population was Medicare-heavy, geographically limited, and reflects 1992 anticoagulation practice and laboratory reporting.

What would change the score: Would increase with broader regions, younger populations, contemporary practice, and replication across health systems.

6/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The study clearly identifies operational failure points—initial dosing, PTT follow-up, platelet monitoring, overlap, and early warfarin—and supports system-level QI thinking.

Evidence basis: Hospital-level variation and prior nomogram experience are used to motivate simple institutional interventions.

Principal limit: Its specific process targets should not be reused as a current protocol without contemporary guideline verification.

What would change the score: Would increase with intervention results, implementation details, and modern protocol mapping.

Validity

Strengths: stratified random hospital selection; multiple hospitals spanning rural/urban and size strata; chart-level abstraction; explicit process endpoint definitions; clustered statistical analysis; confidence intervals for major proportions; survival analysis for censored PTT timing.

Limitations: retrospective chart review; Medicare/older Pennsylvania population; 1992 care; no independent confirmation of VTE; laboratory-dependent PTT target; observational early-warfarin/length-of-stay association; process rather than patient-centered primary outcomes; the 90-day readmission comparison is not shown; and Table 1 contains an internal count/percentage inconsistency.

Practice translation

The reusable lesson is systems-oriented: anticoagulation quality depends not only on choosing a drug but on reliable dosing, timely lab follow-up, safety monitoring, transition overlap, and local protocol execution. Hospital-level variation provides a direct argument for standardized workflows and feedback loops.

The specific numeric thresholds and monitoring practices are historically anchored. Any present-day application should verify current guidelines, laboratory calibration, and institutional protocol rather than transplanting the paper's 1989 consensus benchmarks.

Bottom line

Use now: as historical evidence for protocolization, audit design, and implementation failure modes in UFH/warfarin management. Do not use now: as stand-alone guidance for current UFH dosing, therapeutic aPTT definitions, HIT monitoring cadence, or duration/overlap requirements.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Audit the whole anticoagulation chain
Initial dose, time to first lab, response to a subtherapeutic lab, platelet surveillance, treatment duration, transition timing, and overlap are distinct failure points.
2. Hospital variation is actionable
Median time to therapeutic PTT varied from 6.5 to 40 hours by hospital, supporting institution-level process review rather than attributing all delay to patient factors.
3. Protocolization was the authors’ proposed lever
They cite better historical performance with heparin nomograms and suggest nomograms/education as relatively simple interventions.
4. Separate process adherence from clinical benefit
The paper describes whether process targets were met; it does not prove that each target causally improves recurrence, bleeding, or mortality.
5. Treat historical assay thresholds cautiously
The authors explicitly acknowledge that the 1.5× control PTT target can be inaccurate across reagents and laboratories.
6. Preserve source anomalies
Table 1’s male count/percentage inconsistency is a reminder to retain primary-source provenance rather than silently normalizing contradictory data.
