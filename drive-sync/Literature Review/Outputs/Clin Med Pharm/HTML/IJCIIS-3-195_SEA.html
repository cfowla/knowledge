Comparison of heparin dosing based on actual body weight in non-obese, obese and morbidly obese critically ill patients

Gerlach AT, Folino J, Morris BN, Murphy CV, Stawicki SP, Cook CH. International Journal of Critical Illness and Injury Science. 2013;3(3):195-199. DOI: 10.4103/2229-5151.119200.

Metadata and scope

Source type
Original article; retrospective single-center cohort/review of clinical records.
Population
62 critically ill adults: 21 non-obese, 21 obese, 20 morbidly obese.
Exposure/intervention
Continuous IV UFH without bolus; initial 16 units/kg/h if non-obese and 12 units/kg/h if obese/morbidly obese, using actual body weight.
Primary focus
Dose and time to first therapeutic aPTT; secondary steady-state and aPTT-control outcomes; bleeding observations.
Study period
July-December 2007; published 2013.
Scope warning
This study evaluates one institution's aPTT-based UFH protocol and surrogate anticoagulation control; it does not establish patient-centered efficacy or a universally optimal modern UFH regimen.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In this small ICU cohort, obese and morbidly obese patients started at a reduced actual-body-weight UFH infusion rate reached the first therapeutic aPTT and steady state in a similar amount of time as non-obese patients, but required lower units/kg/h at steady state.

The study was prompted by uncertainty about whether actual or ideal body weight should drive continuous UFH dosing in critically ill patients with obesity. Investigators retrospectively reviewed adults receiving UFH for >24 hours in surgical or medical ICUs. UFH was given without a bolus: 16 units/kg/h for non-obese patients and 12 units/kg/h for obese/morbidly obese patients. The institutional nomogram targeted either aPTT 57-84 seconds or 57-70 seconds.

Across BMI groups, at least one therapeutic aPTT occurred in 91%, 91%, and 95% of non-obese, obese, and morbidly obese patients, respectively (P=0.83). Mean time to first therapeutic aPTT was 17.2, 20.8, and 16.8 hours (P=0.54). At the first therapeutic aPTT, actual-body-weight-normalized infusion rates were not significantly different (13.5, 11.7, and 12.5 units/kg/h; P=0.35), whereas total units/h and IBW-normalized rates increased with obesity. At steady state, actual-body-weight-normalized rates were lower in obese and morbidly obese patients (16.3, 11.6, and 11.1 units/kg/h; P=0.01). Only 57%-62% reached the study's steady-state definition.

Safety inference is weak: six minor bleeds occurred, no major bleeding was observed, and the cohort was too small for reliable bleeding comparisons. The paper supports the plausibility of actual-body-weight dosing with a lower starting rate in substantial obesity, but the evidence is observational, single-center, aPTT-surrogate driven, and protocol-specific.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame obesity as a dosing problem because obesity and critical illness can alter distribution and physiology, while obese patients are underrepresented in UFH studies. They identify uncertainty over actual versus ideal body weight and cite prior retrospective work suggesting that morbidly obese patients may require lower per-kilogram UFH rates.

The local ICU question arose because some physiologic indices and ventilator settings use ideal body weight, prompting debate about whether continuous medications should do the same. The stated study purpose was to compare UFH dosing rates in critically ill patients with and without obesity, especially the time to first therapeutic aPTT.

Materials and methods

After IRB approval, the investigators retrospectively identified adult surgical/medical ICU patients receiving continuous UFH >24 hours during July-December 2007. Exclusions were age <18 years, pregnancy, and prisoner status. BMI strata were 20.0-29.9, 30.0-39.9, and >40.0 kg/m². UFH was not bolused. Initial rates were 16 units/kg/h for non-obese patients and 12 units/kg/h for obese/morbidly obese patients, based on actual body weight.

The main endpoints were UFH dose and time at first therapeutic aPTT. Secondary endpoints included achievement/time to steady state (three consecutive therapeutic aPTTs), dose at steady state, and proportions of aPTTs below/in/above range. Major and minor bleeding were prespecified by clinical definitions. ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, and Fisher exact tests were used as appropriate; P<0.05 was the significance threshold.

Results

Sixty-two patients were analyzed. Baseline age, sex, and ICU service were similar, while body weight and BMI separated the intended groups. Achievement of at least one therapeutic aPTT and time to first therapeutic aPTT did not differ significantly. Total infusion rates and IBW-normalized rates were much higher with morbid obesity, while ABW-normalized dose at first therapeutic aPTT did not differ significantly.

At steady state, total units/h remained higher in morbid obesity, but ABW-normalized rates were lower in obese/morbidly obese patients than non-obese patients. Only around 60% reached steady state. Median proportions of therapeutic, subtherapeutic, and supratherapeutic aPTTs did not differ significantly. No major bleeding was reported; minor bleeding occurred in 1, 2, and 3 patients across the three groups.

Discussion

The authors interpret the results as support for actual-body-weight UFH dosing combined with a lower initial per-kilogram rate in obese and morbidly obese ICU patients. They emphasize that total infusion requirements rise with body size, while the per-kilogram requirement may fall at high body weights. They contrast their no-bolus approach with prior studies in which boluses could dominate the first aPTT.

The discussion appropriately acknowledges the difficulty of UFH pharmacokinetics in critical illness and the limited steady-state attainment. The authors compare their findings with earlier obesity studies and argue that reduced initial ABW-based dosing produced comparable anticoagulation timing.

Limitations and conclusion

Major limitations are retrospective design, small sample size, only about half of patients reaching steady state, unmeasured potential determinants of UFH dose, and a single-center protocol. There is no adjustment for confounders and no powered clinical thrombosis or major bleeding endpoint.

The authors conclude that obese and morbidly obese critically ill patients required lower ABW-normalized UFH rates than non-obese patients and that reduced initial ABW-based dosing yielded similar time to first therapeutic aPTT and steady state. They recommend reducing the initial weight-based UFH rate in obesity; this recommendation is stronger than the study design can definitively establish.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High topical relevance for a heparin/obesity evidence map, but modest evidentiary weight for practice decisions.
9/10

Relevance

Directly addresses therapeutic UFH dosing in critically ill patients across obesity strata.

Evidence basis: Dedicated ICU cohort, ABW/IBW comparisons, aPTT timing, and steady-state dosing.

Main limiter: The protocol is institution-specific and historical.

Would change: Would rise with direct alignment to a current local protocol question; fall if the review scope excludes surrogate dosing studies.

7/10

Novelty

For its era, it was a dedicated study of UFH dosing in critically ill obese and morbidly obese patients.

Evidence basis: The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from broader hospitalized cohorts and bolus-containing studies.

Main limiter: Small retrospective dataset and overlapping prior obesity studies.

Would change: Would rise with prospective pharmacokinetic or mechanistic validation; lower today because later studies have expanded the evidence base.

4/10

Method strength

The study has explicit eligibility, protocol, endpoint definitions, and group comparisons, but remains a small retrospective single-center analysis.

Evidence basis: N=62, prespecified BMI groups, reproducible aPTT targets and statistical tests.

Main limiter: No randomization, no multivariable adjustment, limited steady-state sample, confounding by indication/clinical factors.

Would change: Would rise with prospective multicenter design, adjusted analyses, standardized indication mix, and assay strategy.

4/10

Evidence strength

Data support a dosing-pattern association, not a causal or clinically superior regimen.

Evidence basis: Similar aPTT timing, group-specific infusion requirements, bleeding observations.

Main limiter: Surrogate aPTT endpoints; no thrombosis efficacy endpoint; underpowered safety outcomes.

Would change: Would rise with adequately powered clinical outcomes and external replication.

3/10

External validity

Generalizability is restricted by one institution, one nomogram, no bolus, and specific aPTT ranges.

Evidence basis: Mixed medical/surgical ICU population with wide body-weight range.

Main limiter: Protocol and monitoring choices may differ substantially across institutions and current anti-Xa/aPTT practices.

Would change: Would rise with multicenter replication across assays, indications, and protocol designs.

5/10

Implementation value

Useful for designing or interrogating an obesity-sensitive UFH nomogram, but insufficient as a standalone dosing standard.

Evidence basis: Provides concrete initial rates, titration thresholds, and observed ABW/IBW dose behavior.

Main limiter: The study does not identify an optimal starting-rate function or validate clinical outcome superiority.

Would change: Would rise with prospective protocol validation and contemporary monitoring/outcome data.

Clinical-practice translation

Do not use this paper alone to define a contemporary UFH regimen. The evidence is retrospective, surrogate-based, and underpowered for bleeding/thrombosis outcomes. It is better treated as one observational component in a broader obesity/UFH evidence synthesis.

External currency check — not source-derived

A 2024 review focused on UFH dosing and monitoring in critically ill patients continues to emphasize substantial inter- and intra-patient variability and the need for close monitoring (Samimi MN et al. Expert Opin Pharmacother. 2024;25(8):985-997. PMID 38825778; DOI 10.1080/14656566.2024.2364057). A 2025 University of Illinois Chicago Drug Information Group review of therapeutic UFH in obesity reports that the obesity literature remains observational/retrospective and notes that VTE treatment guidelines do not provide specific UFH dosing recommendations for obesity. These external sources support treating the 2013 study as hypothesis-/protocol-informing evidence rather than a definitive modern standard.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build/useful concept
ABW may be a workable dosing scalar, but the initial units/kg/h requirement may need downward adjustment at higher BMI/body weight.
Monitoring lesson
Only ~60% achieved three consecutive therapeutic aPTTs, underscoring how unstable UFH control can be in critical illness.
Protocol-design warning
First-therapeutic-aPTT findings can be confounded by bolus strategy; this cohort used no bolus.
Evidence gap
Patient-centered thrombosis and major bleeding outcomes were not adequately evaluated.
