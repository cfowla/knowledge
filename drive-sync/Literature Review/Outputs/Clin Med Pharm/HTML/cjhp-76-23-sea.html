One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. Highly relevant to heparin nomogram design, but the evidence is best used as implementation-supporting observational data rather than proof that fixed titration is equivalent to weight-based titration.

The institution changed from fully weight-based IV UFH protocols to weight-based initial dosing followed by non-weight-based titration to fit EMR ordering capabilities. In 267 retrospectively sampled patients, the median number of dose adjustments needed to reach therapeutic aPTT was 1 in both eras (p=0.48), and 24-hour therapeutic aPTT occurred in 96.2% versus 99.3% (p=0.09). Adjusted analyses were also nonsignificant: RR 1.23 (95% CI 0.95-1.58) for number of adjustments and OR 0.18 (95% CI 0.02-1.60) for therapeutic aPTT at 24 hours.

The low-target subgroup complicates the interpretation. The newer low-target protocol not only used fixed titration increments but also reduced the initial weight-based dose; only 37.9% reached target at first aPTT versus 44.6% with the prior protocol (p=0.033), with more subtherapeutic first values. Because the before-and-after eras differed in more than titration method, the study cannot isolate the causal effect of weight-based versus fixed titration. The strongest practical takeaway is narrower: after a weight-based start, non-weight-based titration appeared operationally capable of producing high 24-hour aPTT target attainment in this setting.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the institutional switch from fully weight-based dosing to weight-based initiation with non-weight-based titration and states the primary comparison of therapeutic aPTT performance. It reports 137 before-era and 130 after-era patients, a median of one adjustment in each group, and 24-hour target attainment of 96.2% versus 99.3%.

For low-target patients, first-measurement target attainment was 37.9% versus 44.6% (p=0.033). The abstract concludes that non-weight-based titration was similarly effective for achieving therapeutic aPTT, a claim that should be interpreted as similarity of observed process outcomes rather than formal equivalence.

Introduction

The introduction reviews UFH monitoring and the institution's two aPTT pathways: low target 50-70 s for ACS and other prevention-oriented indications, and standard target 60-90 s when active thrombus is present. Before May 2019, both initiation and titration were weight-based; the redesign retained weight-based initiation but converted subsequent titration to non-weight-based steps for EMR compatibility.

A clinically important co-intervention occurred in the low-target pathway: for an 80-kg patient, the newer protocol used a 5600-unit bolus plus 1100 units/h initially versus 6400 units plus 1400 units/h previously. This means low-target first-aPTT differences cannot be attributed solely to titration logic.

Methods

Adults receiving therapeutic IV UFH on cardiology or cardiac-surgery wards were identified from pharmacy dispensing records. Exclusions included non-protocol dosing, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, active liver failure (ALT ≥3× ULN during UFH), and UFH contraindications such as prior HIT or heparin allergy. The before era was January 7, 2015-October 14, 2016; the after era was January 5-October 16, 2020, starting two months after EMR implementation.

Co-primary outcomes were the number of dose adjustments to first therapeutic aPTT and the proportion therapeutic by 24 hours. aPTT was checked every 6 hours until therapeutic and every 24 hours thereafter. The secondary outcome was first-measurement target attainment in low-target patients. Analyses included t test, Wilcoxon, chi-square, Poisson, logistic, and multinomial logistic regression; adjusted models included age, sex, and weight. Each co-primary outcome used p<0.025; secondary significance used p<0.05.

Results

Of 1969 screened records, 267 were included: 130 after-era and 137 before-era. Mean age was 65.6 years, mean weight 82.8 kg, and 30.7% were female. Atrial fibrillation was the most common indication (40.8%). The protocol mix differed by era: low-target use was 50.8% after versus 67.2% before.

There was no statistically significant difference in median adjustments to target (1 [IQR 0-2] vs 1 [IQR 0-1], p=0.48) or therapeutic aPTT at 24 hours (96.2% vs 99.3%, p=0.09). Adjusted RR for adjustments was 1.23 (95% CI 0.95-1.58; p=0.12), and adjusted OR for 24-hour target attainment was 0.18 (95% CI 0.02-1.60; p=0.12). In the low-target subgroup, first-aPTT target attainment was lower after the redesign (37.9% vs 44.6%, p=0.033), with 37.9% versus 19.6% below target.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the high 24-hour target-attainment rates as evidence that weight-based initiation may matter more than weight-based titration. They also attribute the poorer low-target first-aPTT performance partly to the lower initial dose in the newer protocol. This is plausible but not isolated experimentally.

They acknowledge retrospective observational design, small sample size, convenience sampling, possible EMR implementation confounding, and absence of bleeding or thrombosis outcomes. Additional concerns from appraisal are the nonconcurrent eras, imbalance in low-target use and indications, adjustment only for age/sex/weight, absence of formal equivalence/noninferiority design, and limited generalizability of institution-specific aPTT targets.

Conclusion

The paper concludes that weight-based initiation with non-weight-based titration was similarly effective to fully weight-based UFH for the median number of adjustments to therapeutic aPTT and 24-hour target attainment. The defensible implementation interpretation is that fixed titration did not obviously degrade these process outcomes in this cohort; it should not be extrapolated to clinical safety or formal equivalence.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses IV UFH nomogram design, including fixed versus weight-based titration after weight-based initiation.

Evidence basis: The intervention and comparator map closely to practical heparin protocol design decisions.

Principal limit: Single-centre protocol specifics limit transferability.

Would raise score: Replication in broader populations and modern monitoring frameworks would strengthen relevance to implementation.

Novelty
6/10

The authors report this as the first direct comparison of non-weight-based titration with fully weight-based IV UFH protocols.

Evidence basis: The study isolates a practically important protocol-design question not commonly studied head-to-head.

Principal limit: The before-and-after design changes more than titration strategy, particularly the low-target initial dose.

Would raise score: A prospective head-to-head study changing only the titration rule would establish the contribution more cleanly.

Method strength
4/10

The retrospective single-centre before-and-after design is vulnerable to secular change, confounding, and protocol-era effects.

Evidence basis: Eligibility, outcomes, statistical tests, and adjusted models are described, and multiplicity was addressed for co-primary outcomes.

Principal limit: Different calendar eras, convenience sampling, EMR implementation, protocol-target imbalance, and low-target initial-dose changes weaken causal attribution.

Would raise score: Prospective concurrent allocation or randomized/noninferiority design with prespecified power and stable initial dosing would raise this score.

Evidence strength
4/10

The data support similar observed aPTT-process outcomes but do not establish equivalence or noninferiority and do not address clinical events.

Evidence basis: Both groups achieved high 24-hour therapeutic aPTT rates and adjusted estimates with confidence intervals were reported.

Principal limit: No bleeding or thrombosis outcomes; adjusted OR for 24-hour therapeutic aPTT is imprecise (0.18, 95% CI 0.02-1.60); failure to detect a difference is not proof of equivalence.

Would raise score: Adequately powered equivalence/noninferiority testing plus bleeding and thrombosis endpoints would materially strengthen the evidence.

External validity
4/10

Applicability is constrained by one institution, cardiology/cardiac-surgery wards, institution-specific aPTT targets, and historical protocol eras.

Evidence basis: The cohort included several common therapeutic UFH indications and both low- and standard-target pathways.

Principal limit: Results may not generalize to other reagents, aPTT targets, anti-Xa monitoring, ICU/medicine populations, or different titration increments.

Would raise score: Multicentre validation across monitoring strategies and broader indications would raise the score.

Implementation value
7/10

The study is useful for protocol engineering because it suggests fixed titration steps can be operationally workable after weight-based initiation.

Evidence basis: Process outcomes, target ranges, timing, and a concrete low-target dose example are reported.

Principal limit: Complete nomograms are not contained in the retrieved PDF, and clinical safety/outcome equivalence was not tested.

Would raise score: Access to the appendices plus prospective implementation data on time-in-range, bleeding, thrombosis, and workload would raise this score.

Strengths

  • Directly studies a practical UFH nomogram design decision.
  • Reports protocol-specific aPTT targets and measurement timing.
  • Includes both unadjusted and adjusted analyses with confidence intervals.
  • Uses multiplicity control for the two co-primary outcomes.
  • Provides transparent limitations and a detailed chart-screening flow.

Critical limitations

  • Not an equivalence/noninferiority study: nonsignificant differences cannot establish that the strategies are equivalent.
  • Nonconcurrent eras: 2015-2016 versus 2020 permits secular-practice confounding.
  • Intervention bundle changed: the low-target initial dose was also reduced, confounding the effect of titration strategy.
  • Baseline/protocol imbalance: low-target use and indication distributions differed between groups.
  • Limited adjustment: reported adjustment included age, sex, and weight, not all apparent between-era differences.
  • Surrogate/process endpoints only: no bleeding, thrombosis, mortality, or other clinical outcomes.
  • Single-centre aPTT framework: target ranges and reagent calibration are institution-specific.
  • Protocol appendices absent from retrieved PDF: exact titration increments cannot be independently reconstructed here.

Bottom line: The study supports the feasibility of a fixed/non-weight-based titration approach after weight-based UFH initiation in one institutional aPTT protocol, but it does not establish clinical equivalence, safety equivalence, or that weight-based titration is unnecessary.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use nowAs a supporting data point when designing or reviewing UFH nomograms and EMR-friendly titration increments.
Do not use forClaiming fixed titration is clinically equivalent or equally safe, or replacing local validation.
Protocol-design signalWeight-based initiation may be the higher-leverage dosing component, while subsequent fixed steps may be operationally adequate.
Monitoring signalEvaluate first-aPTT subtherapeutic rates separately from 24-hour target attainment; lower initial dosing can shift early performance.
Local validationTrack time to target, time in therapeutic range, number of adjustments, bleeding, thrombosis, supratherapeutic aPTT/anti-Xa, and pharmacist workload.
Next evidence needConcurrent prospective comparison that holds initial dose constant and varies only titration strategy.

Best reusable insight: For CDS/protocol architecture, the paper provides evidence that weight-based initiation and fixed subsequent titration can be separated as design components; however, they should be validated independently rather than treated as an inseparable “weight-based versus non-weight-based” binary.
