2. One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this single-center hospital-wide transition, clean non-sterile urinary catheterization was non-inferior to sterile catheterization for suspected CAUTI by a narrow prespecified 0.5 percentage-point margin and produced a large reduction in catheterization-set carbon footprint.

What the study found

Among 3,293 catheterized adults, suspected CAUTI occurred in 3.8% during the sterile phase and 3.3% during the clean phase. The adjusted risk difference was -0.44 percentage points with a 95% CI from -1.4 to 0.5 percentage points, meeting the study's non-inferiority criterion because the upper bound did not exceed the 0.5 percentage-point margin.

Positive urine cultures were essentially unchanged at 13.1% in both phases. The top cultured organisms remained clinically plausible urinary pathogens or flora, with no reported signal of increased skin-bound bacteria. Clean catheterization sets had a carbon footprint of 0.056 kg CO2-equivalent per use, versus 0.191 kg for the sterile ward/emergency set and 0.316 kg for the sterile operating-room set.

How much to trust it

The study is useful because it uses a large real-world hospital dataset, prespecified non-inferiority margin, adjusted risk differences, interrupted time series, protocol-adherence measures, and life-cycle assessment. Baseline characteristics were generally balanced, and no major institutional changes in patient care, infection control, or microbiology analysis were reported across the study window.

The study is not definitive because it is non-randomized and pre-post, CAUTI was an inferred "suspected CAUTI" based on catheter timing, positive culture, and antibiotic administration rather than standardized prospective adjudication, urine-culture ordering was clinician-dependent, and some high-risk sterile-protocol patients could not be identified and excluded.

5. Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames bladder catheterization as a common hospital procedure traditionally performed sterile to prevent infection, despite cost and carbon-footprint concerns. The study asks whether adopting clean catheterization as standard hospital practice is non-inferior to sterile catheterization for suspected CAUTI and evaluates catheterization-set carbon footprint.

The reported main result is 3.8% suspected CAUTI in the sterile phase versus 3.3% in the clean phase, adjusted risk difference -0.44 percentage points with 95% CI -1.4 to 0.5, meeting the stated non-inferiority criterion. Positive urine cultures were unchanged at 13.1% in both groups. Clean sets reduced carbon footprint by 70.7%-82.3% compared with sterile sets.

Introduction

The introduction argues that catheterization is common, resource-intensive, and historically sterile despite limited direct evidence that sterile insertion prevents CAUTI better than a clean approach. It contrasts guidelines that recommend sterile insertion with Dutch infection-prevention guidance that acknowledges limited evidence and supports clean insertion.

The implementation context is important: the institution adopted a clean non-sterile urinary catheterization protocol hospital-wide in 2024, creating a pragmatic before-after opportunity. The clinical relevance is strongest for routine catheter placement where sterility may be a low-value default, not for high-risk procedural exceptions.

Methods

The study compared two 6-month periods at Radboud University Medical Center: Aug 2023-Jan 2024 with sterile catheterization and Oct 2024-Mar 2025 after transition to clean catheterization. The clean protocol was introduced Apr-Jun 2024, with post-transition analysis delayed until Oct 2024 to allow staff familiarization. Adult inpatients with at least one urinary catheter were eligible; exclusions included age under 18, catheterization less than 120 minutes, kidney transplant, urodynamic examinations, and intravesical therapies where identifiable.

CAUTI was not prospectively adjudicated or routinely surveilled, so the primary outcome was a suspected CAUTI definition requiring an indwelling catheter, a positive urine culture during catheterization, and UTI-directed antibiotics administered 2 days after insertion through 7 days after removal. Analyses used multivariable logistic regression with marginal standardization for adjusted risk differences, a prespecified 0.5 percentage-point non-inferiority margin, interrupted time series for temporal trends, exploratory catheter-duration interactions, and life-cycle assessment using ISO14040/ReCiPe2016 methods.

Results

The analysis included 3,293 patients: 1,647 in the pretransition sterile phase and 1,646 in the post-transition clean phase. Baseline patient, admission, and catheter characteristics were generally balanced by standardized mean differences; median catheter duration was about 1.9 days. Self-reported clean-protocol adherence was high at 91%, and sterile catheterization-set purchasing dropped 96.7% from 4,274 sets in the 6-month pretransition phase to 141 sets in the post-transition phase.

Suspected CAUTI occurred in 62/1,647 patients during the sterile phase and 55/1,646 during the clean phase. The adjusted risk difference was -0.44 percentage points, 95% CI -1.4 to 0.5. Interrupted time series did not identify a significant preintervention trend or postimplementation trend change. Positive urine cultures occurred in 215/1,647 and 215/1,646 patients, respectively. Carbon footprint was 0.056 kg CO2-equivalent for the clean set versus 0.191 kg for the sterile ward/emergency set and 0.316 kg for the sterile operating-room set.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusion

The authors interpret the findings as support that clean catheterization can be a safe default alternative to sterile catheterization for standard hospital practice, while substantially reducing environmental impact. They argue that the absence of increased CAUTI and absence of increased skin-bound bacteria are consistent with CAUTI prevention depending more on catheter necessity and timely removal than on maximal sterility at insertion.

The limitations materially affect causal strength: pre-post design, residual temporal confounding, suspected rather than adjudicated CAUTI, clinician-dependent culture ordering, inability to identify all immunocompromised or guidewire-catheterized patients, self-reported adherence, and single-center setting. The conclusion is credible as a practice-review signal but should be implemented through local infection-prevention governance, prospective monitoring, and explicit sterile-procedure exceptions.

Evidence basis: Diverse hospital adult inpatient cohort and real-world training/adherence data. Limiting factor: Unclear applicability to low-resource settings without reliable clean water, immunocompromised populations, guidewire catheterization, or units with different CAUTI surveillance. Would raise/lower: Raise with multicenter, nursing-home, home-care, and high-risk subgroup data; lower in settings with weak basic infection-control practices.

Implementation value
8.0 / 10

Rationale: The paper provides enough protocol, adherence, purchasing, outcome, and LCA information to support a local quality-improvement proposal.

Evidence basis: Implementation timeline, clean protocol components, staff training, 91% reported adherence, 96.7% reduction in sterile-set purchases, and quantified CO2-equivalent reductions. Limiting factor: Full supplemental protocol and product specifications are not contained in the uploaded PDF. Main strengths

  • Large real-world inpatient dataset with nearly identical pre/post patient counts.
  • Narrow non-inferiority margin, adjusted risk differences, and ITS temporal checks.
  • Protocol adherence assessed through both self-report and purchasing data.
  • Culture spectrum and LCA address mechanism and sustainability, not only headline CAUTI rate.

Main limitations

  • Pre-post observational design cannot fully remove secular confounding.
  • Suspected CAUTI definition depends on culture plus antibiotic administration, not adjudicated symptoms.
  • Urine-culture decisions were based on clinician judgment rather than a standardized trigger.
  • Immunocompromised and guidewire-catheterized patients could not be reliably identified.
  • Single-center Dutch implementation may not map directly to other hospitals or supply chains.

8. Implementation and practice takeaways

Reasonable use

Use this as a source for local IPC/nursing/urology review of whether routine sterile catheterization is necessary for standard adult inpatient catheter placement.

Do not overclaim

Do not claim clean catheterization is proven universally equivalent for all patient groups or all procedural contexts. The study evaluates suspected CAUTI in one tertiary hospital.

Best next step

If adopted locally, pair the protocol change with prospective CAUTI surveillance, culture-ordering review, adherence audits, and explicit sterile exceptions.
