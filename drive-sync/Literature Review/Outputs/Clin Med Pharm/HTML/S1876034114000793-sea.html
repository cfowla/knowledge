One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The report is useful as an antimicrobial-resistance and diagnostic-suspicion signal, especially for cutaneous lesions after travel, but its single-patient design cannot support treatment comparisons or resistance prevalence estimates.

Core thesis. The authors describe a previously healthy, fully vaccinated 14-year-old girl who returned from Ethiopia with nonhealing ulcerated lower-extremity lesions after a traumatic skin injury. Initial cultures yielded Group A Streptococcus, but the clinical course prompted targeted culture on Hoyle's agar. A reference laboratory confirmed non-toxigenic Corynebacterium diphtheriae by PCR and ELEK testing. The isolate is presented as penicillin resistant and erythromycin sensitive, and the authors frame the case as the first clinical report of penicillin-resistant C. diphtheriae in the UK.

Clinical course. The patient received several sequential antibiotics. After erythromycin was added, nausea and vomiting led to replacement with oral azithromycin after one day. Benzylpenicillin was stopped three days after macrolide therapy began, and a total one-week macrolide course was planned. The ulcerations were described as notably improved at that point and the patient was discharged. Because multiple antibiotics were used, Group A Streptococcus was also isolated, and there was no comparator, the case does not establish that macrolide therapy caused the improvement.

Why it matters. The report's durable value is not efficacy evidence; it is the combination of diagnostic pattern recognition and a resistance warning. The authors argue that non-toxigenic cutaneous diphtheria should remain in the differential even in fully vaccinated patients with cutaneous ulcers and recent foreign travel, and that penicillin resistance matters because penicillin was a recommended first-line empiric option for suspected diphtheria in nonallergic patients at the time.

Main limitations. This is one patient, with incomplete antimicrobial susceptibility detail, no MICs, no controlled treatment comparison, and a polymicrobial finding. The literature review is brief and secondary; its six cited studies require direct reading before their quantitative claims should be reused as primary evidence.

Section condensation

Summary

The summary introduces a travel-associated case of non-toxigenic C. diphtheriae in a previously healthy 14-year-old girl and identifies penicillin resistance as the central unusual feature. It states that the case was acquired in Ethiopia and presented locally in the UK.

The authors characterize the report as the first UK clinical case of penicillin-resistant C. diphtheriae and link the finding to the contemporary role of penicillin in prophylaxis and treatment.

Case

The patient presented with bilateral ulcerated lesions of the lower legs and soles with foul-smelling discharge after a month in Ethiopia and a lower-leg injury on a rusty metal gate about three weeks earlier. She was afebrile and systemically well. Later history disclosed fresh-water and sheep exposure. Initial therapy included IV benzylpenicillin and IV flucloxacillin; testing included a malaria film, stool and wound cultures, and later targeted Hoyle's agar culture.

Group A Streptococcus was isolated first, but cellulitis did not improve as expected after a switch back to benzylpenicillin. Black colonies on Hoyle's agar and Gram-positive bacilli led to reference-laboratory confirmation of non-toxigenic C. diphtheriae by PCR/ELEK. The isolate is reported as penicillin resistant and erythromycin sensitive. Oral erythromycin was added but caused nausea/vomiting and was replaced after one day by oral azithromycin. After three days of macrolides, benzylpenicillin was stopped; four more days of azithromycin were planned, the ulcers were notably improved, and the patient was discharged. Public health, family screening, isolation, and infection-control measures were implemented while toxigenicity was being clarified.

Discussion

The discussion emphasizes two author conclusions: penicillin resistance is clinically important given penicillin's then-recommended empiric role, and non-toxigenic cutaneous diphtheria should be considered in fully vaccinated patients with ulcerative skin disease and recent foreign travel. The authors repeat their claim that this is the first UK clinical report of penicillin-resistant C. diphtheriae.

The brief literature review describes prior non-toxigenic invasive or cutaneous disease in Brazil, Vancouver, Poland, and the United States, including endocarditis, bloodstream infection, and risk-factor patterns. These are secondary summaries in this source rather than independently verified primary-study findings.

Validity and interpretation

Strengths: direct clinical observation, reference-laboratory organism confirmation using PCR/ELEK, a reported susceptibility phenotype that motivated the paper, and a clearly described treatment timeline. Weaknesses: uncontrolled case design, absent MICs and susceptibility-method details, multiple sequential antimicrobials, co-isolation of Group A Streptococcus, and no way to separate spontaneous healing or contributions of different antibiotics.

Interpretation boundary: the report supports existence of this particular clinical presentation and reported resistance phenotype. It does not support an incidence estimate for penicillin resistance, comparative efficacy of macrolides versus penicillin, or a general recommendation to replace penicillin based on this case alone.

Source-text issue preserved. The susceptibility paragraph contains an incomplete phrase stating that the bacteria were “penicillin and sensitive to erythromycin.” The title, summary, and authors' highlighted discussion point independently state penicillin resistance, so the appraisal treats resistance as source-supported without silently repairing the incomplete sentence itself.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Diagnostic signal
Vaccination does not by itself exclude non-toxigenic cutaneous diphtheria in a patient with compatible ulcers and relevant travel/exposure history.
Microbiology signal
Targeted culture and confirmatory reference-laboratory testing were decisive when the initial bacterial finding did not explain the clinical course.
Stewardship signal
A reported penicillin-resistant isolate makes organism-specific susceptibility data important rather than assuming historical susceptibility.
Do not overclaim
Do not use this single case as proof that macrolides are superior, that penicillin resistance is common, or that current guideline therapy should be changed.
