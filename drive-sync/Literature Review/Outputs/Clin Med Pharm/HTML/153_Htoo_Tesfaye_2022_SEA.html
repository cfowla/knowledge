One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read soon. The study is a strong real-world comparative-effectiveness analysis for older adults, particularly useful for quantifying HHF differences and absolute benefit in cardiorenal disease. Its causal certainty is limited by nonrandom treatment assignment, claims-based measurement, and short treatment persistence.

Core thesis. In propensity-score–matched older adults with T2D, empagliflozin had similar overall modified-MACE risk to liraglutide (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.79–1.03) but lower HHF risk (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.52–0.82). Compared with sitagliptin, empagliflozin was associated with lower modified MACE (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.60–0.77) and HHF (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.36–0.56). Absolute differences were largest in patients with established ASCVD, HF, or CKD.

Most decision-useful nuance. The empagliflozin-vs-liraglutide MACE association was not uniformly favorable: benefit appeared concentrated in ASCVD/HF strata and the sex interaction was potentially heterogeneous (male HR 0.85; female HR 1.16; P=.02 for homogeneity). By contrast, empagliflozin-vs-sitagliptin MACE and HHF associations favored empagliflozin across all prespecified major subgroups, with substantially larger absolute RDs in high-risk patients.

Section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame a practical comparative-effectiveness gap: SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1RAs both have cardiovascular evidence, but treatment effects may differ by baseline ASCVD, HF, CKD, age, and individual agent. Direct comparisons in routine-care older populations were limited.

The study therefore compares empagliflozin with liraglutide as an active cardioprotective alternative and sitagliptin as a widely used DPP-4 inhibitor with neutral cardiovascular trial evidence, both overall and across prespecified cardiorenal and demographic strata.

Methods

Two new-user cohorts were built from Medicare fee-for-service claims. Patients were older than 65 years, had T2D, and had 12 months of continuous Parts A/B/D coverage before initiation. Major exclusions included type 1/secondary diabetes, cancer, ESRD/kidney replacement, HIV, transplant, and nursing-home admission. Follow-up began one day after initiation and was censored for treatment discontinuation/switching, within-class switching, disenrollment gap >30 days, death, study end, or outcome; exposure was extended 60 days past last supplied dose.

Confounding control used 143 baseline covariates and 1:1 nearest-neighbor PS matching without replacement (caliper 0.01), repeated within each subgroup. HRs used Fine-Gray Cox models; RDs used weighted least squares. Sensitivity analyses included 30-day exposure grace, 2-year ITT, baseline-metformin restriction, baseline-insulin exclusion, class comparators, and quantitative residual-confounding analysis.

Results

Matched cohort 1 contained 22,894 pairs (empagliflozin vs liraglutide) and cohort 2 contained 22,812 pairs (empagliflozin vs sitagliptin). Postmatch measured balance was strong (treatment-model C statistic ≈0.5). Median treatment duration was about 5 months. Compared with liraglutide, empagliflozin showed no clear overall modified-MACE reduction but lower HHF; compared with sitagliptin it showed lower modified MACE and HHF.

Subgroup analyses emphasized absolute-risk gradients. Against liraglutide, MACE benefit appeared in baseline ASCVD and HF, while HHF generally favored empagliflozin. Against sitagliptin, both outcomes favored empagliflozin across age, sex, ASCVD, HF, and CKD strata, with especially large RDs in HF and CKD. Secondary and sensitivity analyses were directionally consistent, though ITT attenuated the empagliflozin-vs-sitagliptin MACE HR to 0.83.

Discussion and conclusions

The authors interpret the findings as evidence that empagliflozin may offer greater HHF protection than liraglutide or sitagliptin in older adults, and greater MACE protection than sitagliptin. For liraglutide, the comparative MACE advantage was concentrated in patients with cardiovascular disease history rather than the entire cohort.

They explicitly acknowledge the strengths of new-user active-comparator design and extensive claims-based adjustment, but also that residual confounding cannot be excluded. Claims-defined outcomes, unavailable cardiovascular-specific mortality, and brief treatment persistence constrain causal and long-term interpretation.

Evaluation and appraisal

9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses comparative cardiorenal outcomes for three commonly used glucose-lowering agents in older adults.

Evidence basis: Large Medicare cohorts; clinically meaningful cardiovascular and HHF outcomes.

Principal limiting factor: Study is restricted to adults older than 65 years and reflects 2014-2018 use patterns.

Would raise/lower: Contemporary replication across broader age groups and current treatment patterns.

7/10

Novelty

Rationale: Adds agent-to-agent real-world comparison and subgroup absolute effects beyond placebo-controlled CVOTs.

Evidence basis: Direct empagliflozin-vs-liraglutide and empagliflozin-vs-sitagliptin comparisons.

Principal limiting factor: Observational design and several questions had prior trial/meta-analytic context.

Would raise/lower: Prospective head-to-head randomization or strong multi-database replication.

8/10

Method strength

Rationale: Uses a new-user active-comparator design, 143-covariate PS matching, subgroup-specific rematching, competing-risk methods, and multiple sensitivity analyses.

Evidence basis: Postmatch balance was strong; sensitivity analyses were largely directionally consistent.

Principal limiting factor: Residual confounding remains possible; treatment selection and claims measurement are nonrandom.

Would raise/lower: Negative controls, richer clinical covariates/labs, and replicated target-trial emulation across databases.

7/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: The large matched cohorts support robust associations, especially for HHF, but cannot establish randomized causal effects.

Evidence basis: Consistent HRs/RDs, sensitivity analyses, and class-comparator analyses.

Principal limiting factor: Short median treatment duration and observational residual confounding cap certainty.

Would raise/lower: Longer follow-up, randomized head-to-head evidence, or convergent causal analyses.

7/10

External validity

Rationale: Medicare routine-care data improve applicability to older US adults with multiple comorbidities.

Evidence basis: Large real-world population with ASCVD, HF, and CKD strata.

Principal limiting factor: Generalizability to younger patients, Medicare Advantage, non-US systems, and newer treatment eras is uncertain.

Would raise/lower: Replication in younger/diverse populations, contemporary cohorts, and non-US settings.

8/10

Implementation value

Rationale: Provides relative and absolute effect estimates that can inform comparative discussions in older adults.

Evidence basis: Absolute RDs highlight larger potential benefit in patients with cardiorenal disease.

Principal limiting factor: It is not a current guideline and does not incorporate cost, adverse effects, glycemic targets, kidney-function thresholds, or patient preferences.

Would raise/lower: Integration with current guidelines, safety, renal eligibility, cost, and patient-centered outcomes.

Main strengths: large real-world cohorts; active comparators; new-user design; extensive measured-confounder adjustment; separate subgroup rematching; both relative and absolute effects; multiple sensitivity analyses; transparent disclosure of residual-confounding limits.
Main limitations: nonrandom treatment allocation; claims-only covariates and outcomes; no cardiovascular-specific mortality; brief median on-treatment follow-up; older US fee-for-service population; historical treatment era; sponsor/industry relationships warrant awareness even though the authors state funders had no analytic or publication role.

Confidence in appraisal: high for what the source reports and moderate for causal/practice translation because the complete article and supplement were available and internally reconciled, but the design remains observational.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now

Use as supportive comparative-effectiveness evidence when discussing cardiorenal outcome differences among older adults, especially the HHF signal and absolute-risk gradient.

Do not use alone

Do not treat the study as proof that empagliflozin is universally superior to liraglutide or as a substitute for current guidelines, individualized safety/renal eligibility, glycemic goals, cost, or patient preference.

Key clinical nuance

Empagliflozin vs liraglutide: HHF association is consistently favorable, while overall MACE is not clearly different. Empagliflozin vs sitagliptin: both MACE and HHF associations favor empagliflozin.
