One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a German internal medicine discharge workflow, clinician-reviewed GPT-4o-generated patient-oriented discharge summaries substantially improved immediate patient activation versus standard discharge summaries, with a median PAM-13 improvement of 9.6 points.
Why it matters
Discharge summaries are usually written for clinicians, not patients. This paper tests a practical LLM use case: translating existing discharge content into patient-facing language while preserving the legal/clinical standard summary.
Main result
PAM-13 scores were higher in the intervention arm: median 72.5 versus 59.4, Hodges-Lehmann median difference 9.6 points (95% CI 4.3-15.1), p=0.0009. The effect exceeds the cited 4-point minimal clinically important difference.
Secondary results
Health literacy did not significantly improve. Patient ratings improved for helpfulness, comprehensibility, empathy, and satisfaction; trust did not differ.
Best use of the paper
Use as early randomized evidence supporting pilot design for human-reviewed, patient-facing discharge-summary translation workflows.
Main constraint
Outcomes were measured only at discharge and were not linked to adherence, readmissions, adverse events, patient safety incidents, time saved, or cost.
Practice implication
Reasonable to consider controlled pilots with clinician review, privacy controls, audit trails, and outcomes monitoring. Not enough evidence for autonomous production or broad routine adoption.

Section-by-section condensation

Summary / abstract

The article tests whether a large language model can convert standard hospital discharge summaries into patient-oriented discharge summaries and improve patient activation. The trial randomly allocated 128 adults 1:1 to receive either a standard discharge summary or a GPT-4o-generated PODS reviewed by the treating physician.

The intervention improved PAM-13 scores at discharge by a Hodges-Lehmann median difference of 9.6 points (95% CI 4.3-15.1; p=0.0009). The authors interpret this as evidence that LLM-generated PODS can improve patient-oriented transitions, while stating that quality assurance, privacy, and regulation require additional work before routine adoption.

Introduction and research context

The introduction frames discharge as a vulnerable transition: patients often struggle with adverse events, readmissions, and comprehension of clinician-facing discharge summaries. Patient activation is presented as a modifiable construct tied to self-care behavior, health outcomes, and resource use.

Manual PODS can improve comprehension, satisfaction, health literacy, and possibly readmissions, but implementation is limited by labor and clinical expertise requirements. LLMs are proposed as a way to simplify discharge summaries while maintaining content, but real-world effects on patient activation had not been tested in an RCT according to the authors' search.

Methods: design, eligibility, randomization, and masking

This was a single-centre, single-blind RCT in the Department of Internal Medicine at Helios University Hospital Wuppertal. Adults scheduled for discharge were eligible if they were German-proficient and gave informed consent, including agreement to cloud-based processing through an LLM. Randomization used computer-generated permuted blocks with fixed block size six.

Patients were masked to allocation until study completion. Clinicians and research staff were not masked, which matters because the intervention required LLM generation and clinician review. Randomization was not stratified by diagnosis or baseline characteristics.

Methods: LLM intervention workflow

Attending physicians wrote the standard discharge summary as usual. For intervention patients, physicians created a duplicate containing selected sections, de-identified it, and submitted it to GPT-4o version 2024-11-20 through the Microsoft Azure OpenAI Service API. The model was instructed to convert the existing content into plain patient-facing language without adding new clinical information or changing facts.

Sections were processed sequentially within one chat session. The model used fixed sampling parameters: temperature 0.7 and top_p 0.95. Treating physicians reviewed all outputs, reinserted necessary dates and phone numbers, and corrected inaccuracies if needed. No critical errors were flagged by attending physicians, and retrospective review by a study physician found no substantial quality issues.

Methods: outcomes and statistics

The primary outcome was PAM-13, a 13-item measure converted to a 0-100 scale and classified into four activation levels. Secondary outcomes included health literacy using the German short form of the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire and patient perception ratings for helpfulness, comprehensibility, empathy, trust, and satisfaction.

The planned sample size was 128 patients, based on Cohen's d=0.5, SD 10, 80% power, and two-sided alpha 0.05. Analyses used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous/ordinal outcomes, chi-square tests for categorical outcomes, Hodges-Lehmann median differences, Cliff's delta, and Holm-Bonferroni adjustment for patient-perception outcomes and post-hoc PAM-level comparisons.

Results

From 172 screened patients, 128 were randomized and all completed the primary outcome assessment: 64 intervention and 64 control. Median age was 65 years, 53% self-identified as men, 45% as women, and 2% as diverse. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly similar between arms.

The intervention arm had higher PAM-13 scores than controls: median 72.5 (IQR 56.9-80.9) versus 59.4 (50.5-70.2), median difference 9.6 (95% CI 4.3-15.1), Cliff's delta 0.34, p=0.0009. PAM level 4 was more common with intervention (34/64, 53%) than control (12/64, 19%). Health literacy scores were not significantly different: median difference 1.0 point (95% CI 0.0-2.0), p=0.17. Patient-perception outcomes favored the intervention except for trust.

Discussion and limitations

The authors argue that LLM-generated PODS probably act through improved patient knowledge and confidence: jargon is translated into plain language, and vague medical recommendations are converted into concrete action items. They compare the activation effect with traditional patient-activation interventions and suggest potential system-level value, but explicitly state that they did not collect cost or time data.

Limitations are material: outcomes were assessed only at discharge; readmissions, medication adherence, clinical adverse events, and costs were not measured; the setting was a single German internal medicine department; race/ethnicity were not collected; multiple high-need populations were excluded; and the single-blind design could introduce experimenter bias or functional unmasking.

Implementation and conclusion

The implementation discussion identifies four requirements before routine use: safety assurance, data privacy/security, clinician acceptance through workflow integration, and regulatory clarity. Potential harms include omissions, hallucinations, incorrect urgency classification, and insensitive or confusing language. The authors regard human-in-the-loop review as essential.

The conclusion is appropriately cautious: clinician-reviewed LLM-generated PODS improved patient activation and perceptions of discharge-information quality, but broad clinical implementation requires stronger quality assurance, privacy-preserving deployment, regulatory pathways, and evidence on downstream clinical outcomes.

Validity and bias appraisal

Internal validity strengths
  • Prospective randomized design with completed trial registration.
  • All randomized participants completed the primary outcome assessment.
  • Primary outcome used a validated German PAM-13 instrument with reported internal consistency.
  • A priori power calculation was performed for the primary endpoint.
  • LLM output was reviewed by treating physicians, making safety controls explicit.
Internal validity limitations
  • Single-blind only; clinicians and research team were aware of allocation.
  • Functional unmasking is plausible because a patient-facing PODS likely looks different from a standard summary.
  • No baseline PAM-13 reported; endpoint measured only after the intervention at discharge.
  • No stratification by diagnosis, baseline literacy, activation, or clinician.
  • Physician writing style may affect both source summaries and LLM outputs.
External validity strengths
  • Real inpatient discharge workflow rather than vignette-only testing.
  • Older medical population with high chronic-disease burden makes the use case clinically relevant.
  • Intervention uses an available general-purpose LLM and a plausible EHR-adjacent process.
External validity limitations
  • Single German tertiary academic hospital and internal medicine only.
  • German proficiency and independent questionnaire completion required.
  • Rare diseases and serious newly disclosed diagnoses excluded.
  • Race/ethnicity not collected; disparities cannot be assessed.
  • Manual anonymization is not scalable without additional tooling.
Clinical-evidence limitation: PAM-13 is meaningful, but it is still a surrogate for care transitions. This trial should not be treated as evidence that LLM-generated PODS reduce readmissions, adverse events, medication errors, emergency visits, or total costs.

Implementation and practice takeaways

What could change now
Clinical teams can reasonably evaluate patient-facing discharge-summary simplification as a discharge-quality intervention, but only in a controlled pilot with clinician review and local governance.
What should not change yet
Do not replace standard discharge summaries, discharge counseling, or medication counseling. Do not allow unrevised LLM output to reach patients.
Minimum pilot controls
Version-locked model/prompt, structured source sections, PHI handling, clinician sign-off, logging, error categorization, patient comprehension testing, and escalation rules for high-risk diagnoses or medications.
Outcomes to measure locally
PAM-13 or similar activation measure, medication adherence, readmissions/ED visits, patient calls, adverse events, clinician review time, correction rate, patient language access, and equity by literacy/language status.
Clinical pharmacy relevance
The medication section is a high-risk component. A pharmacy review layer may be warranted for dose, duration, indication, renal/hepatic adjustments, stop/start changes, interaction warnings, and medication-specific return precautions.
Operational bottleneck
Manual anonymization and physician review are safety strengths but scalability limits. EHR integration and privacy-preserving generation must be solved before routine use.
Safety requirement: Human review is not optional in the evidence tested here. The paper's positive result applies to reviewed LLM-generated PODS, not raw model output.

What not to overclaim

  • Not proven: lower readmissions, lower emergency visits, improved medication adherence, fewer medication errors, or reduced cost.
  • Not proven: safety in patients with rare diseases, serious new diagnoses, cognitive impairment, low language proficiency, or inability to complete questionnaires.
  • Not proven: that GPT-4o is superior to other LLMs, templates, human writers, teach-back interventions, pictogram-enhanced PODS, or professional plain-language services.
  • Not proven: autonomous generation without physician review or quality assurance.
  • Not measured: time saved, cost-effectiveness, patient-level adverse events from incorrect instructions, or long-term persistence of activation gains.
