Scope warning: This appraisal summarizes and critiques the supplied meta-analysis. It does not establish therapeutic equivalence, noninferiority, formulary interchangeability, or superiority of either SGLT2 inhibitor.

One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper is highly relevant to a dapagliflozin-versus-empagliflozin formulary question because it directly pools head-to-head observational comparisons. Its main pooled estimates show no statistically significant difference in MACE, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke. The signal favoring empagliflozin in participants with heart failure is a subgroup result with limited study support and should remain hypothesis-generating.

The review searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library through 15 April 2025 and included eight retrospective studies. The paper reports 280,617 participants, but Table 1 sums to 290,600; the 9,983-person discrepancy exactly equals the Suzuki et al. row, indicating the narrative group totals omit one listed study. MACE was pooled from five studies and showed RR 1.04 (95% CI 0.96-1.13; I² 64%). Mortality was RR 1.05 (0.96-1.15), myocardial infarction RR 1.04 (0.94-1.16), and stroke RR 1.00 (0.91-1.09), each with I² 0% as reported.

Clinical interpretation must distinguish “no statistically significant difference detected” from “equivalent.” The analysis is entirely retrospective, does not report a prospective review protocol/registration, does not assess publication bias, does not describe sensitivity analyses, and does not explain how hazard ratios versus risk ratios were harmonized. Safety, renal outcomes, glycemic control, adherence, dosing, and concomitant therapies are not sufficiently developed for a formulary interchange decision.

Section condensation

Introduction and background

The authors frame dapagliflozin and empagliflozin as widely used SGLT2 inhibitors with established class-level cardiovascular benefits but limited direct head-to-head comparison. Their stated objective is to compare cardiovascular outcomes in people with type 2 diabetes using newly available comparative studies.

The background discusses pharmacokinetic and mechanistic differences, but these mechanistic arguments are not themselves evidence of comparative clinical outcome superiority and should not be used to interpret null pooled estimates as mechanistically resolved.

Methodology

The review searched four databases from inception through 15 April 2025 plus reference lists, without language or publication-status restriction. Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. Eligible studies had to directly compare dapagliflozin and empagliflozin in type 2 diabetes and report MACE, all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke.

Observational quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. RevMan 5.4.1 random-effects risk ratios were used, with I² for heterogeneity. Subgroup interaction testing covered sex, heart failure, ASCVD, and CKD. Publication bias was not assessed because fewer than 10 studies were included. The report does not provide a full reproducible search string, protocol/registration, sensitivity analysis, GRADE assessment, or clear RR/HR harmonization procedure.

Results

PRISMA accounting reports 854 records, 62 duplicates removed, 792 screened, 776 excluded, 16 reports assessed, and eight studies included. All eight included studies are described as retrospective. Table 1 includes cohorts from Denmark, Korea, Taiwan, and Japan, with follow-up from 21.87 to 40.91 months.

Evaluation and appraisal

Most important validity issue: the paper's principal clinical wording risks treating null difference testing as evidence of comparable effectiveness. The meta-analysis did not test an equivalence or noninferiority hypothesis, and its observational design cannot exclude clinically relevant residual confounding.

Relevance 9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses empagliflozin-versus-dapagliflozin cardiovascular outcomes in T2D.

Evidence basis: Direct comparative meta-analysis of eight retrospective studies.

Limiting factor: No randomized head-to-head evidence.

Would change score: A large prospective randomized direct comparison would raise the score.

Novelty 5/10

Rationale: Updates an earlier direct-comparison meta-analysis with newer observational cohorts.

Evidence basis: Search through 15 April 2025 and inclusion of 2024 cohorts.

Limiting factor: Core conclusion remains broadly no detected difference.

Would change score: New randomized comparative evidence or validated agent-specific effect modification would raise the score.

Method strength 4/10

Rationale: Systematic-search and dual-review methods are present, but all included studies are retrospective and reporting has internal inconsistencies.

Evidence basis: Four databases, dual review, NOS, random-effects models, subgroup interaction tests.

Limiting factor: No protocol/registration reported; unclear RR/HR harmonization; no sensitivity analysis described; source inconsistencies in sample totals and NOS labeling.

Would change score: Prospective protocol registration, ROBINS-I/validated bias assessment, sensitivity analyses, and transparent effect-measure harmonization would raise the score.

Evidence strength 4/10

Rationale: Pooled estimates consistently cross the null, but observational residual confounding and possible cohort non-independence limit causal comparative inference.

Evidence basis: MACE RR 1.04 (0.96-1.13); mortality 1.05 (0.96-1.15); MI 1.04 (0.94-1.16); stroke 1.00 (0.91-1.09).

Limiting factor: Absence of statistical difference is not proof of equivalence or noninferiority; MACE heterogeneity is 64%.

Would change score: Randomized head-to-head data with prespecified equivalence/noninferiority margins would raise the score.

External validity 5/10

Rationale: Large real-world populations across several countries support breadth, but the authors note concentration in Asian and European populations.

Evidence basis: Eight retrospective cohorts and large sample sizes.

Limiting factor: Generalizability to other ethnic groups and health systems is uncertain; subgroup evidence is sparse.

Would change score: Broader multinational comparative cohorts with validated common definitions would raise the score.

Implementation value 6/10

Rationale: Useful for framing formulary questions but insufficient alone to justify interchangeability.

Evidence basis: Direct agent-to-agent pooled estimates and clinically familiar cardiovascular endpoints.

Limiting factor: Safety, renal outcomes, glycemic control, dose/adherence, cost, and inpatient operational outcomes were not extensively evaluated.

Would change score: Integration with randomized CVOT/HF/CKD evidence, safety, renal outcomes, dosing, and formulary constraints would raise the score.

Additional appraisal

  • Selection/reporting: Four-database search and dual review are strengths, but no protocol/registration is reported and the full reproducible search strategy is not shown.
  • Risk of bias: NOS is used, but the table footnote conflicts with the methods regarding domain maxima. No certainty-of-evidence framework is reported.
  • Statistical interpretation: MACE heterogeneity is substantial. The report does not describe influence/sensitivity analysis. Eligibility permits studies reporting RR or HR, yet the pooling section only states that RRs were calculated; conversion/harmonization is not explained.
  • Independence: Several included studies arise from the same countries and periods; the article does not report an assessment for overlapping source populations. This creates a plausible but unquantified risk of non-independent patient contribution.
  • Clinical scope: Safety, renal outcomes, glycemic control, adherence, dose, and concomitant therapy are not adequately assessed for a formulary substitution decision.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use this source for
Direct observational orientation: no clear pooled difference detected in MACE, mortality, MI, or stroke; identify the heart-failure subgroup hypothesis.
Do not use this source for
Declaring dapagliflozin and empagliflozin therapeutically equivalent, proving noninferiority, or selecting a formulary agent on cardiovascular outcomes alone.
What should be paired with it
Drug-specific randomized outcome trials, heart-failure and CKD evidence, safety/tolerability, renal dosing and hold criteria, acquisition cost, inpatient workflow, and guideline recommendations.

Bottom line: The meta-analysis is useful because it asks the right direct-comparison question, but its retrospective evidence base and internal reporting inconsistencies materially limit confidence. It supports “no demonstrated difference in these pooled observational endpoints,” not “proven equivalence.”
