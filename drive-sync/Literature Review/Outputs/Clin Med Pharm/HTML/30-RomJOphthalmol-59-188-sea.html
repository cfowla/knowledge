One-page synthesis

The article describes two young women with plateau iris syndrome and marked angle-closure findings. Both had shallow peripheral but relatively preserved central anterior chamber depth and a positive indentation gonioscopy "double hump" sign. Case 1 had extreme intraocular pressure elevation, severe right-eye field loss, and near-total right optic-disc excavation after earlier symptoms had been attributed to refractive issues. Case 2 had bilateral glaucoma treatment before referral and coexisting retinal findings considered suspicious for autosomal recessive bestrophinopathy.

Both cases underwent laser treatment. Case 1 received acute pressure-lowering therapy followed by peripheral iridotomy and argon laser peripheral iridoplasty, with later IOP of 15 mmHg in both eyes and an open angle through 360 degrees. Case 2 had prior iridotomies, then bilateral iridoplasty because one angle remained occludable. At the last visit her angles were not considered occludable and IOP was 16 mmHg in each eye on beta-blocker plus carbonic-anhydrase-inhibitor therapy. The discussion uses these cases to stress gonioscopic recognition, continued surveillance after iridotomy, and ALPI when residual occlusion persists. Those management statements partly summarize cited literature rather than comparative evidence generated by the two cases.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors distinguish plateau iris configuration from plateau iris syndrome. Syndrome refers to recurrent or persistent gonioscopically confirmed angle closure after a patent iridotomy has relieved relative pupillary block, without central anterior-chamber shallowing. They state that isolated plateau iris syndrome is uncommon and present two young patients whose diagnoses emerged during ophthalmic evaluation.

Introduction

The introduction frames plateau iris as an important cause of primary angle closure in younger patients and separates configuration from syndrome. It states that treatment can include laser iridoplasty or long-term postoperative pilocarpine and recommends considering the diagnosis when IOP rises after an adequate iridotomy or iridectomy.

These are background and management statements supported by cited references, not findings generated by the two reported cases.

Case 1

A 43-year-old woman with moderate hyperopia requested an incidental evaluation for blurred right-eye vision. The right eye had severe visual-field loss with MD -30.88 dB and PSD 6.88 dB. Peripheral chamber depth was shallow with preserved central depth. IOP measured 70 mmHg OD and 36 mmHg OS. Fundus examination showed C/D=1 in the right eye and 0.5/0.4 in the left. Indentation gonioscopy showed the double-hump pattern.

The authors diagnosed plateau iris syndrome and used IV 20% mannitol, acetazolamide, and topical pilocarpine 2%, followed by peripheral iridotomy and ALPI when IOP fell. After laser treatment IOP was 21 mmHg OD and 15 mmHg OS. At later follow-up after pilocarpine withdrawal, the angle was open through 360 degrees and IOP was 15 mmHg in both eyes.

Case 2

A 29-year-old woman referred for juvenile decompensated glaucoma had ocular pain and bilateral visual loss. Earlier IOP was 38 mmHg OD and 40 mmHg OS, falling to 19 and 28 mmHg on multidrug topical therapy. At the authors' examination IOP was 10 mmHg OD and 19 mmHg OS. Peripheral chamber depth was shallow, central depth remained normal, and gonioscopy showed 270 degrees of angle closure that opened to Schaffer IV through 360 degrees with indentation and a positive double-hump sign.

Visual fields showed MD -3.69 dB OD and -5.65 dB OS. Fundus and OCT examinations also showed bilateral retinal abnormalities, and the source labels autosomal recessive bestrophinopathy as presumed. After prior iridotomies, the patient underwent bilateral ALPI because the left angle still appeared occludable. Repeat gonioscopy showed improvement. At the last visit the angle was not considered occludable and IOP was 16 mmHg in each eye on beta-blocker plus carbonic-anhydrase-inhibitor therapy.

Discussion

The authors describe plateau iris syndrome as an uncommon primary angle-closure mechanism in younger adults. They state that a patent iridotomy can reduce angle-closure risk without necessarily normalizing anatomy and argue for periodic gonioscopy even when the angle initially opens. They identify ALPI as the preferred procedure for residual occlusion after iridotomy, citing prior literature for its durability.

The case-specific discussion emphasizes delayed or incidental diagnosis, severe glaucoma that had escaped recognition, intermittent angle-closure features, and macular disease as an additional source of visual loss. The article does not present a formal limitations section.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. The article is useful for diagnostic pattern recognition and historical case context. Do not use it as standalone evidence of ALPI effectiveness or as a current practice guideline.

Validity

The report gives detailed serial observations but has the expected limits of a two-case design. Treatment decisions were individualized, several therapies occurred in sequence or together, and there is no untreated or alternative-treatment comparison. The paper reports no prespecified outcome definitions, sampling frame, statistical analysis, or uncertainty estimates.

Main evidence limit. Improvement after treatment is temporally documented, but the design cannot isolate the effect of iridotomy, ALPI, medication, natural fluctuation, or their combination.

Practice translation

The most reusable point is diagnostic. A young patient with angle closure, preserved central chamber depth, and a double-hump sign on indentation gonioscopy may have plateau-iris anatomy even after iridotomy. The article also shows why follow-up gonioscopy matters. Its treatment claims should be checked against current glaucoma guidance before use in care.

Source-level uncertainty. Case 1’s labeling as plateau iris syndrome is not fully reconciled with the article’s own post-iridotomy definition because the narrative describes the current iridotomy after the diagnosis. Treat this as a terminology/sequence ambiguity in the source, not as evidence that the definition does not apply.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use this source for

Recognizing the pattern of peripheral chamber shallowing with preserved central depth, the double-hump sign, and the possibility of persistent occludability after iridotomy.

Do not use it for

Estimating ALPI success rates, comparing laser with medical or surgical alternatives, or defining a modern treatment standard.
