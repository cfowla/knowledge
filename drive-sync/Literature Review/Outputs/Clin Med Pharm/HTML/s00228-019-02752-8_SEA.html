One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The review found a sparse and heterogeneous controlled-trial literature for hospital system-level anticoagulant prescribing interventions. Some process and surrogate improvements were observed, but direct safety evidence was weak, and no reviewed intervention had sufficient evidence for a strong implementation recommendation.

Most favorable signal

A physician-led anticoagulation consultation service was the only intervention group with a reviewed study showing a statistically significant safety outcome: Landefeld et al. reported 58% lower in-hospital bleeding versus standard care (95% CI 2%–82%; p=0.03). The review considered this insufficient alone for widespread implementation because the study was small and had many exclusions.

Warfarin decision support

Decision-supported warfarin dosing improved anticoagulation control but not demonstrated safety. The pooled major-bleeding odds ratio was 0.46 (95% CI 0.10–2.09; p=0.31; I²=0%), while the pooled time-in-therapeutic-range analysis favored decision support by 11.55 percentage points when the source's control-minus-intervention orientation is reversed (source figure reports -11.55, 95% CI -18.21 to -4.89; p=0.0007; I²=0%).

Heparin monitoring

Point-of-care, thromboelastography, and heparin-assay monitoring systems did not demonstrate statistically significant safety benefit. Process measures improved in some studies: point-of-care testing shortened phlebotomy-to-dose-adjustment time (1.35 vs 6.42 h; p=0.0001), and heparin assay reduced dose adjustments and testing frequency; TEG was more costly and produced fewer therapeutic-range results than standard aPTT monitoring.

Other CDSS and education

Electronic alerts produced selected prescribing-process improvements but did not directly establish patient safety benefit. A hard-stop interaction alert had a strong process effect, whereas a passive drug-interaction alert did not. Two systematic education/feedback programs did not improve the reviewed safety or quality outcomes.

Methods

Protocol, search, and selection

The review registered a PROSPERO protocol and reports using PRISMA. EMBASE, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library, Pretty Darn Quick-Evidence, and Health Systems Evidence were searched from inception through 24 March 2018 without language restriction, supplemented by reference review. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts; full-text disputes were resolved by a third reviewer.

The review deliberately restricted the evidence base to controlled designs and current-practice comparators. It excluded prophylaxis, intra-operative anticoagulation, outpatient/transition interventions, and controls judged inconsistent with current practice. This improves relevance and internal validity but narrows the evidence base.

Extraction, bias assessment, and synthesis

Two reviewers independently extracted study design, intervention, patient characteristics, and all safety/quality outcomes relevant to the question using an EPOC form. Risk of bias was independently assessed using EPOC criteria with third-reviewer adjudication.

Interventions were grouped by consensus. Similar studies were pooled in RevMan 5.3 using inverse-variance random-effects models; heterogeneity was assessed with I² and two-sided p<0.05 defined statistical significance. Where published data were not poolable, authors were contacted and variance was estimated if needed.

Results by intervention family

Anticoagulation consultation services (3 studies)One physician-led service showed lower bleeding and improved selected control metrics; pharmacist-led consultation did not demonstrate significant safety benefit in another study. The review considered consultation promising but insufficiently established.

Decision-supported warfarin dosing (7 studies)No individual study showed significant safety improvement. Pooled major bleeding was not significantly different. TTR favored decision support, and several studies shortened time to INR stability. The review did not support a specific dosing approach and stated pharmacogenetic algorithms could not be recommended from this evidence.

Heparin monitoring systems (3 studies)POC coagulation testing, TEG, and heparin assay did not improve safety outcomes. POC accelerated dose adjustment; heparin assay reduced testing and adjustments; TEG produced fewer therapeutic-range results and higher cost. All three studies were judged high risk of bias.

Other clinical decision support systems (4 studies)Two alert studies addressed warfarin drug interactions and two supported anticoagulant selection. Process outcomes improved in selected systems, but none directly evaluated patient safety outcomes.

Systematic education and feedback (2 studies)No improvement in thrombosis, bleeding, medical errors, or the reported quality measures was established. The authors did not recommend implementation on current evidence.

Validity and interpretation

The review methods are stronger than the underlying evidence base. Restricting to controlled designs and using independent review reduces some review-level bias, but the primary studies are heterogeneous and frequently inadequately powered for bleeding/thrombosis outcomes. The pooled TTR result is statistically precise enough to support a process/surrogate effect; the bleeding meta-analysis is too imprecise to establish safety benefit.

The paper is most defensible as a map of what had been tested through March 2018 and where evidence was missing. It should not be interpreted as proof that consultation services, decision support, or alternative heparin monitoring improve clinical outcomes.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use this source toIdentify the major intervention families, locate foundational warfarin/UFH system trials, and justify safety-powered evaluation of anticoagulation-system changes.
Do not use it toSelect a specific UFH monitoring strategy, mandate a warfarin dosing platform, or infer efficacy for LMWH/DOAC prescribing interventions.
Strongest build implicationMeasure patient-centered safety outcomes alongside process metrics; faster dose adjustment or higher TTR should not substitute for bleeding/thrombosis assessment without evidence linking the intervention to those outcomes.
Research implicationPrioritize high-risk inpatients, contemporaneous standard-care controls, adequately powered safety endpoints, and interventions addressing LMWH/DOAC as well as UFH/warfarin.

Limitations & caveats

  • The search ended 24 March 2018; the paper itself emphasizes lack of recent evidence and absent LMWH/DOAC-specific prescribing-intervention studies.
  • Heterogeneity in population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes limits pooling and direct comparison.
  • Most studies were underpowered for safety and frequently relied on process or surrogate markers of uncertain clinical importance.
  • Eight studies had at least one high-risk-of-bias domain; randomization and allocation methods were often incompletely reported.
  • Online Resource 1 and Online Resource 2 were not present in the retrieved PDF; this artifact does not claim to have inspected those supplementary files.

Source inconsistencies preserved, not silently repaired. The narrative description of Landefeld's 90-day thrombosis outcome uses denominators 46 and 55, while Table 2 reports 42 and 52 for the 90-day event counts; both report p=0.06. The heparin-monitoring paragraph cites Panigada as [49] but attaches citation [47] to the TEG supratherapeutic-result sentence, an apparent internal citation mismatch. These discrepancies do not change the main review conclusion but reduce confidence in secondary numerical transcription.
