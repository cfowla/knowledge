One-page synthesis

The review treats PI3K/Akt as a central signaling network linking extracellular growth cues to survival, proliferation, metabolism, angiogenesis, invasion, immunity, and treatment resistance. It starts with PI3K classes and Akt isoforms, then maps upstream inputs such as RTKs, TLRs, BCRs, GPCRs, and PTEN. The downstream section centers on mTOR, GSK3, FOXO proteins, TSC2, and the p53-MDM2 axis, with substantial attention to crosstalk with RAS/MAPK, NF-kB, and Wnt/beta-catenin. The biological argument is coherent: pathway activation can support several malignant phenotypes, and feedback between nodes can blunt single-agent inhibition.

The treatment section is a 2021 snapshot rather than a durable prescribing reference. The authors review pan-PI3K, isoform-selective PI3K, dual PI3K/mTOR, Akt, and mTOR inhibitors, then argue for rational combinations with receptor-targeted drugs, MAPK inhibitors, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. The strongest limitation is methodological. The paper does not describe a reproducible literature search, selection process, risk-of-bias assessment, or evidence-grading method. Preclinical findings and clinical findings therefore sit in the same narrative without formal weighting. The conclusion that pathway inhibitors and combinations are broadly effective is more confident than the review method can support.

Verdict: Skim deeply. Read the pathway maps, resistance logic, and 2021 inhibitor tables. Use the references to locate primary studies. Do not use the drug-status table as a current practice source.

Section condensation

Introduction and pathway overview

The authors frame PI3K/Akt as a cancer-relevant signaling network that is activated by genomic changes involving PIK3CA, PTEN, Akt, TSC1, and mTOR. PI3K converts PIP2 to PIP3, recruiting signaling proteins including Akt. The review separates class I, II, and III PI3Ks by structure and lipid biology, then distinguishes Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3 by tissue distribution and cancer-related roles.

The class I discussion is the most directly tied to oncology drug development. It emphasizes p110alpha, beta, gamma, and delta, while class II PI3Ks are presented as distinct monomeric enzymes with different lipid products. VPS34, the class III PI3K, is linked to autophagy and vesicular biology. This section is mechanistically useful, but most statements are synthesis of earlier basic-science literature rather than results generated by the review.

Upstream activation

RTKs, TLRs, BCRs, GPCRs, and PTEN are the main upstream topics. EGFR, VEGFR, and FGFR signaling provide examples of receptor-driven PI3K activation, while TLR and antigen-receptor signaling connect the pathway with immune biology. GPCRs and small GTPases add another route into class I PI3K signaling.

PTEN is the main inhibitory counterweight in this section. The review uses PTEN loss or mutation to explain sustained pathway activation and links PTEN with migration and angiogenesis. Figure 1 is useful here because it shows that the pathway is not a linear EGFR-to-Akt chain. Multiple receptor systems converge on PI3K, and several feedback or parallel nodes connect to Akt and mTOR.

Downstream effectors and crosstalk

Akt is presented as a hub that controls mTOR complexes, GSK3, FOXO proteins, TSC2, and MDM2/p53. These nodes connect pathway activity to growth, survival, metabolism, protein synthesis, angiogenesis, inflammation, immunity, and drug resistance. Figure 2 compresses this section effectively by pairing each downstream node with the cellular functions emphasized by the authors.

The review also describes crosstalk with RAS/MAPK, NF-kB, and Wnt/beta-catenin. This is important for therapy because inhibition of one pathway can release feedback or shift signaling through another. The article repeatedly uses this network behavior to explain resistance and to justify combination therapy.

Cancer phenotypes and tumor microenvironment

The phenotype section links PI3K/Akt signaling with tumorigenesis, apoptosis avoidance, drug resistance, metabolic adaptation, angiogenesis, metastasis, inflammation, and immunity. The review reports examples of pathway-mediated resistance, including an IGF1R/p110beta/Akt/mTOR route associated with resistance to alpelisib in PIK3CA-mutant breast cancer. It also connects Akt activity with PD-L1 regulation in preclinical models.

The tumor-microenvironment section extends the pathway beyond malignant cells. Cancer-associated fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells, extracellular matrix, and paracrine factors are discussed as sources or targets of PI3K/Akt signaling. The authors suggest that stromal and endothelial pathway targeting may complement tumor-directed therapy, but this remains a translational argument rather than a demonstrated cross-tumor treatment rule.

PI3K, Akt, and mTOR inhibitors

The treatment section divides PI3K inhibitors into pan-class I, isoform-selective, and dual PI3K/mTOR approaches. It includes specific early clinical findings, such as a 100 mg/day maximum tolerated dose in a 35-patient BKM120 phase I study, a 29% remission figure reported for alpelisib plus fulvestrant in heavily pretreated PIK3CA-mutant tumors, and a 36% response rate reported with taselisib in PIK3CA-mutant breast cancer. These are secondary reports in a narrative review and should be checked against the cited primary trials before reuse.

The Akt and mTOR sections cover MK-2206 and other Akt inhibitors, rapamycin analogues, and ATP-competitive mTOR inhibitors. The authors emphasize incomplete blockade, feedback activation, toxicity, and genotype-specific sensitivity as reasons that drug development is difficult. Table 2 is especially useful as a 2021 toxicity and development-status snapshot, but it is now historically dated.

Combination strategies and conclusions

The review describes combinations with receptor-targeted drugs, MAPK inhibitors, chemotherapy, PARP inhibition, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. The rationale is mostly network-based: pathway redundancy and feedback can limit monotherapy, so simultaneous targeting may restore sensitivity or deepen response. The authors also acknowledge increased toxicity, particularly when PI3K and MAPK pathways are inhibited together.

The conclusion identifies hyperglycemia, rash, and other toxicities as persistent problems and questions how well conventional development models predict PI3K-inhibitor efficacy and toxicity. The authors call for stronger mechanistic understanding, better patient selection, and more rational combinations. Those points remain useful as research questions, but the broad therapeutic conclusion is not supported by a systematic evidence-selection method.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 8/10

The pathway remains central to cancer biology and targeted therapy, but the treatment-status section is dated.

Basis. Broad coverage of PI3K classes, Akt, mTOR, tumor phenotypes, inhibitors, and combinations.

Limit. It is a 2021 review and not a current treatment guide.

Would change with. A current systematic review or guideline tied to present approvals and biomarkers.

Novelty: 5/10

The article is mainly a broad synthesis of established pathway biology and drug development rather than a new method or dataset.

Basis. It integrates upstream activation, downstream effectors, cancer phenotypes, and therapeutic strategies in one review.

Limit. Most mechanistic content predates the review.

Would change with. A clearly new organizing framework or new comparative synthesis with explicit methods.

Method strength: 3/10

The review is narrative and does not report a reproducible search, selection, or risk-of-bias method.

Basis. The paper cites 274 references and spans preclinical and clinical literature.

Limit. No systematic search strategy, eligibility criteria, evidence grading, or study-quality assessment is reported.

Would change with. A prespecified systematic-review method with transparent selection and appraisal.

Evidence strength: 5/10

Many claims are linked to primary literature, but evidence types are mixed and are not formally weighted.

Basis. The review includes mechanistic studies, animal models, early trials, phase III results, and drug-status tables.

Limit. Clinical and preclinical findings are often presented in one narrative without a formal certainty framework.

Would change with. Direct primary-study appraisal and evidence grading for each therapeutic claim.

External validity: 4/10

The article discusses many cancer types, but that breadth does not establish generalizability across tumors or patient populations.

Basis. Examples span breast, lung, hematologic, gastrointestinal, and other cancers.

Limit. The pathway behaves differently by tumor genotype, isoform, treatment history, and microenvironment.

Would change with. Tumor-specific evidence synthesis with biomarker-defined populations and external validation.

Implementation value: 5/10

It is useful for mechanism teaching and for reconstructing the 2021 drug landscape, but it should not drive current treatment decisions.

Basis. Figures and tables organize pathway nodes, inhibitor classes, targets, phases, and toxicities.

Limit. Regulatory status and therapeutic options have changed since publication.

Would change with. Current labels, guidelines, and comparative clinical evidence.

Main strength. The article gives a compact map of pathway architecture, cancer phenotypes, resistance logic, and the inhibitor pipeline available in 2021.
Main limitation. There is no reported systematic-review method. The source mixes cell-line work, animal studies, early trials, and later trials without a formal hierarchy of evidence or risk-of-bias assessment.

Confidence in appraisal: high. The source type, section structure, main figures and tables, therapeutic claims, conclusion language, and methodological omissions were directly inspected in the supplied PDF.

Current-context verification

This section is external verification, separate from the 2021 source. FDA approved capivasertib with fulvestrant on November 16, 2023 for selected HR-positive, HER2-negative advanced or metastatic breast cancer with PIK3CA, AKT1, or PTEN alterations. FDA approved inavolisib with palbociclib and fulvestrant on October 10, 2024 for endocrine-resistant, PIK3CA-mutated HR-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer. FDA also lists the follicular-lymphoma accelerated approval for copanlisib as withdrawn. These later changes confirm that the review's inhibitor-status table is not suitable for current treatment selection.
