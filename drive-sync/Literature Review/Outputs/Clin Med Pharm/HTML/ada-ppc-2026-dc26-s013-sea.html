One-page synthesis

The chapter treats older-adult diabetes care as a problem of heterogeneity rather than chronological age alone. It asks clinicians to assess medical illness, cognition, self-management function, social support, treatment burden, hypoglycemia, polypharmacy, frailty, mobility, nutrition, and what matters to the individual. Figure 13.1 organizes that assessment around the 4Ms. Table 13.1 turns geriatric assessment into a set of screening questions, tools, cutoffs, and time estimates.

Glycemic intensity changes with health status. Healthy older adults may use A1C targets below 7.0 to 7.5 percent and standard CGM targets. Complex or intermediate health generally supports A1C below 8 percent and more permissive CGM targets. Very complex or poor health shifts the goal away from A1C toward avoiding hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia. The same time-to-benefit logic applies to blood pressure, lipid treatment, and other prevention.

The pharmacotherapy section favors low-hypoglycemia regimens, deintensification, and simpler plans when treatment exceeds the individual's capacity or expected benefit. It keeps cardiorenal benefit in view for SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1-based therapy, but adds older-adult cautions such as volume depletion, weight loss, gastrointestinal effects, cognition, dexterity, and cost. Figure 13.2 gives a four-step treatment-difficulty pathway. Figure 13.3 gives a concrete insulin-simplification algorithm. Table 13.3 links health state to reasons for simplification and deprescribing.

Care-setting changes matter. PALTC sections call for staff training, facility policies for dysglycemia, attention to unpredictable food intake, and prompt notification thresholds. End-of-life care changes the outcome of interest to comfort, symptom control, quality of life, and dignity. Strict glucose, blood pressure, and lipid management can be reduced when it no longer supports those goals.

Section condensation

Comprehensive assessment and geriatric syndromes

More than 29 percent of people older than 65 years have diabetes according to the chapter's cited sources. The chapter emphasizes variation in diabetes type, duration, complications, comorbidities, function, cognition, support, and treatment burden. It recommends a recurring comprehensive assessment rather than a fixed age-based treatment template.

The 4Ms framework and Table 13.1 provide the operational structure. Screening covers cognition, delirium, depression, falls, frailty, malnutrition, pain, polypharmacy, sarcopenia, hearing, vision, insomnia, urinary incontinence, dexterity, dizziness, and executive function.

Neurocognitive function

Diabetes is associated in the cited evidence with cognitive decline and several dementia outcomes. Hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, and longer diabetes duration are also associated with cognitive decline. Recommendation 13.3 calls for screening at age 65 or older at the initial visit, annually, and as needed.

Cognitive impairment can make glucose monitoring, insulin dosing, meal timing, and hypoglycemia recognition unsafe. The chapter therefore connects a positive cognitive screen to plan simplification and greater care-partner support.

Hypoglycemia and diabetes technology

Older adults have multiple hypoglycemia risks, including erratic intake, insulin use, kidney dysfunction, and cognitive decline. The chapter recommends routine hypoglycemia review and CGM for older adults with type 1 diabetes and for type 2 diabetes treated with insulin. It also supports AID when ability and support permit.

The cited technology evidence includes small randomized and crossover studies plus Medicare real-world data. Reported findings include a 27 percent increase in TIR over 12 weeks in an older type 2 diabetes AID trial, less hypoglycemia in the 82-participant AIDE T1D crossover study, 91 percent AID selection in its extension, and a 10 percent TIR increase in a 4,243-person Medicare analysis. These are chapter-level reports of cited studies, not independently verified primary results.

Treatment goals and cardiovascular risk

Recommendations 13.7a through 13.10 and Table 13.2 make health status, frailty, cognition, function, hypoglycemia, treatment burden, and life expectancy part of target selection. Healthy older adults receive lower glycemic targets. Complex health supports less stringent targets. Very complex health prioritizes avoidance of hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia.

The chapter generally targets blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg when safe and allows a more relaxed target such as below 140/90 mmHg in poor health or when adverse-effect risk is high. It applies time-to-benefit reasoning to lipids and antiplatelet therapy and cites 2.5 years as a statin time-to-benefit estimate.

Lifestyle management

The chapter recommends at least 0.8 g/kg/day of protein, with individualized higher intake when needed to regain lean mass and function. Aerobic, weight-bearing, resistance, balance, and flexibility work are used according to ability and safety. For older adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity who can exercise safely, it supports intensive lifestyle intervention with weight loss such as 5 to 7 percent.

Look AHEAD provides much of the cited lifestyle evidence but excluded low-functioning people and required the ability to complete maximal exercise testing. It did not meet its primary cardiovascular event outcome, yet the intervention improved several metabolic and functional measures. That selection limits direct transfer to frailer adults.

Pharmacologic therapy

The core medication rules are to prefer low-hypoglycemia therapy, deintensify when harms or burden exceed benefits, simplify complex insulin plans, preserve cardiorenal risk-reduction therapy when indicated, and account for cost. Metformin is presented as low risk for hypoglycemia with use down to eGFR 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 under the chapter's cautions. Glyburide should be avoided in older adults because of hypoglycemia risk. DPP-4 inhibitors have low hypoglycemia risk but weaker glucose lowering and no comparable cardiorenal benefit.

GLP-1-based therapies and SGLT2 inhibitors retain cardiovascular and kidney roles, but the chapter adds practical geriatric cautions. GLP-1-based therapy can worsen gastrointestinal symptoms or unwanted weight loss. SGLT2 inhibitors can contribute to volume depletion, genitourinary adverse effects, increased urine volume, and rare euglycemic DKA. Insulin requires visual, motor, cognitive, and caregiver capacity. Simplification can reduce hypoglycemia and distress without worsening glycemic outcomes in cited studies.

Post-acute and long-term care

PALTC care requires staff diabetes training, device competence, individualized goals, and facility policies for hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia. Irregular meals, undernutrition, swallowing problems, renal dysfunction, polypharmacy, and dependence on staff all affect medication safety.

The chapter proposes an alert system with immediate notification for glucose below 70 mg/dL without delaying treatment, and prompt notification for repeated or persistent hyperglycemia. It also discusses CGM studies in long-term care, including a 65-resident observational report and a randomized trial using real-time CGM for up to 60 days.

End-of-life care

End-of-life diabetes care shifts from long-term risk reduction to comfort, prevention of distressing hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia, dehydration prevention, quality of life, and dignity. The chapter supports reducing testing and treatment intensity when those actions no longer improve symptoms or comfort.

It distinguishes stable, organ-failure, and actively dying states. Basal insulin should not be stopped in type 1 diabetes during organ failure because of DKA risk. For a dying person with type 2 diabetes and no oral intake, stopping all diabetes medication may be reasonable. For type 1 diabetes in the actively dying phase, the chapter notes no consensus but describes a small basal insulin dose as one way to prevent acute hyperglycemic complications.

Figure 13.3. Insulin-simplification algorithm

Start: optimize technology use and caregiver engagement.

Basal insulin
- Move bedtime basal insulin to morning.
- Fasting goal: 90–150 mg/dL, adjusted for overall health and goals.
- If at least 50% of fasting values exceed goal, increase basal insulin by at least 2 units.
- If more than 2 fasting values per week are below 80 mg/dL, decrease basal insulin by at least 2 units.

Prandial insulin
- Above 10 units per dose: reduce by 50%, add noninsulin therapy, then titrate toward stopping prandial insulin.
- At or below 10 units per dose: stop prandial insulin and add noninsulin therapy.

Premixed insulin
- Use 70% of the total premixed dose as morning basal insulin.

Example correction scale
- Premeal glucose above 250 mg/dL: 2 units short- or rapid-acting insulin.
- Premeal glucose above 350 mg/dL: 4 units short- or rapid-acting insulin.
- Stop the correction scale when it is no longer needed daily.

Main strengths

  • Links glycemic targets to cognition, function, frailty, life expectancy, and treatment burden.
  • Includes concrete screening tools, numeric targets, medication cautions, and two treatment workflows.
  • Addresses both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, diabetes technology, PALTC, and palliative care.

Main limitations

  • The full ADA evidence-grading method and guideline-development process are outside this PDF.
  • Some technology and lifestyle studies in older adults are small or enroll selected participants with relatively good baseline function or glycemic control.
  • The chapter summarizes 194 cited sources. This run did not independently verify those primary publications.

Practice takeaways

  • Assess cognition, function, frailty, support, treatment burden, hypoglycemia, and polypharmacy before setting targets.
  • Use less stringent glycemic targets as health complexity rises, and stop relying on A1C in very complex or poor health when symptom avoidance is the useful goal.
  • Prefer low-hypoglycemia therapy. Simplify or deintensify when the treatment plan is unsafe, too complex, or unlikely to provide benefit within the individual's life expectancy.
  • Do not remove cardiorenal treatment value from the decision solely because a patient is older. Balance it against frailty, adverse effects, cost, function, and time to benefit.
  • Use CGM and AID when the individual or care partners can use them safely. Reassess technology when cognition, function, or support changes.
  • In PALTC, pair treatment with staff training, food-intake awareness, device competence, and an explicit dysglycemia notification policy.
  • At the end of life, make comfort and symptom prevention the treatment endpoints. Reduce testing and medication burden when they no longer improve those endpoints.
