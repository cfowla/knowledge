One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In HART patients receiving rt-PA plus intravenous heparin, inadequate early aPTT prolongation was associated with substantially lower infarct-related coronary artery patency at approximately 18 hours. The association showed a threshold/dose-response pattern across aPTT categories, persisted after multivariable adjustment, and was not seen for day-7 patency. Overall hemorrhage did not track with higher aPTT, although access-site bleeding was more frequent when the 8-hour aPTT exceeded 100 seconds.

The study leveraged a prospective randomized parent trial but the key aPTT-patency question was observational within the heparin arm. The heparin regimen was a 5,000-IU intravenous bolus followed by 1,000 IU/h, titrated to 1.5-2 times control, started with a 100-mg rt-PA regimen. Coronary angiography at a mean of about 18 hours was read in a blinded core laboratory. Mean 8-hour aPTT was 81 +/- 4 seconds in patients with an open artery versus 54 +/- 9 seconds in those with a closed artery (p<0.02). Only 5/11 (45%) patients with aPTT <45 seconds at both 8 and 12 hours had a patent artery, compared with 54/61 (88%) with values >=45 seconds at both times and 37/39 (95%) with values >60 seconds at both times.

The principal evidentiary constraint is causal interpretation: patients were not randomized to aPTT strata, reocclusion was not directly measured, and the closed-artery group with complete aPTT data was small (n=16). The paper therefore strongly supports an association between early anticoagulation intensity and short-term patency in this historical rt-PA setting, but it does not establish an optimal modern heparin regimen or therapeutic aPTT target.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors tested whether coronary patency after rt-PA was sustained by effective anticoagulation. Serial aPTT values in heparin recipients were related to infarct-related artery patency at approximately 18 hours and to repeat angiography at 7 days, with hemorrhage examined as the principal safety outcome.

Early patency tracked strongly with aPTT: mean 8-hour aPTT was 81 +/- 4 seconds with an open artery versus 54 +/- 9 seconds with a closed artery. Patency was 45% when both 8- and 12-hour aPTTs were <45 seconds, 88% when both were >=45 seconds, and 95% when both were >60 seconds. Day-7 patency was not related to ongoing aPTT, and excess anticoagulation did not generally predict hemorrhage except for more access-site bleeding at 8 hours with aPTT >100 seconds.

Introduction

The paper frames adjunctive anticoagulation after thrombolysis as a mechanism for preserving reperfusion after fibrin-specific thrombolysis. The specific question is narrower than the parent HART comparison of heparin versus low-dose aspirin: whether the degree of systemic anticoagulation among heparin recipients is associated with subsequent infarct-related artery patency.

Methods

HART involved three clinical centers, eight hospitals, and 24 investigators. Eligible patients presented within 6 hours of chest-pain onset with >=0.1 mV ST elevation in two contiguous leads and without specified bleeding, neurologic, surgical, pregnancy, anticoagulant, or major medical exclusions. All received 100 mg rt-PA; adjunctive randomization was aspirin 80 mg/day versus intravenous heparin. This analysis focused on the heparin group: 5,000 IU bolus, then 1,000 IU/h adjusted to aPTT 1.5-2 times control. The infusion was reduced 50% for 2-3 hours before catheterization.

Patency was assessed 7-24 hours after rt-PA (mean about 18 hours) and on day 7; TIMI 2-3 was open and TIMI 0-1 closed. Angiograms were read by a single blinded core-laboratory observer. aPTT was measured at baseline, 8 and 12 hours, daily, and 3 hours after heparin-rate changes. Statistical testing used chi-square/Fisher exact tests, two-tailed t tests, and multivariable logistic regression.

Results

Of 205 HART participants, 106 were assigned heparin. One hundred had analyzable approximately 18-hour angiograms and 94 had complete baseline, 8-hour, and 12-hour aPTT values. In heparin recipients, mean aPTT was 76 +/- 4 seconds at 8 hours and 73 +/- 4 seconds at 12 hours (p<0.001 versus baseline). At 8 hours, open-artery patients had substantially higher aPTT than closed-artery patients; by 12 and 23 hours the mean differences were not statistically significant. Persistent low aPTT was concentrated among closed arteries, and the aPTT-patency relation remained significant after adjustment for age, gender, clinical site, time to angiography, and time to treatment (p=0.025).

Among 60 patients with an initially patent artery who underwent repeat day-7 angiography, 53 remained open and 7 had closed; mean aPTT and the frequency of subtherapeutic measurements did not differ materially between those groups. Five patients had puncture-site bleeding and nine had nonaccess-related hemorrhage. Overall bleeding was not associated with higher aPTT, but at 8 hours access-site hemorrhage occurred in 4/22 patients with aPTT >100 seconds versus 2/78 with values <=100 seconds (p=0.02); the relation was not apparent at 12 hours.

Discussion and conclusions

The authors interpret the short-term association as evidence that therapeutic heparinization helps preserve patency after rt-PA. They explicitly acknowledge that HART did not measure reocclusion directly and instead infer rethrombosis from reduced 18-hour patency among patients with low aPTT, supported by prior literature. They also note that no alternative heparin regimens or aPTT targets were prospectively compared and that relatively few patients had persistently subtherapeutic values.

The discussion emphasizes that 25% of heparin recipients remained below 1.5 times control at 8 hours and 21% at 12 hours despite the protocol regimen. The heparin arm did not receive aspirin, limiting inference about combined antiplatelet-anticoagulant therapy. The final claim is that vigorous heparin anticoagulation is an important adjunct to fibrin-specific thrombolytic therapy.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for the heparin deep dive as historical mechanistic/adjunctive-therapy evidence. Best use: understanding the early aPTT-patency association after rt-PA. Do not use this paper alone to set a modern heparin dose, aPTT target, thrombolysis protocol, or bleeding threshold.

Validity

  • Strengths: protocolized rt-PA/heparin exposure; repeated aPTT measurements; angiographic patency measured objectively; blinded core-laboratory interpretation; clear threshold analyses; multivariable adjustment.
  • Principal bias risk: anticoagulation intensity is an achieved post-treatment exposure, not a randomized assignment. Factors influencing heparin response or treatment adjustment could also relate to patency.
  • Precision: several key strata are small (11 patients persistently <45 s; 16 closed arteries with complete serial aPTT), and confidence intervals are not reported.
  • Endpoint: 18-hour patency is clinically relevant to reperfusion maintenance but is not direct observation of reocclusion, and day-7 patency was available only in a selected repeat-angiography subset.

Source claims versus appraisal

Source-supported: greater early aPTT prolongation was associated with higher approximately 18-hour infarct-related artery patency; the association persisted after the authors' multivariable adjustment; day-7 aPTT did not distinguish patent from closed arteries; overall hemorrhage did not rise clearly with higher aPTT, apart from an 8-hour access-site signal above 100 seconds.
Appraisal boundary: the data do not prove that forcing a patient above a specific aPTT threshold causes patency, do not directly establish reocclusion prevention, and do not validate the paper's thresholds as current therapeutic targets.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Preserve for synthesisThe early post-thrombolysis interval is the key exposure window in this paper; 8- and 12-hour aPTT values should remain distinct in downstream synthesis rather than being collapsed into a single generic "therapeutic heparin" label.
Useful thresholds45 s, 60 s, and 100 s are source-specific analytic thresholds. Preserve them as historical study thresholds with provenance, not as decontextualized dosing rules.
Safety signalThe only clear high-aPTT safety association was access-site hemorrhage at 8 hours when aPTT exceeded 100 s; overall hemorrhage did not show a clear increase.
