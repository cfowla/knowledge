One-page synthesis

Bottom line: DAX use was associated with a short-term reduction in pharmacists' EHR note time, but the study is small, single-system, observational, and underpowered for productivity or patient-outcome claims. Best use: a practical implementation signal for ambulatory care pharmacy AI-scribe pilots.
Core thesis

Ambient AI documentation can reduce clinical pharmacist documentation burden in ambulatory care workflows that resemble primary care documentation and ordering workflows.

Main empirical signal

Nine DAX users had a 31.17% relative reduction in median minutes spent in notes per day over the 2-month pre/post window: 38.5 to 26.5 minutes, p=0.04.

Main caveat

The study cannot establish causality or durable productivity gain because users volunteered, sample size was very small, follow-up was short, and nonusers were a descriptive comparator rather than a formal control group.

Section condensation

Abstract

The study addresses EHR documentation burden among ambulatory care clinical pharmacists and tests whether an ambient AI documentation tool can reduce note-writing time. The authors compare 2 months before and after DAX implementation for pharmacist users, with nonusers evaluated over the same calendar windows for context.

The reported primary signal is a statistically significant reduction in median time spent documenting notes per day among 9 DAX users: 38.5 to 26.5 minutes, p=0.04. Visits per 1.0 FTE increased by about 33/month in DAX users and 17/month in nonusers, but these productivity changes were not statistically significant.

Background

The background frames clinical pharmacy documentation as a major ambulatory workload and draws analogy to primary care providers, where EHR time and documentation burden are linked to inefficiency and burnout. Ambient AI scribes are described as tools that listen to clinician-patient conversations and generate draft notes for clinician review.

The local practice setting matters: pharmacists at the FQHC conduct comprehensive medication management under collaborative practice agreements, sign prescriptions under approving physicians' names, order labs, manage chronic conditions, complete refill work, and handle inbasket tasks. This makes the setting a plausible test case for whether physician-facing ambient documentation tools translate to pharmacist workflows.

Methods

This was a retrospective observational pre-post study. DAX was implemented on a rolling basis, with pharmacists volunteering for access but implementation order assigned by a randomized waitlist lottery. The main analytic window was the 2 months immediately before and after each user's implementation date, chosen because it was the longest consistent period available across users at analysis.

EHR efficiency data came from Epic Signal; DAX usage frequency came from a custom Power BI report; productivity data came from standardized monthly team reports. Months with less than 80% of expected scheduled days and pharmacists without usable data were excluded. Time-based workflow metrics were analyzed with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and productivity metrics were analyzed with paired t-tests after normalization to 1.0 FTE.

Results

Of 15 pharmacists, 10 were DAX users and 5 were nonusers; 9 DAX users and 4 nonusers were included. Baseline characteristics were similar on mean FTE and visits/month per 1.0 FTE. DAX users had baseline median note time/day of 38.5 minutes compared with 44.75 among nonusers; average DAX use covered 46.23% of encounters in the 2-month postimplementation window.

The only statistically significant primary finding was reduced median minutes in notes/day among DAX users: -12 minutes/day, -31.17%, p=0.04. Time in notes per appointment decreased from 4.75 to 4.10 minutes but was not significant. Visits/month per 1.0 FTE rose from 181.29 to 214.30 among DAX users, p=0.07, and from 180.95 to 198.08 among nonusers, p=0.08.

Discussion

The authors interpret the 2-month reduction in note time as evidence that ambient listening technology can improve documentation efficiency for clinical pharmacists. They also suggest the nonsignificant increase in visits may represent clinically meaningful capacity in underserved settings if replicated and scaled.

The discussion appropriately warns about hallucinations, transcription errors, omissions, misinterpretation, and inaccurate summarization. It notes that all users were required to review and edit AI-generated notes before sign-off. The authors also emphasize that inbasket and intervention metrics fluctuate with learners, message volume, patient volume, and administrative duties, limiting inference from the short window.

Limitations

The main limitations are small sample size, single FQHC setting, short 2-month observation window, volunteer-user selection bias, low early utilization, and lack of formal causal comparison against nonusers. The randomized waitlist timing partially mitigates but does not remove selection bias because the decision to volunteer for DAX was not randomized.

Longer 3- and 6-month analyses did not show significant findings, partly because sample sizes fell to 7 and 6 DAX users, respectively. The study also did not assess patient outcomes, note accuracy, clinician burnout, patient satisfaction, cost-benefit, privacy/security performance, or downstream safety events.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that DAX use reduced note documentation time and may support increased clinical capacity in ambulatory care pharmacy. They position the work as early implementation evidence showing feasibility and measurable workflow impact.

The conclusion is reasonable for feasibility and short-term workflow signal, but stronger claims about capacity, outcomes, or broad deployment require larger multicenter designs, longer follow-up, stronger control methods, and direct measurement of note quality, safety, cost, and patient/provider experience.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. This is highly relevant for clinical pharmacy AI documentation workflows and worth preserving, but the evidence is too small and local to support broad deployment claims without additional controls and monitoring.
8.5 /10
Relevance

Directly matches clinical pharmacy workflow, ambulatory documentation burden, EHR efficiency, and AI-scribe implementation interests. Evidence basis: pharmacist-specific DAX implementation in an FQHC. Limiting factor: ambulatory FQHC only. Would rise with inpatient, specialty pharmacy, and health-system replication.

7.5 /10
Novelty

Novel because prior ambient AI documentation literature largely centered physicians and advanced practice providers, not pharmacists. Evidence basis: explicit pharmacist cohort and pharmacy workflow framing. Limiting factor: tool and outcome design are not technically novel. Would rise with deeper pharmacist-specific note-quality or workflow redesign analysis.

4.5 /10
Method strength

Useful real-world pre-post design, but small sample, volunteer uptake, short window, and descriptive comparator substantially limit causal inference. Evidence basis: 9 DAX users, 4 nonusers, 2-month main window, post hoc longer windows. Limiting factor: nonrandomized adoption and underpowering. Would rise with randomized stepped-wedge or difference-in-differences design.

4.5 /10
Evidence strength

The note-time reduction is supported, but productivity, capacity, and clinical-benefit claims remain hypothesis-generating. Evidence basis: statistically significant 2-month notes/day change with nonsignificant productivity outcomes and nonsignificant longer windows. Limiting factor: no uncertainty beyond p values and limited sample. Would rise with confidence intervals, power planning, and patient-centered outcomes.

3.5 /10
External validity

Generalizability is narrow because the study occurred in one FQHC with a specific collaborative-practice pharmacy model and one commercial ambient AI product. Evidence basis: single health center system, 10 clinics, localized workflow. Limiting factor: setting-specific staffing, reimbursement, patient complexity, EHR configuration, and team culture. Would rise with multicenter replication.

6.5 /10
Implementation value

Good for pilot planning because it identifies feasible metrics, early uptake issues, and governance need for pharmacist review. Evidence basis: Epic Signal, Power BI usage capture, FTE-normalized productivity, and explicit note review requirement. Limiting factor: sparse operational detail on training, consent, note auditing, privacy, cost, and monitoring. Would rise with implementation toolkit and quality/safety dashboard.

Main strengths

  • Addresses a specific evidence gap: ambient AI documentation for clinical pharmacists rather than only physicians or advanced practice providers.
  • Uses real-world EHR-derived efficiency metrics rather than only self-report.
  • Normalizes productivity metrics to 1.0 FTE, important because pharmacists had variable direct patient-care FTE.
  • Includes contemporaneous nonuser context and longer post hoc windows, which make the interpretation more cautious.
  • Explicitly acknowledges generative AI documentation risks and requires clinician review before sign-off.

Main limitations

  • Very small analytic sample: 9 DAX users and 4 nonusers.
  • Volunteer-user selection bias: willingness to use DAX may correlate with documentation burden, technology comfort, productivity goals, or workflow style.
  • Nonuser comparison was contextual rather than a causal comparator.
  • Short primary follow-up; longer 3- and 6-month analyses were smaller and nonsignificant.
  • No direct assessment of note accuracy, hallucination rate, omission rate, patient outcomes, patient satisfaction, pharmacist burnout, cost, billing, or compliance.
  • Mean DAX usage was only 46.23% of encounters during the first 2 months, so exposure was partial and early-adoption dependent.

Implementation / practice takeaways

What can change now

Use the paper to justify a monitored pilot or business case for ambient AI documentation in ambulatory care pharmacy, especially where pharmacists perform comprehensive medication management and write encounter notes.

What should not change yet

Do not expand staffing assumptions, appointment templates, reimbursement models, or productivity targets based on this study alone.

Minimum pilot metrics

Notes/day, notes/appointment, pajama-time if available, clinical review time, visits/FTE, intervention volume, inbasket volume, DAX-use percentage, note edit burden, error/omission rate, and pharmacist/patient satisfaction.

Safety controls

Require pharmacist review before sign-off; audit a sample of AI-generated notes; track hallucinations, medication-list errors, dosing/frequency omissions, assessment-plan mismatches, and patient consent/refusal.

Governance needs

Define consent workflow, storage/transcription handling, privacy review, vendor risk assessment, downtime procedure, documentation ownership, escalation path, and periodic quality reporting.

Research next step

Run a stepped-wedge or difference-in-differences multicenter study with longer follow-up, note-quality adjudication, cost analysis, burnout measures, and clinical outcomes such as A1c or blood pressure control where appropriate.

Local pharmacy implementation checklist

  • Start with a small cohort of pharmacists with high documentation burden and comparable visit types.
  • Collect at least 8-12 weeks of preimplementation EHR and productivity baseline data.
  • Predefine whether success means less documentation time, more access, less after-hours work, improved note quality, or lower burnout.
  • Separate efficiency endpoints from clinical quality endpoints; do not let a time-saving endpoint imply clinical benefit.
  • Track tool utilization by encounter type because low utilization can obscure true effect and identify where the tool does not fit.
  • Build a pharmacist-specific note-quality rubric that checks medication reconciliation, indication, dose, route, frequency, monitoring, labs, assessment, plan, follow-up, and patient counseling.
