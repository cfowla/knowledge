One-page synthesis

Core finding: In 131 obese adults treated with an institutional anti-Xa-guided UFH protocol, patients who achieved two consecutive therapeutic anti-Xa levels did so at similar mean times whether the infusion was dosed by TBW or ABW (29.4 vs 27.6 h; p=0.93). Major bleeding, overt bleeding, and death also did not differ statistically between the dosing-weight cohorts, but the study was not designed or powered to establish equivalence or noninferiority.

The study addresses a practical dosing problem: UFH distribution does not scale linearly with adipose mass, yet weight-based nomograms are standard and there is limited evidence on which dosing weight should be used in obesity. Eligible adults weighed at least 100 kg and had BMI at least 30 kg/m². The institutional protocol targeted anti-Xa 0.3-0.7 units/mL, started at 80 units/kg bolus plus 18 units/kg/h infusion, and recommended ABW when weight exceeded 125 kg. The primary endpoint was time to the second of two consecutive therapeutic anti-Xa values.

Interpretation is constrained by confounding by indication/protocol assignment: the ABW group was substantially heavier (151.1 vs 127.4 kg) with higher BMI (47.4 vs 41.5 kg/m²), while the TBW group was older and used warfarin more often. Only 42/131 patients received the protocol-specified bolus. The retrospective, single-center design, large exclusion burden, lack of adjusted comparative modeling, small numbers of bleeding events, and internal reporting inconsistencies mean the paper supports feasibility/similarity within this protocol more strongly than it supports interchangeable dosing strategies.

Internal source inconsistencies: The flow statement reports 359 reviewed and exclusion categories totaling 221, yet 131 were analyzed, leaving 7 patients unaccounted for unless categories overlapped or another exclusion was omitted. For major bleeding across TBW categories, the Results text reports p=0.26, Table 4 p=0.94, and Discussion p=0.9362.

Section condensation

Abstract and highlights

The abstract frames the evidence gap around intravenous UFH dosing in obesity and reports similar time to two consecutive therapeutic anti-Xa levels for TBW versus ABW dosing (29.4 vs 27.6 h; reported 95% CI -4.63 to 8.11; p=0.93). It also reports no statistical difference in major bleeding, overt bleeding, or death between dosing-weight groups.

The highlights emphasize that UFH requirements do not rise linearly with total body weight because adipose tissue is less vascular, and they present TBW and ABW as producing similar efficacy and safety in this cohort. The final highlight explicitly calls for future work on bolus dosing, BMI thresholds for ABW, and the infusion rate used with TBW.

Background

The authors describe obesity as a dosing challenge because UFH volume of distribution and requirements may not scale directly with body mass. Prior studies were mixed: some suggested higher bleeding with TBW in obese patients, while others found no difference. The authors also stress that therapeutic anticoagulation should be achieved promptly and maintained, and that two consecutive therapeutic values are a more stringent marker than one isolated value.

The institution already recommended ABW for patients above 125 kg, providing the natural clinical contrast evaluated in this retrospective cohort. The paper therefore tests the local protocol's real-world performance rather than prospectively assigning a dosing-weight strategy.

Methods

The investigators reviewed EHR data from January 2013 through December 2015. Inclusion required age ≥18 years, weight ≥100 kg, BMI ≥30 kg/m², and intravenous UFH. Exclusions were alternative UFH protocol, less than 24 h on the standard protocol, or inadequate protocol compliance. ABW was calculated as IBW + 0.4(TBW-IBW), and compliance allowed less than 25% deviation from protocol elements.

The primary outcome was time from infusion start to the second consecutive anti-Xa level within 0.3-0.7 units/mL. Secondary efficacy analyses examined weight categories, BMI categories, and bolus receipt. Overt bleeding was visible documented bleeding; major bleeding required overt bleeding plus a ≥3 g/dL hemoglobin fall, ≥2 units PRBC transfusion, inotrope-requiring hypotension, or intracranial symptoms. Analyses included Fisher exact/chi-square/trend tests, t tests or Mann-Whitney tests, log-rank testing, and one-way ANOVA, with p<0.05 as the threshold.

Results

Of 131 analyzed patients, 109 reached two consecutive therapeutic anti-Xa levels within 96 h. Mean time was 29.4 h for TBW dosing and 27.6 h for ABW dosing (p=0.93); among patients ≥125 kg it was 30.2 h with TBW versus 27.1 h with ABW (p=0.65). Therapeutic anti-Xa proportions were nearly identical (62.6% vs 62.4%), but TBW dosing yielded a larger supratherapeutic fraction (21.7% vs 13.1%) and a smaller subtherapeutic fraction (15.7% vs 24.5%) across all measured levels.

Overall overt bleeding was 11.5%, major bleeding 7.6%, and death 4.6%. Major bleeding was 10.4% with TBW versus 4.7% with ABW (p=0.61), overt bleeding 11.9% versus 10.9% (p=1.00), and death 4.5% versus 4.7% (p=1.00). Only 32.1% received the protocol-recommended initial bolus; the no-bolus group reached the endpoint faster by the paper's categorical comparison (p=0.02), an observational finding vulnerable to confounding and protocol-selection effects.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret their results as supporting either TBW or ABW in obese patients, noting similar time to therapeutic anti-Xa and no detected safety difference. They contextualize their findings against prior aPTT- and anti-Xa-based studies and argue that anti-Xa monitoring may have operational advantages over aPTT, while acknowledging that the literature in obese patients is limited.

The authors explicitly cite retrospective design, single-center setting, protocol-specific practice, exclusion for noncompliance, and limited sample size. They recommend future study of initial bolus use, BMI classification for ABW, and TBW infusion rates. Their conclusion is appropriately framed as added insight, but the wording that either strategy is similarly safe/effective is stronger than the design can establish.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Highly relevant to UFH dosing in obesity and anti-Xa monitoring, useful for hypothesis generation and protocol design context, but insufficient as a standalone basis to declare TBW and ABW interchangeable.

Validity

Selection and confounding: Assignment to TBW versus ABW was not randomized and was related to body weight because the protocol recommended ABW above 125 kg. The groups therefore differed substantially in weight and BMI; age and warfarin use also differed. Unadjusted comparisons cannot fully separate dosing-weight effects from these baseline differences.

Outcome and statistical interpretation: The primary endpoint is a laboratory-process endpoint rather than a patient-centered thrombotic endpoint. Two consecutive therapeutic anti-Xa levels are clinically relevant for dosing stability, but absence of a detected difference is not evidence of equivalence. Safety event numbers were small, and the major-bleeding point estimate numerically favored ABW while remaining statistically imprecise.

Additional source-quality concerns

The reported patient-flow arithmetic does not reconcile cleanly: 359 reviewed minus the 221 listed exclusions would leave 138, not 131. The paper does not explain the remaining seven in the visible flow narrative. The weight-category major-bleeding p value is also inconsistent across Results, Table 4, and Discussion. These are not necessarily outcome-changing, but they reduce confidence in exact secondary-result reuse.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use nowUse this paper as supporting observational evidence that both TBW- and ABW-based starts can be made workable inside an anti-Xa-guided titration system.
Do not overclaimDo not treat p=0.93 as proof that TBW and ABW are equivalent, or the safety p values as proof of equal bleeding risk.
Protocol designTable 1 is highly reusable as an example of explicit anti-Xa titration logic, including hold times and rate adjustments.
Bolus findingDo not infer that omitting the bolus is beneficial. Bolus receipt was nonrandom, protocol adherence was incomplete, and the analysis is vulnerable to selection/confounding.
Monitoring signalThe therapeutic fraction was similar between groups, while TBW produced more supratherapeutic and fewer subtherapeutic levels; this distribution may be more informative than the binary primary comparison for protocol refinement.
Read nextThe primary studies cited for obese UFH dosing and anti-Xa monitoring are the appropriate next layer if building a dosing synthesis or evidence graph.
Bottom line: Useful, directly relevant protocol evidence; moderate confidence in the reported primary timing result, lower confidence in causal or safety equivalence claims. Best used as one node in a broader UFH-obesity evidence synthesis.
