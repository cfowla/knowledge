Section condensation

Abstract

The study asks whether bedside POCT-aPTT can support UFH monitoring in ICU patients relative to laboratory aPTT and anti-Xa. The authors emphasize that UFH monitoring is difficult in critical illness because standard coagulation tests are influenced by inflammatory and hemostatic disturbances, while laboratory turnaround can delay dose changes.

Across the analyzed cohort, POCT-aPTT correlated more closely with laboratory aPTT than with anti-Xa, but categorical agreement was only slight. POCT produced results within minutes, creating a large workflow-speed advantage. The authors conclude that the speed benefit is insufficient to justify applying existing laboratory-aPTT nomograms to POCT values.

1. Introduction

The introduction frames UFH as useful in ICU care because of its short half-life and reversibility but notes the absence of a universally reliable monitoring method in critically ill patients. It distinguishes aPTT, which is sensitive to numerous biological and reagent effects, from anti-Xa, which more directly reflects heparin activity but also has analytical limitations and does not inherently solve workflow delay.

The rationale for POCT is operational: a bedside result could support faster titration. The study therefore compares a point-of-care aPTT with both laboratory aPTT and anti-Xa, while explicitly examining confounding biological variables and the real timing from blood collection to result use and dose adjustment.

2. Methods

This monocentric prospective longitudinal study enrolled adult ICU patients expected to receive IV UFH for at least 72 hours between July 2019 and July 2020. Key exclusions included previous anticoagulation, thrombolytic therapy, contraindication to heparin or HIT, and COVID-19. Follow-up ended with ICU discharge, completion of CVVH/ECMO support, or 15 days. The study was exploratory and did not use a formal sample-size calculation.

UFH was adjusted using local laboratory-aPTT nomograms adapted from published protocols. The low therapeutic target was an aPTT ratio of 1.5–2.0 and the high target 2.0–2.5. At each monitoring episode, POCT-aPTT was measured from fresh whole blood while laboratory aPTT and anti-Xa were measured from citrated plasma before UFH dose adjustment. Anti-Xa 0.3–0.7 IU/mL was divided into low (0.3–0.5) and high (0.5–0.7) target bands. Analyses included Spearman correlation, Cohen kappa for categorical agreement, and linear mixed-effects models for biological confounders.

3.1. Study population

Forty-five patients were screened, 40 entered the study after five ineligibilities, and 35 were analyzed after five further exclusions for missing/fragmented monitoring. Baseline POCT-aPTT before UFH was available in 29 (82.9%). Median age was 64.7 years (IQR 56.9–70.7), median weight 76.5 kg (65.0–94.5), and median inclusion duration 6.0 days (5.0–11.2).

Twenty-three patients used the low therapeutic range, eight the high range, and four switched ranges. Median time to the desired therapeutic range was 29.1 hours (15.4–37.6). Major bleeding occurred in 6 patients and minor bleeding in 6; 7 patients died in ICU. These are descriptive cohort outcomes, not comparative effects of a monitoring strategy. No thrombotic events were recorded.

3.2. Assay relationships and agreement

Across all 35 patients, POCT-aPTT correlated strongly with laboratory aPTT (795 pairs; rₛ 0.77, 95% CI 0.74–0.79) and weakly with anti-Xa (729 pairs; rₛ 0.46, 95% CI 0.40–0.52); both p<0.0001. Excluding ECMO/CVVH patients did not erase the pattern: rₛ 0.79 for POCT versus lab aPTT and 0.59 for POCT versus anti-Xa. Among 29 patients with baseline values, ratio-based correlations were strongest in the high therapeutic range for POCT versus lab aPTT (rₛ 0.74) and weakest in the low range for POCT versus anti-Xa (rₛ 0.24).

Therapeutic-category agreement remained slight despite these correlations. Kappa for POCT/lab-aPTT ratios was 0.20 in the low range and 0.13 in the high range; for POCT-ratio/anti-Xa it was 0.11 and 0.08, respectively. This distinction between correlation and clinically usable agreement is the central analytic finding.

3.3. Confounding factors

Individual mixed-effects models found that CRP, fibrinogen, factors VIII, XI and XII, antithrombin, and lupus-anticoagulant positivity modified the POCT/lab-aPTT relationship. Higher CRP and lupus-anticoagulant positivity strengthened correlation, whereas higher fibrinogen and factors VIII, XI, and XII weakened it. Antithrombin and lupus-anticoagulant positivity also affected systematic differences between assays.

The final multivariable model retained CRP, fibrinogen, factor XII, and lupus-anticoagulant positivity as modifiers. Exact coefficients are located in Supplementary Table S2, which is cited but not embedded in the retrieved PDF; therefore this appraisal does not reconstruct coefficient magnitudes.

3.4. Time tracking

Median collection-to-result turnaround was 50.9 minutes for laboratory aPTT (IQR 38.4–69.1) and 66.3 minutes for anti-Xa (49.0–91.8), with a statistically significant difference (Mann–Whitney U 84473, p<0.0001). Bedside POCT produced a result within 5 minutes.

Workflow delay continued after the laboratory result existed: median time from chart delivery to ICU-staff reading was 32.4 minutes (17.6–60.3). When a dose change was required, the mean additional time after reading was 3.6 minutes (SD 14.2). Median total time from collection to a laboratory-aPTT-based dose adjustment was 92.0 minutes (69.3–121.2).

4. Discussion

The authors interpret POCT-aPTT as analytically closer to laboratory aPTT than anti-Xa, particularly in the high therapeutic range, but emphasize that individual and therapeutic-category disagreement prevents simple substitution. They note that POCT ratios tended to be lower than laboratory ratios when assays disagreed and argue that the manufacturer cutoffs do not define an adequate UFH titration range for this setting. A POCT-specific therapeutic range would need validation.

Strengths include prospective repeated paired measurements and a heterogeneous ICU cohort. Major limitations are the single-center sample of 35 patients, unexplained within- and between-patient variability, unstandardized interoperator POCT variation across shifts, lack of a cohort actually managed by POCT or anti-Xa, frequent subtherapeutic observations with fewer supratherapeutic data, and missing baseline POCT in 17.1%. The speed advantage is therefore an implementation hypothesis rather than demonstrated clinical benefit.

5. Conclusions

The authors conclude that POCT-aPTT can substantially shorten time to a coagulation result but has only slight agreement with laboratory aPTT and anti-Xa therapeutic categories. They do not recommend combining this POCT assay with existing aPTT-based nomograms.

They call for dedicated prospective studies to determine an optimal POCT therapeutic range and to test whether faster results improve time in therapeutic range or reduce complications. The source itself does not answer those clinical-effectiveness questions.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first. For a heparin deep-dive, this is a high-value analytical and workflow paper because it separates correlation from therapeutic-category agreement and quantifies the real delay between sampling and UFH dose adjustment. Do not use it as stand-alone practice guidance.

Relevance

9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses ICU unfractionated-heparin monitoring, assay discordance, therapeutic-category agreement, and turnaround time.

Evidence basis: Prospective paired POCT-aPTT, laboratory aPTT, and anti-Xa measurements plus a detailed local UFH nomogram.

Principal limiting factor: The device, reagents, and local nomogram are specific to this study setting.

Would raise/lower: Higher with direct replication in the intended local workflow; lower if the use case does not involve UFH monitoring or point-of-care coagulation.

Novelty

6/10

Rationale: Adds a longitudinal ICU evaluation of the CoaguChek Pro II aPTT assay during UFH therapy, including repeated measures and timing data.

Evidence basis: The authors describe this as the first longitudinal evaluation of this POCT configuration in critically ill patients receiving UFH.

Principal limiting factor: The broader problem of aPTT/anti-Xa discordance and reagent-dependent aPTT behavior was already established.

Would raise/lower: Higher with externally replicated clinical-effectiveness data; lower if similar device-specific cohorts predating this study are considered equivalent.

Method strength

6/10

Rationale: Prospective simultaneous sampling and repeated-measures modeling strengthen the analytic comparison, but the cohort is small and single-center.

Evidence basis: 35 analyzed patients, 795 POCT/lab-aPTT pairs, 729 POCT/anti-Xa pairs; mixed-effects modeling was used for confounder analysis.

Principal limiting factor: No formal sample-size calculation, missing baseline POCT in 6 patients, and no randomized or independently assigned monitoring strategy.

Would raise/lower: Higher with prespecified power, multicenter enrollment, standardized operator assessment, and randomized or parallel monitoring strategies; lower with evidence of selective measurement or unresolved assay handling problems.

Evidence strength

6/10

Rationale: The study convincingly demonstrates rapid POCT availability and only slight therapeutic-category agreement, but cannot establish improved or worsened clinical outcomes from a POCT monitoring strategy.

Evidence basis: Strong correlations for some assay comparisons coexist with low kappa agreement; median lab-aPTT TAT was 50.9 min and global collection-to-dose-adjustment time 92.0 min, whereas POCT results were available within 5 min.

Principal limiting factor: Bleeding and thrombosis were observed during care but were not causally compared across monitoring strategies.

Would raise/lower: Higher with a prospective strategy trial measuring time in range, bleeding, thrombosis, and protocol adherence; lower if key agreement results fail replication.

External validity

4/10

Rationale: Generalizability is constrained by one Belgian ICU, 35 patients, a specific POCT platform and laboratory assays, and a local aPTT-based nomogram.

Evidence basis: The cohort was clinically heterogeneous and included CVVH and ECMO patients, which improves within-ICU breadth but not cross-site transferability.

Principal limiting factor: Assay sensitivity, therapeutic ranges, workflows, and extracorporeal-circuit mix differ across institutions.

Would raise/lower: Higher with multicenter external validation across different reagents, POCT lots/operators, and UFH populations; lower if local assay calibration materially differs.

Implementation value

8/10

Rationale: The article is highly useful for designing or auditing UFH-monitoring workflows because it quantifies both assay discordance and laboratory delay and shows why lab-aPTT nomograms should not be transplanted to this POCT assay.

Evidence basis: Detailed dosing nomograms, therapeutic-category definitions, paired-assay results, confounder analysis, and TAT decomposition are provided.

Principal limiting factor: The source is an analytical observational study rather than current practice guidance, and POCT-specific therapeutic thresholds were not validated.

Would raise/lower: Higher if paired with current guideline/lab validation evidence and local assay verification; lower if used as a stand-alone dosing standard.

Main strengths

  • Prospective, simultaneous paired assay collection.
  • Large number of repeated measurements relative to patient count.
  • Explicit therapeutic-category agreement analysis rather than correlation alone.
  • Mixed-effects approach for repeated observations and biological modifiers.
  • Direct measurement of multiple workflow timing components.
  • Detailed local UFH nomograms make the operational context interpretable.

Main limitations

  • Single center and only 35 analyzed patients.
  • No formal power calculation.
  • Six patients lacked baseline POCT for ratio/agreement analyses.
  • Interoperator POCT variability was not minimized across shifts.
  • No group was actually managed by POCT or anti-Xa, preventing clinical-effectiveness comparison.
  • Many observations were subtherapeutic; supra-therapeutic behavior is less well characterized.
  • Supplementary model coefficients are unavailable in the retrieved PDF.
Critical appraisal distinction: a high Spearman correlation does not validate substitution into a dose nomogram. The study's low kappa values and nontrivial contradictory-category rates show why assay-specific calibration and clinical validation matter.

High for the paper's reported analytical and workflow results because the full retrieved main article, all main-text figures, and all main-text tables were reconciled. Moderate for detailed confounder-effect magnitudes because Supplementary Tables S1–S3 were not present in the PDF.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use now
Use the paper to support a design rule: do not transplant laboratory-aPTT therapeutic thresholds or dose nomograms to a POCT-aPTT assay without assay-specific validation.
Use for workflow design
Model the whole monitoring loop, not only assay runtime: collection, laboratory processing, chart posting, clinician review, and dose action each add delay.
Use for evidence mapping
Track assay correlation, dose-category agreement, time in therapeutic range, bleeding/thrombosis, confounders, and operator effects as separate evidence domains.

What this should change in a CDS / protocol evidence model

  • Represent assay identity and reagent/platform as part of the monitoring method, not just the label “aPTT.”
  • Keep therapeutic target definitions versioned with the assay/nomogram that validated them.
  • Do not infer clinical interchangeability from correlation; require category agreement or outcome validation for rule substitution.
  • Preserve extracorporeal support and biological modifiers (e.g., CRP, fibrinogen, factor XII, lupus anticoagulant) as context because they can alter assay relationships.
  • Capture turnaround-time provenance across the complete decision loop when comparing monitoring strategies.
What not to overclaim: the study does not demonstrate that POCT-guided UFH dosing is safer, more effective, or superior to anti-Xa or laboratory aPTT monitoring. It demonstrates faster result availability and important non-interchangeability in this cohort.
