One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this retrospective series, patients whose average activated coagulation time (ACT) was at least 180 seconds had faster pain improvement and no recorded delayed responses or treatment failures in the objectively confirmed response-analysis subset, while higher ACT was associated with more minor bleeding but not more major bleeding. The authors therefore argued for more aggressive heparin dosing.

What the source reports. The study included 121 patients with 156 DVT episodes. Continuous IV heparin was used in 153 cases, with an average dose of 21 units/kg/hour. Treatment response was evaluated only in objectively confirmed DVT. Among 33 cases with average ACT ≥180 seconds, there were no delayed responses or treatment failures; among 65 with ACT <180 seconds, 4 (6.2%) had delayed response and 7 (10.8%) had treatment failure. Mean time to response was 2.0 versus 3.1 days (p<0.05). Across all 156 treated cases, any bleeding occurred in 43 (27%), minor bleeding in 33 (21%), and major bleeding in 10 (6%).

Independent appraisal. The numerical signal is internally coherent but causal interpretation is weak. ACT category was achieved rather than randomized, the response endpoint depended heavily on chart-documented pain relief, only 71% of cases had objective diagnostic support, repeated DVT episodes from the same patients contribute to case-level analyses, and long-term post-thrombotic outcomes were not measured. The paper is best treated as historical hypothesis-generating evidence rather than a dosing trial.

High-value point for a heparin deep dive. The paper captures an early clinical argument that “adequate anticoagulation” may matter more than a fixed heparin dose, while simultaneously showing the tradeoff between intensity and minor bleeding. That conceptual tension remains more reusable than the paper’s exact ACT target or printed dosing units.
Source-specific anomaly. The article prints a recommended “loading dose of 50 units/kg/hour” and later describes a planned-study “heparin bolus of 300 units/kg/hour.” Those units are unusual for bolus wording. They are retained here as printed and are not silently corrected.

Section condensation

Introductory rationale

The authors note that heparin had long been used for DVT but that the relation between anticoagulation level, efficacy, and complications remained poorly defined. Their stated purpose was to review local experience and identify factors associated with treatment success and risk.

Material and Methods

Inpatient records from 1977–1978 were reviewed. Eligible cases had active, clinically apparent DVT treated with heparin; asymptomatic DVT and thrombosis caused by malignant venous obstruction were excluded. The hospital’s recommended regimen was printed as a 50 units/kg/hour IV loading dose followed by 25 units/kg/hour continuous infusion, adjusted using ACT measured at least daily to a target of 150–210 seconds (approximately 1.5–2.5 times normal).

Outcome assessment was retrospective. Significant pain relief documented in the chart was used to quantify response. A response taking ≥7 days was “delayed.” Treatment failure was recurrent DVT on heparin, pulmonary embolism on heparin, or persistent pain after at least 1 week of heparin. Treatment-response analysis was limited to cases with objectively confirmed DVT.

Results — cohort and diagnosis

There were 121 patients with 156 DVT episodes; 75 unique patients were men and 46 women, ages 17–80 years (average 50). Multiple episodes occurred in 20% of patients. Previous DVT was the most common reported predisposing condition (73 patients, 47%), followed by surgery (27, 17%), medical illness (16, 10%), leg/pelvic trauma (16, 10%), multisystem trauma (6, 4%), other venous disease (4, 3%), oral contraceptive use (1, 0.6%), and no identified predisposing condition (12, 8%).

Objective diagnostic support was present in 71% of cases: phlebography 49%, iodine-123-fibrinogen scan 19%, Doppler 1%, and autopsy 1%. Three additional patients with DVT plus pulmonary symptoms had pulmonary embolism documented by ventilation-perfusion scanning.

Results — treatment exposure

Continuous IV heparin was used in 153 cases; one received intermittent IV heparin and two full-dose subcutaneous heparin. Monitoring used ACT in 146 cases, partial thromboplastin time in 5, and no coagulation test in 5. Mean heparin dose was 21 units/kg/hour; among those whose average ACT was 150–210 seconds, dose requirements ranged from 9 to 39 units/kg/hour. Average ACT was in target in 103 patients/cases, high in 8, and low in 35 as reported.

Heparin duration ranged from 1–31 days (average 9). Warfarin was begun in 63% of cases, typically between heparin days 1 and 5, and heparin was stopped after the prothrombin time reached twice control or after pain relief in slower responders. Outpatient warfarin was generally continued for 3–6 months when feasible.

Results — efficacy / response

Four delayed responses and seven treatment failures were recorded in the objectively confirmed response-analysis set. All occurred in patients whose average ACT was <180 seconds. The ACT ≥180 group (n=33) had no delayed response or treatment failure; the ACT <180 group (n=65) had 4 delayed responses (6.2%) and 7 treatment failures (10.8%). The paper reports the group difference as statistically significant (p<0.05, chi-square).

Mean time to significant pain relief was 3.1 days with ACT <180 seconds versus 2.0 days with ACT ≥180 seconds (p<0.05, analysis of variance). The source also reports one recurrent DVT and one clinically manifested pulmonary embolism during heparin therapy in the overall 156-episode series.

Results — bleeding and thrombocytopenia

Any major or minor bleeding occurred in 43 cases (27%). Minor bleeding occurred in 33 (21%); major bleeding in 10 (6%). Nine major bleeds required transfusion and one patient had a cerebrovascular accident. No patient died as a result of anticoagulation, but heparin was stopped prematurely in nine patients because of bleeding.

Table I shows more minor bleeding with ACT ≥180 seconds (40% vs 14%) without a corresponding increase in major bleeding (7% vs 6%). Recent operation was the clearest major-bleeding risk signal (50% vs 3%, significant at the 0.05 level). Women and patients ≥70 years had numerically more bleeding in some comparisons, but the narrative says these increases were not significant. Platelet counts were infrequently monitored; two patients were noted to have clinically inconsequential thrombocytopenia on heparin, while a third case was attributed to chemotherapy.

Comments

The authors interpret the ACT-response association as evidence that inadequate anticoagulation permits continued thrombosis. They connect persistent pain to ongoing clotting and argue that continuing pain after 24 hours should raise concern that clotting persists and that more heparin may be required. They also discuss platelet factor 4 as a possible heparin antagonist during active thrombosis and use prior literature to support prolonged oral anticoagulation after discharge.

The authors acknowledge that their study did not specifically examine long-term outcomes. Nonetheless, they propose that more aggressive heparin might prevent clot propagation and postphlebitic sequelae. For most established DVT they favor ACT above 180 seconds, while describing a more cautious risk-benefit approach in elderly postoperative patients. They call for a prospective controlled long-term comparison and state that such a trial was underway.

Summary

The authors summarize the series as showing faster recovery and no recurrent DVT, embolism, or failure to improve among 33 patients with average ACT >180 seconds, compared with seven failures among 65 patients below 180 seconds. They emphasize that major bleeding was not increased above 180 seconds, although minor bleeding was.

The paper closes by advocating higher heparin doses for most DVT patients because the authors believed this produced more rapid and complete recovery.

Published Discussion after the article

This section contains meeting commentary and should not be confused with the study’s own evidence. Bernard W. Thompson agreed that heparin requirements vary and supported ACT monitoring, but criticized the limited rate of objective DVT confirmation. Erich W. Pollak reported a separate experience with more bleeding and treatment failures even at ACT >180 seconds, arguing that propagating thrombosis may continue despite apparent anticoagulation. Jeffrey R. Saffle highlighted heparin-associated thrombocytopenia as an important hazard.

In closing, Kashtan stated that warfarin was generally continued for 3–6 months and agreed that patients should have objective diagnostic confirmation before heparin treatment. These remarks clarify the authors’ practice approach but are commentary rather than additional controlled data.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High relevance to the history of therapeutic heparin monitoring and intensity, but methodologically inadequate as a stand-alone basis for dosing or ACT targets.
Relevance
9/10

Directly examines heparin dose/intensity, anticoagulation measurement, treatment response, and bleeding in DVT.

Evidence basis: core study question and Table I. Limiting factor: historical monitoring context. Would rise: direct linkage to current protocol questions with validated translation.

Novelty
6/10

Historically important in emphasizing achieved anticoagulation rather than a single fixed heparin dose.

Evidence basis: broad dose requirement range and ACT-stratified outcomes. Limiting factor: concept is no longer novel. Would rise: evidence that this paper directly changed practice or seeded a pivotal trial.

Method strength
3/10

Retrospective, single-center, nonrandomized chart review with subjective outcome ascertainment and mixed case/patient denominators.

Evidence basis: methods and response definitions. Limiting factor: confounding and non-independence. Would rise: prospective randomized dosing strategy, blinded outcome assessment, patient-level analysis.

Evidence strength
4/10

Clear counts and consistent direction of association, but no confidence intervals, limited multivariable control, and weak causal identification.

Evidence basis: ACT strata, response times, bleeding table. Limiting factor: achieved ACT is observational. Would rise: randomized comparison with objective thrombus outcomes and prespecified safety analysis.

External validity
3/10

Single 1977–1978 surgical-center cohort with historical diagnostics, ACT monitoring, and treatment practices.

Evidence basis: setting and diagnostic methods. Limiting factor: only 71% objective confirmation and heterogeneity. Would rise: multicenter validation with contemporary diagnosis and monitoring.

Implementation value
5/10

Useful for historical rationale and for generating hypotheses about heparin responsiveness and bleeding tradeoffs, not for direct protocol implementation.

Evidence basis: dose variability, ACT-response relationship, bleeding risk factors. Limiting factor: obsolete/uncertain exact target translation. Would rise: validated bridge from ACT exposure to modern monitoring and clinical outcomes.

Main strengths

  • Reports concrete heparin exposure, monitoring, response, and bleeding data rather than only narrative opinion.
  • Shows large interpatient heparin dose variability even within the same ACT target range.
  • Separates minor from major bleeding and provides a risk-factor table.
  • Explicitly identifies the absence of long-term outcome assessment and calls for a prospective controlled study.

Main limitations

  • Nonrandomized exposure: ACT ≥180 is an achieved laboratory state, not an assigned strategy; confounding by disease severity, heparin responsiveness, clinician behavior, and time is unavoidable.
  • Retrospective outcome ascertainment: pain relief was abstracted from charts and is subjective; treatment failure included persistent pain.
  • Diagnostic verification: only 71% of cases had objective support, and the response subset excludes nonconfirmed cases.
  • Repeated episodes: 121 patients contributed 156 DVT episodes, yet case-level analyses appear to treat episodes as independent.
  • Statistical reporting: p-values are coarse, confidence intervals are absent, and no multivariable model is shown for major confounding factors.
  • Safety ascertainment: platelet counts were infrequently monitored, limiting conclusions about thrombocytopenia.
  • No long-term outcomes: the paper motivates prevention of postphlebitic sequelae but does not measure them.
  • Historical treatment context: exact printed heparin/ACT regimen should not be presumed transferable to current practice.

What would most strengthen the claim?

A prospective randomized comparison of prespecified heparin intensity strategies, objective serial thrombus imaging, standardized bleeding definitions, patient-level handling of repeat events, blinded outcome adjudication, and long-term recurrent VTE/post-thrombotic outcomes.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use this source forHistorical rationale that fixed heparin dosing can produce variable anticoagulant response; early evidence linking lower achieved anticoagulation with poor short-term response; early bleeding-risk observations.
Do not use this source forContemporary heparin bolus/infusion doses, modern monitoring targets, causal claims that ACT ≥180 improves outcomes, or definitive thrombocytopenia risk estimates.
Evidence-graph valueHigh as a historical node linking heparin dose variability → achieved coagulation response → short-term efficacy signal / minor bleeding tradeoff; moderate as a practice node.
Read-next logicFollow the cited monitoring studies and the prospective controlled high-dose study the authors state was underway; prioritize primary trials that randomize heparin intensity or rigorously define therapeutic anticoagulation.
Bottom line. Preserve the paper as historical mechanistic/clinical context, not as a current dosing rule. Its most durable contribution is the observation that heparin requirements were highly variable and that low achieved anticoagulation clustered with short-term treatment failures in this series.
