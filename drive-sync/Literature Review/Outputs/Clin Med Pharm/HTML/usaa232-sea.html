One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Heavy or prolonged rucksack carriage can present as an isolated motor mononeuropathy rather than the more familiar upper-trunk brachial plexopathy. In these three cases, the affected nerves were the spinal accessory nerve and long thoracic nerve, and scapular winging was a prominent clue.

The strongest part of the paper is its clinical pattern recognition. Case 1 had chronic incomplete left spinal accessory neuropathy with lateral winging, reduced spinal accessory CMAP, and mildly reduced trapezius recruitment. Cases 2 and 3 had incomplete long thoracic neuropathies with medial winging and serratus anterior abnormalities on EMG. The longitudinal follow-up is useful: case 2 was pain free and passed a Navy SEAL screener at one year; case 3 returned to unrestricted military duty by 17 months but still had difficulty with weighted overhead presses.

The paper also reviews rucksack design, load distribution, and prevention. Those claims mostly come from prior literature rather than the three cases. The authors themselves note that comparative claims about modern rucksack superiority are largely anecdotal and that more research is needed. The case series therefore supports awareness and differential diagnosis more than it supports a specific prevention device or treatment strategy.

Section condensation

Abstract and introduction

The authors distinguish isolated mononeuropathy from the better-described brachial plexopathy caused by rucksack use. They cite prior military estimates of 29.7 to 53.7 cases per 100,000 personnel per year, while warning that the true incidence is unknown. The stated aim is to show the clinical patterns of spinal accessory and long thoracic neuropathies and to discuss rucksack use and prevention.

The introduction frames prolonged shoulder load, improper use, and heavy loads as risk factors. It also notes that rucksack palsy can resemble neuralgic amyotrophy, thoracic outlet syndrome, cervical radiculopathy, or a space-occupying lesion. These background statements are literature-based, not results generated by this case series.

Case 1: spinal accessory neuropathy

A 29-year-old active duty soldier had about five years of left shoulder and periscapular pain with shrugging weakness during frequent body-armor and rucksack use. Examination showed upper trapezius atrophy and lateral scapular winging. NCS showed a markedly reduced left spinal accessory CMAP relative to the right, and trapezius EMG showed mildly reduced recruitment.

The authors diagnosed chronic incomplete left spinal accessory neuropathy. Ultrasound-guided hydrodissection produced good pain relief. He continued his military occupation, and the report notes no later documented encounters for left shoulder symptoms.

Case 2: long thoracic neuropathy

A 21-year-old Naval Academy midshipman developed right medial scapular winging and push-up weakness after a 13-mile ruck march with an older internal-frame pack loaded to 65 lb. EMG showed spontaneous activity in the right serratus anterior without evidence of right brachial plexopathy.

The authors diagnosed incomplete right long thoracic neuropathy and used physical therapy, dry needling, and relative rest. At five months he could perform 91 push-ups but still noticed disproportionate right-sided fatigue. At one year he was pain free and passed the Navy SEAL screener.

Case 3: long thoracic neuropathy

A 25-year-old Army Ranger developed left shoulder weakness after prolonged rucksack marches. Active flexion and abduction were limited to 90 degrees, passive motion was preserved, and medial scapular winging appeared with forward flexion and wall push-up. Serratus anterior EMG showed spontaneous activity and reduced recruitment, and left long thoracic CMAP was markedly reduced compared with the right.

Follow-up EMG at nine months showed reinnervation and normalized recruitment. At eight months he could perform more than 53 push-ups. By 17 months he had returned to unrestricted duty, although weighted overhead presses remained difficult.

Discussion and conclusion

The discussion explains scapular biomechanics and the expected winging patterns for spinal accessory and long thoracic nerve lesions. It also reviews rucksack frames, hip belts, strap design, and behavioral factors. One cited biomechanical study reported fourfold lower subclavian artery strain and 50% lower skin stress with anatomically modeled straps. A cited 1987 study reported lower rucksack paralysis incidence with framed packs than without frames.

The authors conclude that isolated rucksack-associated mononeuropathy is under-recognized. They recommend considering electrodiagnostic testing when rucksack exposure and scapular winging occur together. They call for education and more research on rucksack design. The paper does not test those prevention recommendations prospectively.

Strengths

Detailed physical examination and electrodiagnostic descriptions, three distinct cases, and longitudinal outcomes extending to 12 to 17 months in two cases.

Main limits

The sample is three selected cases. Exposure intensity was not measured uniformly. There is no comparator group. Treatments differed by case. Recovery may reflect natural history. The prevention discussion depends on cited literature, and the authors note that most comparative claims about modern rucksacks are anecdotal.

Clinical-practice lens

PICO-like framing. Young military personnel with symptoms after rucksack carriage; no standardized intervention or comparator; outcomes were clinical pattern, electrodiagnostic findings, and functional recovery.

Practice translation. Consider isolated long thoracic or spinal accessory neuropathy when scapular winging follows load carriage. EMG/NCS can help localize the lesion. Do not use this case series alone to choose hydrodissection, neurolysis, a specific rucksack, or a specific load threshold.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use now

Add rucksack-associated isolated SAN/LTN neuropathy to the differential when scapular winging appears after load carriage.

Confirm localization

Use examination pattern plus NCS/EMG to distinguish mononeuropathy from brachial plexopathy and other causes.

Do not overclaim

The paper does not prove hydrodissection efficacy, a preferred rucksack design, or a prevention threshold.

Research gap

Prospective cohorts with standardized load, pack configuration, body habitus, diagnostics, and functional follow-up would answer the paper's prevention questions better.
