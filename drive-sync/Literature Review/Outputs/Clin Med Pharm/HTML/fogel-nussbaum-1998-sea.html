One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a low-MRSA outpatient hemodialysis center, the authors argued that cefazolin could reduce routine vancomycin use because local culture susceptibility was similar for selected clinically significant infections, while postdialysis cefazolin in anuric patients produced prolonged interdialytic exposure and substantial dialysis removal.

The article combines two related evidence streams. First, 225 positive non-urine cultures collected over 7 months were examined for in-vitro susceptibility to cefazolin, vancomycin, cefazolin plus gentamicin, and vancomycin plus gentamicin. Among 146 cultures designated clinically significant, cefazolin covered 60% and vancomycin 68% (P=0.113), while cefazolin plus gentamicin covered 90% versus 68% with vancomycin (P<0.001). Vancomycin plus gentamicin provided the broadest coverage at 97%. These findings depended strongly on the center's low MRSA prevalence and on post hoc classification of likely wound pathogens.

Second, selected anuric hemodialysis patients received postdialysis cefazolin, primarily 1 g, during F80 polysulfone dialysis. Mean nondialysis elimination was slow (Ke 0.027/h; half-life 26.4 h), while dialysis elimination was faster (Ke 0.254/h; half-life 3.19 h). Peak concentrations averaged 193 microg/mL; predialysis concentrations remained above the authors' referenced approximately 8 microg/mL susceptibility threshold across the studied/extrapolated interdialytic intervals. The authors therefore proposed 1 g IV after each dialysis session, with 750 mg below 50 kg and no supplemental nondialysis dose for anuric patients. These are historical, narrowly conditioned conclusions rather than contemporary practice standards.

Section condensation

Abstract and introduction

The paper frames routine vancomycin use in chronic hemodialysis as convenient but potentially contributory to vancomycin-resistant enterococci. It proposes cefazolin, alone or with gentamicin, as a possible empiric alternative when local susceptibility supports it.

The stated objectives were to characterize culture susceptibility in a large outpatient dialysis center and then determine whether postdialysis cefazolin could maintain usable peak and trough concentrations in anuric patients.

Materials and Methods

All non-urine cultures from a 217-patient freestanding dialysis center over 7 months were categorized by blood/wound source, Gram category, and susceptibility to four antibiotic regimens. The authors did not determine whether every positive culture represented infection versus colonization/contamination. Because surface wound isolates could be nonpathogenic, they created a probable-pathogen subset; clinically significant cultures combined positive blood cultures with that wound subset.

For pharmacokinetics, selected anuric patients with clinical infection and cefazolin-susceptible cultures received cefazolin after dialysis: 1 g for 11 dose observations and 2 g for 4, infused over 5 minutes. Dialysis used an F80 polysulfone dialyzer. Concentrations were measured around 15 doses, 15 dialysis treatments, and 9 interdialysis periods. The investigators calculated volume of distribution, Ke, half-life, URR, and cefazolin reduction ratio; susceptibility comparisons used chi-square, and dialysis-removal relationships used regression plus categorical chi-square testing.

Results: susceptibility and microbiology

There were 225 positive cultures, including 18 blood and 207 wound cultures; 19 cultures (8%) were MRSA. Across all cultures, cefazolin coverage was lower than vancomycin (52% vs 73%, P<0.001). For blood cultures, cefazolin and vancomycin were not statistically different (78% vs 89%, P=0.371). For wound cultures, cefazolin was lower (49% vs 72%, P<0.001).

After excluding likely nonpathogens, 128 probable-pathogen wound cultures remained: cefazolin 57% versus vancomycin 66% (P=0.158), while cefazolin plus gentamicin reached 91% versus 66% with vancomycin (P<0.001). In 146 clinically significant cultures, cefazolin was 60% versus vancomycin 68% (P=0.113); cefazolin plus gentamicin was 90% versus 68% (P<0.001); vancomycin plus gentamicin was 97% versus 90% with cefazolin plus gentamicin (P=0.015). Table 1 contains an internal inconsistency: the all-cultures cefazolin-plus-gentamicin cell prints 175 (50%), although 175/225 calculates to 77.8% and the narrative treats the regimen as comparable to vancomycin.

Results: pharmacokinetics, dialysis removal, clinical response

Mean cefazolin volume of distribution was 0.126 L/kg (SD 0.047; range 0.070-0.182) or 8.97 L (SD 3.86). Peak concentrations ranged 79-452 microg/mL (mean 193). Nondialysis Ke averaged 0.027/h with half-life 26.4 h; predialysis concentrations ranged 8.9-176 microg/mL (mean 78), and extrapolated 48- and 72-hour levels remained above 8 microg/mL in the studied doses.

During dialysis, Ke averaged 0.254/h and half-life 3.19 h; mean concentrations fell from 50 microg/mL predialysis to 25 microg/mL postdialysis. Linear regression between cefazolin and urea reduction was not significant (r²=0.111, P=0.246), but high/low categorical analysis was significant (chi-square 7.90, P=0.007). All cefazolin-treated study patients were reported to resolve infection; one patient developed a morbilliform rash that resolved after discontinuation.

Discussion

The authors interpret local susceptibility as supporting cefazolin, with or without gentamicin, for clinically stable outpatient hemodialysis patients when MRSA prevalence is low. They explicitly caution that cefazolin may not be appropriate where MRSA rates are higher and that vancomycin plus gentamicin offers the broadest empiric coverage in critically ill patients.

For susceptible organisms, they argue that postdialysis dosing can avoid off-dialysis intravenous access. They note that the recommendation applies only to anuric patients, that residual kidney function increases nondialysis clearance, that only polysulfone dialyzers were studied, and that MICs were not measured. They also emphasize lower acquisition cost and shorter infusion time for cefazolin.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that empiric cefazolin may replace vancomycin in low-MRSA outpatient dialysis settings and that cefazolin plus gentamicin provides broader modeled coverage than vancomycin alone for the defined pathogenic-culture subsets.

They recommend 1 g IV cefazolin after each dialysis session for susceptible infections in anuric patients, with 750 mg for patients below 50 kg and no supplemental nondialysis dose. The source itself limits this conclusion to its studied population and dialysis context.

Main strengths

  • Links antimicrobial stewardship motivation to local microbiology and directly measured pharmacokinetics.
  • Reports explicit dose timing, dialyzer type, serum sampling, pharmacokinetic equations, and quantitative concentration ranges.
  • Separates all cultures, blood cultures, wound cultures, probable-pathogen wounds, and a clinically significant composite.
  • States important applicability boundaries: low MRSA setting, anuria, and polysulfone dialysis.

Main limitations

  • Susceptibility analysis is retrospective and based on in-vitro coverage rather than randomized comparative clinical outcomes.
  • Positive cultures were not prospectively adjudicated as infection versus colonization/contamination; probable-pathogen definitions were analytical exclusions.
  • PK observation counts were small; the number of unique patients is not clearly reported in the extracted source text.
  • No cefazolin-treated study patient had bacteremia, so bacteremia efficacy was not directly established.
  • MICs were not measured; interpretation used a referenced approximate susceptibility threshold.
  • Only F80 polysulfone dialysis and anuric patients were studied; residual renal function changed clearance in an excluded patient.
  • Table 1 contains a visible internal numeric inconsistency for the all-cultures cefazolin-plus-gentamicin percentage.
  • The article is from 1998; present-day resistance patterns, dialysis technology, and guidance were not externally verified in this task.
