One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper is useful as historical dialysis pharmacokinetic and stewardship evidence. It should not be used by itself to set current cefazolin or vancomycin practice.

The paper asks two linked questions. First, could cefazolin reduce routine vancomycin use in a low-MRSA outpatient hemodialysis unit without sacrificing empiric culture coverage? Second, could cefazolin be dosed only after dialysis in anuric patients using a polysulfone dialyzer? The retrospective culture review included 225 positive non-urine cultures from a 217-patient unit over 7 months. For the 146 cultures the authors labeled clinically significant, cefazolin and vancomycin coverage was 60% and 68%, with no significant pairwise difference. Cefazolin plus gentamicin covered 90%, more than vancomycin alone, while vancomycin plus gentamicin covered 97%.

The PK component supports the mechanics of postdialysis dosing more directly than the paper supports comparative clinical effectiveness. Cefazolin nondialysis half-life averaged 26.4 hours in nine interdialysis observations. Dialysis half-life averaged 3.19 hours in 15 observations. Mean peak concentration was 193 micrograms/mL, and observed predialysis concentrations averaged 78 micrograms/mL. The authors recommended 1 g IV after each dialysis session for susceptible infections in anuric patients, with 750 mg suggested below 50 kg. The main weaknesses are the single-center ecology, unadjudicated positive cultures, small PK sample, polysulfone-only dialysis, no measured cefazolin MICs, and absence of cefazolin-treated bacteremia during the study.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames vancomycin resistance as the reason to test a practical alternative. It reports the 217-patient culture review, says cefazolin was comparable with vancomycin for clinically significant infections in the low-MRSA setting, and reports PK values of Ke 0.027 with half-life 26.4 hours off dialysis and Ke 0.254 with half-life 3.19 hours during dialysis. It closes with a 1 g postdialysis dose, reduced to 750 mg below 50 kg.

Introduction

The authors describe why vancomycin was attractive in chronic hemodialysis: broad gram-positive activity and convenient intermittent dosing. They then connect heavy vancomycin use with selection pressure for vancomycin-resistant enterococci and propose cefazolin, alone or with gentamicin, as a way to reduce vancomycin exposure.

The study has two aims. It uses local culture data to estimate empiric coverage and separately measures cefazolin kinetics in anuric hemodialysis patients to determine whether dosing after dialysis can maintain usable concentrations between treatments.

Materials and methods

The microbiology component reviews all non-urine positive cultures over 7 months. Cultures are grouped as blood or wound, by Gram stain, and by susceptibility to four regimens. The investigators did not decide whether a positive culture represented infection, colonization, or contamination. They later created a probable-pathogen wound subset by excluding organisms they considered likely colonizers or low-pathogenicity isolates.

The PK component gave selected anuric patients cefazolin 1 g for 11 doses or 2 g for 4 doses, infused over 5 minutes after dialysis with an F80 polysulfone dialyzer. The paper reports 15 peak/trough dose pairs, 15 pre/postdialysis pairs, and 9 interdialysis periods. It calculates volume of distribution, elimination rate constants, half-lives, URR, and CRR. Culture comparisons use chi-square tests. URR and CRR use linear regression and categorical chi-square testing.

Results

The center produced 225 positive cultures, including 18 blood and 207 wound cultures. MRSA accounted for 19 cultures, 8%. In the clinically significant subset of 146 cultures, reported coverage was 60% for cefazolin, 68% for vancomycin, 90% for cefazolin plus gentamicin, and 97% for vancomycin plus gentamicin. Cefazolin versus vancomycin was not statistically different in that subset. Cefazolin plus gentamicin exceeded vancomycin alone, while vancomycin plus gentamicin retained a small coverage advantage.

PK measurements showed mean volume of distribution 0.126 L/kg and mean peak concentration 193 micrograms/mL. Nondialysis Ke was 0.027 per hour with half-life 26.4 hours. Dialysis Ke was 0.254 per hour with half-life 3.19 hours. Mean concentration fell from 50 micrograms/mL before dialysis to 25 after dialysis. CRR and URR did not show a significant linear relation, although the categorical high/low comparison was significant. All cefazolin-treated clinical infections were reported to resolve, and one patient developed a morbilliform rash that resolved after drug discontinuation.

Discussion

The authors interpret the culture data as support for replacing routine empiric vancomycin with cefazolin in clinically stable outpatient patients when local MRSA prevalence is low. They reserve vancomycin plus gentamicin as the broadest of the tested combinations and argue that cefazolin plus gentamicin offers a lower-cost, shorter-infusion alternative with only a small loss of coverage.

For known cefazolin-susceptible infections, they argue that anuric patients on polysulfone dialysis can receive cefazolin after dialysis without supplemental interdialytic doses. They warn that residual renal function can shorten exposure, non-polysulfone dialyzers were not studied, and high-MRSA settings may not fit their empiric strategy. They also acknowledge that MICs were not measured and that their treated study patients did not include bacteremia.

Conclusion

The paper concludes that cefazolin can substitute for vancomycin as empiric therapy in low-MRSA outpatient hemodialysis settings and that susceptible infections in anuric patients can receive 1 g IV after dialysis. It suggests 750 mg below 50 kg, rejects routine supplemental nondialysis doses for the studied population, and cites lower drug cost and shorter infusion time as additional reasons to reduce vancomycin use.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 8/10

Directly addresses outpatient hemodialysis antibiotic selection and postdialysis cefazolin dosing.

Evidence basis: Culture susceptibility, dosing, PK, safety, and workflow costs are all reported.
Limiting factor: The evidence is from 1998 and does not establish current practice.
What would raise or lower it: A current external guideline and dosing check would raise practice relevance.

Novelty at publication: 7/10

The paper joined an antimicrobial stewardship argument to a postdialysis cefazolin PK study.

Evidence basis: It tested a practical after-dialysis regimen and compared four empiric coverage strategies.
Limiting factor: The microbiology and PK components were small and single-center.
What would raise or lower it: Replication across centers and dialyzers would strengthen the contribution.

Method strength: 4/10

The design combines a retrospective culture review with a small prospective PK analysis.

Evidence basis: The paper defines culture subsets, sampling times, PK equations, and statistical tests.
Limiting factor: No randomized clinical comparator exists, and infection status was not adjudicated for positive cultures.
What would raise or lower it: A prospective comparative clinical study with prespecified endpoints would raise this score.

Evidence strength: 4/10

The PK measurements are useful, but the main cefazolin versus vancomycin effectiveness claim rests largely on in vitro susceptibility.

Evidence basis: Table 1 provides denominators and pairwise tests, and Figures 1 to 4 report measured PK data.
Limiting factor: Clinical resolution lacked a control group, the treated cohort had no bacteremia, and MICs were not measured.
What would raise or lower it: Controlled clinical outcomes and direct PK/PD target assessment would raise this score.

External validity: 3/10

The findings apply narrowly to a freestanding unit with low MRSA prevalence and to anuric patients dialyzed with an F80 polysulfone membrane.

Evidence basis: The authors state the MRSA and residual renal function boundaries.
Limiting factor: Single-center ecology, one dialyzer type, small PK sample, and anuric-only dosing limit transferability.
What would raise or lower it: Multicenter data across resistance patterns, residual renal function, and modern dialyzers would raise this score.

Implementation value: 5/10

The paper gives explicit postdialysis doses, infusion times, and a simple dialysis-session workflow.

Evidence basis: The regimen is concrete: 1 g after dialysis, with a suggested 750 mg dose under 50 kg.
Limiting factor: The source is historical and was not externally checked against current guidance in this run.
What would raise or lower it: Current guideline, susceptibility, and modern high-flux PK verification would be required before implementation.

Validity

The paper is strongest when it reports measured cefazolin concentrations and weakest when it treats in vitro susceptibility as a stand-in for comparative clinical effectiveness. The microbiology review has clear denominators, but the investigators did not adjudicate positive cultures. The probable-pathogen subset depends on investigator classification. The treated cefazolin cohort lacks a control group and contains no bacteremia during the study.

Internal consistency

Table 1 contains one visible numerical conflict. The all-culture cefazolin-plus-gentamicin entry reports 175 cultures and 50%. Those values cannot both describe the same denominator of 225. The paper's prose says this regimen was equivalent to vancomycin for all cultures, and the reported pairwise P value is 0.120. This artifact leaves the table cell unchanged and flags the discrepancy.

What the evidence supports

The source supports that cefazolin had a long nondialysis half-life in the studied anuric patients, that the F80 polysulfone treatment removed cefazolin, and that postdialysis dosing produced the reported peak and predialysis concentrations. It also supports the local culture susceptibility proportions. It does not establish that cefazolin and vancomycin produce equivalent patient outcomes across hemodialysis settings.

Practice translation
