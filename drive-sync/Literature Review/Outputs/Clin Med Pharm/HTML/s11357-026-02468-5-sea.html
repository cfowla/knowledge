One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a large linked NSW cohort of people with AF, OAC use—especially DOAC use—was associated with lower incident dementia and dementia-related mortality than no OAC use. The association did not materially differ across baseline LIBRA risk tertiles, but it was stronger in participants aged ≥75 years.

Source-derived result. The primary IPTW analysis found HR 0.77 (95% CI 0.70-0.85) for any OAC versus no OAC. DOACs were associated with lower dementia risk (HR 0.63, 0.56-0.70), while warfarin was associated with higher risk in the primary model (HR 1.31, 1.16-1.49). Individual DOAC estimates were apixaban 0.56, rivaroxaban 0.69, and dabigatran 0.75. Five-year dementia risk was reported as 5.6% versus 6.8%, ARR ≈1.2 percentage points and NNT ≈83.

Robustness. Competing-risk, propensity-matched, and ≥80% exposure sensitivity analyses preserved the overall DOAC signal. Warfarin's excess-risk signal attenuated in the propensity-matched cohort (HR 1.07, 0.73-1.57) and high-exposure analysis (HR 1.15, 0.99-1.32).

Independent appraisal. The study is methodologically stronger than a simple claims analysis because of linked outcome ascertainment, IPTW, matching, competing-risk modeling, and long follow-up. It still cannot establish cognitive benefit from anticoagulation: treatment choice is vulnerable to confounding by indication, important covariates such as frailty/renal function/baseline cognition were incompletely measured, dispensing is not ingestion, exposure was treated using landmark classification, and dementia ascertainment remained administrative.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors framed prior evidence as limited by incomplete dementia ascertainment, short follow-up, limited agent-specific analysis, and inadequate accounting for baseline lifestyle-related dementia risk. They used linked 45 and Up Study data to compare OAC users with non-users and to examine warfarin, individual DOACs, LIBRA strata, sex, age, and bleeding-risk strata.

The abstract reports the same primary pattern seen in the full results: lower dementia risk with OAC and especially DOAC use, higher risk with warfarin in the primary model, no significant interaction by LIBRA or ORBIT category, stronger association at age ≥75 years, and lower dementia-related mortality with DOACs.

Introduction

The rationale links AF to cognitive decline/dementia through cerebral microembolism, hypoperfusion, inflammation, and shared vascular risk. The authors position anticoagulation as a plausible modifier of this risk but note limitations in previous observational studies, including incomplete outcome capture and class-level treatment of DOACs.

LIBRA is introduced as a weighted composite of modifiable dementia-risk factors. The study question is whether OAC-dementia associations persist across baseline LIBRA risk burden and clinically relevant subgroups, with possible implications for anticoagulant selection.

Methods — design and data source

The study used the population-based 45 and Up Study in NSW, linked to PBS dispensing, hospital admissions, emergency presentations, mental-health encounters, cancer records, and mortality. The parent cohort recruited 267,357 adults aged ≥45 years during 2005-2009; about 19% of invitees participated.

Linkage used probabilistic methods with manual review of uncertain matches. The breadth of linkage is a major strength for outcome capture, but selection into the 45 and Up Study and reliance on administrative coding remain important external- and measurement-validity constraints.

Methods — population and exposure

Eligible participants had a first AF diagnosis from 1 Jan 2010 through 30 Jun 2022, were aged ≥45 years, had no dementia at or before AF diagnosis, and had at least 1 year of follow-up. The final analytic cohort was 21,671.

Primary exposure used a 6-month landmark: participants initiating an OAC during this interval were classified as users, and those without recorded dispensing as non-users. Outcome follow-up began 1 year after AF diagnosis. OAC users were classified by first OAC; a sensitivity analysis required ≥80% dispensing coverage over follow-up.

Methods — LIBRA, outcomes, and covariates

LIBRA used 11 weighted modifiable risk components—hypertension, diabetes, smoking, coronary heart disease, depression, obesity, dyslipidaemia, physical inactivity, chronic kidney disease, alcohol intake, and Mediterranean-style diet adherence—while cognitive stimulation was unavailable. Baseline LIBRA was divided into tertiles.

Incident dementia was ascertained through linked hospital, emergency, mental-health, death, and antidementia-medication records. Dementia-related mortality required dementia as an underlying or contributing cause of death. Baseline clinical, lifestyle, medication, CHA2DS2-VA, and ORBIT variables were assembled from survey and linked data.

Methods — statistical analysis

The primary analysis used propensity-score IPTW with stabilised weights, 1st/99th percentile trimming, and ASMD <0.10 as the balance target. The PS included key demographics, cardiovascular comorbidity, bleeding history, smoking, BMI, and selected medications. Weighted Cox models with robust variance were further adjusted for additional comorbidities, calendar year, and cardiovascular drugs not in the PS model.

Prespecified subgroup analyses included sex, age, LIBRA, and ORBIT. Sensitivity analyses used Fine-Gray competing-risk models, 1:1 PSM with a very narrow 0.001-SD caliper, and ≥80% long-term OAC exposure. The study also reported 5-year ARR and NNT from Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence estimates.

Results — cohort and balance

Of 48,092 participants with AF, 21,671 entered the final cohort: 15,352 OAC users and 6,319 non-users; OAC users comprised 3,314 warfarin and 12,038 DOAC users. Mean age was 74.2 years, 45.9% were women, and mean follow-up was 6.0 years.

The authors report successful balance for all variables used in the weighting model. Table 1 nevertheless shows residual imbalance in several non-weighting medications after IPTW and a matched-cohort table inconsistency; these are relevant to interpretation and are detailed below.

Results — incident dementia

Weighted dementia incidence was 12.9 per 1,000 person-years among OAC users versus 15.5 among non-users. The primary adjusted HR was 0.77 (0.70-0.85). Five-year risk was reported as 5.6% versus 6.8%, ARR ≈1.2 percentage points, NNT ≈83.

Class and agent estimates favored DOACs: DOAC HR 0.63; apixaban 0.56; rivaroxaban 0.69; dabigatran 0.75. Warfarin was associated with increased risk in the primary model (HR 1.31). In exploratory head-to-head analysis, apixaban was lower than dabigatran (HR 0.79), while rivaroxaban versus dabigatran was not significant (HR 0.93).

Results — sensitivity analyses and subgroups

Fine-Gray analysis gave OAC subdistribution HR 0.78, DOAC 0.64, and warfarin 1.25. PSM gave OAC HR 0.68 and DOAC 0.59, while warfarin was no longer significant (1.07). Restriction to ≥80% OAC exposure gave OAC HR 0.81; apixaban 0.65, rivaroxaban 0.71, dabigatran 0.78, and warfarin 1.15 (p=0.054).

There was no significant interaction by sex (p=0.528), LIBRA category (p=0.699), or ORBIT category (p=0.069). Age interaction was significant (p<0.001): HR 1.01 for age <75 and 0.66 for age ≥75. Higher LIBRA score itself was associated with greater dementia risk (HR 1.06, 1.02-1.12).

Results — dementia-related mortality

OAC use was associated with lower dementia-related mortality (HR 0.70, 0.60-0.81), driven by DOACs (0.53, 0.44-0.63). Individual DOAC estimates were apixaban 0.52, dabigatran 0.62, and rivaroxaban 0.50.

Warfarin was associated with higher dementia-related mortality in the primary weighted analysis (HR 1.25, 1.04-1.50). As with incident dementia, causal interpretation is limited by nonrandom treatment allocation and treatment-quality information unavailable for warfarin.

Discussion

The authors interpret the findings as support for a possible cognitive benefit of anticoagulation, particularly DOACs, beyond stroke prevention. Proposed mechanisms include fewer silent emboli, less intracranial bleeding than warfarin, and possible effects of thrombin/factor Xa pathways on inflammation and blood-brain barrier function.

They explicitly acknowledge that residual confounding, dispensing-based exposure, lack of INR/TTR, incomplete dementia subtype ascertainment, baseline-only LIBRA/covariates, and limited generalisability to younger people restrict causal inference. They caution that warfarin's primary excess-risk estimate may reflect prescribing bias, poorer anticoagulation control, and residual confounding rather than a direct adverse cognitive effect.

Conclusion

The source concludes that DOAC use was associated with lower incident dementia and dementia-related mortality, with consistency across LIBRA and ORBIT strata and stronger association in older adults. Warfarin did not show a stable protective pattern because its primary excess-risk signal attenuated in sensitivity analyses.

The authors state that the study should not independently change clinical practice. They frame the results as reinforcing guideline-concordant anticoagulation and as rationale for randomised confirmation of cognition-related effects.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Strong observational pharmacotherapy evidence with useful agent- and risk-stratified estimates, but not sufficient to select an anticoagulant for dementia prevention or infer causality.
Relevance — 8/10Directly relevant to AF anticoagulation, comparative DOAC/warfarin outcomes, and patient-centered long-term risk. Evidence basis: 21,671-person linked cohort with agent-specific results. Limit: dementia prevention is not a standard anticoagulant-selection endpoint. Would rise with: randomised cognition-focused evidence.
Novelty — 7/10Extends prior observational work with baseline LIBRA stratification, individual DOAC estimates, and linked dementia ascertainment. Limit: the overall DOAC-versus-warfarin direction is not conceptually new. Would rise with: validated causal or mechanistic differentiation among DOACs.
Method strength — 7/10Large linked cohort, IPTW, robust Cox models, matching, Fine-Gray analysis, and exposure sensitivity checks are substantial strengths. Limit: residual confounding, static landmark exposure classification, incomplete warfarin-quality data, and source-table inconsistencies. Would rise with: richer clinical covariates, time-varying exposure, validated cognition measures, and prospective design.
Evidence strength — 6/10Effect estimates are internally consistent for DOACs across several sensitivity analyses and include uncertainty. Limit: observational treatment allocation prevents causal attribution; warfarin results are unstable across analyses. Would rise with: randomised or strong target-trial emulation evidence.
External validity — 6/10Real-world NSW data and a large older cohort support applicability to older adults with AF. Limit: 45 and Up participation was ~19%, the cohort is Australian and older, and younger populations may differ. Would rise with: replication across health systems and younger/more diverse populations.
Implementation value — 5/10The study may inform counseling and literature synthesis but does not justify a cognition-driven anticoagulant switch. Limit: no intervention protocol or causal threshold for practice. Would rise with: randomised cognitive outcomes or validated decision-effect estimates.

PICO / clinical question

  • P: Adults ≥45 years with first AF in the 45 and Up Study, without prior dementia and with ≥1 year follow-up.
  • I/E: OAC initiation within 6 months; DOACs and warfarin separately.
  • C: No OAC; exploratory agent head-to-head comparisons.
  • O: Incident dementia; dementia-related mortality.

Principal strengths

  • Large sample and mean 6-year follow-up.
  • Multi-source linkage broadens dementia ascertainment beyond a single claims source.
  • Multiple confounding-control approaches and competing-risk analysis.
  • Agent-specific DOAC estimates and explicit LIBRA/ORBIT/age/sex subgroup analyses.
  • Source itself appropriately cautions against practice change from observational results.

Principal limitations and safety interpretation

  • Confounding by indication remains plausible, particularly for warfarin versus DOAC selection.
  • Frailty, renal function, socioeconomic status, baseline cognitive status, and genetic susceptibility were not fully controlled.
  • Dispensing records do not prove ingestion; discontinuation and switching after landmark classification may misclassify exposure.
  • No INR or time-in-therapeutic-range data, limiting interpretation of warfarin.
  • Administrative dementia ascertainment may miss mild disease and could not reliably separate subtypes.
  • Baseline-only LIBRA and covariates do not capture time-varying risk.
  • Internal Table 1 inconsistencies reduce confidence in some balance-reporting details.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use this forLiterature synthesis on long-term cognitive associations of anticoagulation in AF; hypothesis generation; discussion of whether DOAC benefits may extend beyond stroke prevention.
Do not use this forChoosing apixaban, rivaroxaban, or dabigatran specifically to prevent dementia; asserting warfarin causes dementia; changing anticoagulation solely for cognition.
Most actionable reading pointThe DOAC association is robust across several observational sensitivity analyses, while warfarin's primary adverse association is not stable.
What would change confidenceRandomised cognitive outcomes, target-trial emulation with time-varying exposure, reliable renal/frailty/cognition adjustment, and warfarin TTR data.
Bottom line: The paper adds credible observational support for an association between DOAC use and lower dementia risk in older adults with AF, independent of baseline LIBRA category. It should be treated as supportive—not practice-determining—evidence.
