One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In adults with systolic blood pressure 120–159 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure 80–95 mm Hg, both reducing dietary sodium and eating a DASH dietary pattern lowered blood pressure. Combining the DASH diet with the lowest sodium level produced the greatest reduction, while sodium reduction retained an effect even within the DASH diet.

Four hundred twelve participants were randomized to a control dietary pattern or DASH. Within that parallel assignment, each participant consumed high-, intermediate-, and low-sodium versions of the assigned diet for 30 days each in randomized order. All food was provided, energy intake was adjusted to keep weight stable, adherence was assessed by diet procedures and 24-hour urinary measures, and blood pressure was repeatedly measured. The achieved mean urinary sodium levels across both diets were approximately 142, 107, and 65 mmol/day during the high, intermediate, and low periods.

On the control diet, moving from high to intermediate sodium reduced systolic blood pressure by 2.1 mm Hg, and moving from intermediate to low reduced it by a further 4.6 mm Hg. On DASH, the corresponding reductions were 1.3 and 1.7 mm Hg. DASH itself lowered systolic pressure at every sodium level. Relative to high-sodium control, low-sodium DASH lowered systolic blood pressure by 8.9 mm Hg overall, by 11.5 mm Hg in participants with hypertension, and by 7.1 mm Hg in those without hypertension. The interventions were short and tested a surrogate physiologic endpoint rather than cardiovascular events.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the unresolved question as whether sodium lowering below the then-recommended 100 mmol/day adds blood-pressure benefit, including when combined with DASH. It reports statistically significant stepwise systolic reductions with sodium lowering in both diet groups and consistent effects across hypertension status, race, and sex.

The largest effect was the combined low-sodium DASH condition: systolic pressure was 7.1 mm Hg lower than high-sodium control among participants without hypertension and 11.5 mm Hg lower among those with hypertension. The authors conclude that both lower sodium and DASH reduce blood pressure, with a greater effect in combination.

Introduction

The introduction notes substantial U.S. sodium intake and prior evidence that sodium reduction and DASH each reduce blood pressure. The trial was designed to test three related issues: whether lowering sodium below 100 mmol/day yields additional benefit, whether DASH provides benefit across sodium levels, and how the combined interventions behave.

A key prevention question was whether lowering sodium would reduce blood pressure among people without hypertension. The study therefore included adults above 120/80 mm Hg, rather than limiting enrollment to established hypertension.

Methods — design and diets

The trial used a 2×4 analytic framework: participants first completed a two-week high-sodium control run-in, then were randomized to DASH or control in parallel. Within that diet assignment, they received high, intermediate, and low sodium for 30 consecutive days each, in randomized crossover order. Sodium targets at 2100 kcal were approximately 150, 100, and 50 mmol/day, scaled to energy requirements.

The DASH pattern emphasized fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, whole grains, poultry, fish, and nuts, with less red meat, sweets, and sugar-containing beverages and lower total/saturated fat and cholesterol. All food, including snacks and cooked meals, was provided; nutrient composition was monitored and energy adjusted to maintain stable weight.

Methods — eligibility, measurement, and adherence

Eligible adults were at least 22 years old with average systolic BP 120–159 mm Hg and diastolic BP 80–95 mm Hg. The investigators sought approximately equal representation of women and men and substantial Black enrollment. Exclusions included heart disease, renal insufficiency, poorly controlled hyperlipidemia or diabetes, insulin-treated diabetes, special dietary requirements, heavy alcohol intake, and medications that would materially affect blood pressure or nutrient metabolism.

Blood pressure was measured repeatedly with random-zero sphygmomanometers. Twenty-four-hour urine collections during screening and the final week of each intervention assessed sodium and other diet-linked markers. Participants and dietary staff were not shown outcome data, and personnel collecting outcome data were unaware of diet assignment. Symptoms were assessed by questionnaire.

Methods — statistical analysis

The primary endpoint used the average of the last five blood-pressure measurements of each intervention period; baseline combined screening and run-in measurements. Generalized estimating equations with exchangeable covariance accounted for within-person repeated measurements and included baseline BP, center, cohort, intervention period, and carryover terms. Predefined subgroup analyses considered hypertension status, race, and sex.

Holm adjustment was used for multiple comparisons in the total cohort; subgroup adjusted P values were not used, as prespecified. Analyses were intention-to-treat, with 22 missing intervention-period BP measurements replaced by baseline values. A planned sample of 400 gave 90% power for a 2.1-mm Hg systolic difference between sodium levels and 3.0 mm Hg between diets at each sodium level.

Results — retention and adherence

Of 412 randomized participants, 198 of 208 assigned to DASH (95%) and 192 of 204 assigned to control (94%) completed the study and supplied blood-pressure measurements in all intervention periods. Achieved urinary sodium averaged roughly 142 mmol/day at high, 107 at intermediate, and 65 at low sodium. Urinary potassium, phosphorus, and urea nitrogen were higher on DASH, consistent with the planned dietary contrast, while body weight remained stable.

Results — blood pressure

Sodium reduction lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure in a stepwise fashion on both diets. For systolic BP on control, high→intermediate sodium reduced pressure by 2.1 mm Hg (95% CI 0.8–3.4 in magnitude), and intermediate→low by 4.6 mm Hg (3.2–5.9). On DASH, the corresponding reductions were 1.3 mm Hg (0–2.6) and 1.7 mm Hg (0.4–3.0). The sodium effect was larger on the control diet than on DASH (interaction P<0.001).

DASH also reduced BP versus control at each sodium level. The extreme comparison, high-sodium control versus low-sodium DASH, yielded an 8.9-mm Hg systolic reduction (95% CI 6.7–11.1 in magnitude) and a 4.5-mm Hg diastolic reduction (3.1–5.9). The combined effect was greater than either intervention alone but less than strict arithmetic additivity.

Results — subgroup findings

The high-sodium control versus low-sodium DASH systolic difference was 11.5 mm Hg among participants with hypertension (12.6 in Black participants and 9.5 in other racial/ethnic participants), 7.1 mm Hg among those without hypertension (7.2 and 6.9, respectively), 6.8 mm Hg in men, and 10.5 mm Hg in women. Each of those extreme-comparison subgroup estimates was reported with P<0.001.

Interaction tests suggested larger sodium responses among participants with hypertension than those without hypertension, a larger control-diet sodium effect in Black participants than in other participants, and a larger DASH-diet sodium effect in women than men. These subgroup analyses are useful for effect heterogeneity but receive less inferential protection than the adjusted total-cohort comparisons.

Results — safety and tolerability

Transient systolic BP >170 mm Hg or diastolic BP >105 mm Hg occurred in 36 control-diet participants and 7 DASH participants; across sodium periods, events occurred in 18 high-, 22 intermediate-, and 3 low-sodium periods, with none during low-sodium DASH. No participant met the protocol threshold for sustained elevation requiring referral for antihypertensive pharmacotherapy.

Headache was reported at least once by 47% during high-sodium control, 39% during low-sodium control, and 36% during low-sodium DASH; both low-sodium comparisons were reported as P<0.05 versus high-sodium control. The paper therefore does not show a symptom penalty from the lower-sodium conditions over 30 days.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the trial as evidence that lowering sodium below 100 mmol/day can produce additional BP reduction and that DASH lowers BP across sodium levels. They emphasize the magnitude of the combined intervention, particularly among participants with hypertension, and view the effects in nonhypertensive participants as relevant to prevention.

They also acknowledge implementation constraints: the controlled-feeding design measures biological efficacy more directly than routine real-world adherence, and achieving low sodium at the population level would require changes in processed-food sodium. The study periods were only 30 days, so long-term health outcomes and sustained adherence were not demonstrated by this trial.

Evaluation and appraisal

VerdictRead first

This is a high-value landmark efficacy trial for the independent and combined blood-pressure effects of dietary sodium and DASH. It is methodologically strong for a feeding study and unusually useful because adherence, sodium exposure, diet composition, and weight were tightly controlled.

Relevance
10/10

Rationale: Directly addresses nonpharmacologic BP lowering and hypertension prevention.

Evidence basis: Randomized controlled feeding design with systolic/diastolic outcomes and clinically interpretable effect sizes.

Limiting factor: Does not address medication selection or hard cardiovascular outcomes.

Would change: Lower if the use case were drug therapy rather than BP/diet evidence.

Novelty
9/10

Rationale: The factorial-like parallel diet plus crossover sodium design isolates both interventions and their combination.

Evidence basis: Three sodium exposures within both DASH and control, including a lower-than-then-standard sodium target.

Limiting factor: DASH and sodium lowering were not new concepts individually.

Would change: Higher only if the trial also demonstrated long-term clinical outcomes.

Method strength
9/10

Rationale: Strong internal-control architecture for dietary efficacy.

Evidence basis: Randomization, crossover sodium periods, provision of all food, objective urinary adherence markers, stable weight, repeated BP measures, blinded outcome collection, GEE, ITT.

Limiting factor: Short periods and baseline-value replacement for 22 missing intervention measurements.

Would change: Higher with longer blinded exposure and modern missing-data sensitivity analyses.

Evidence strength
9/10

Rationale: The data directly and consistently support the short-term BP-lowering claims.

Evidence basis: Dose-step response, 95% CIs, interaction testing, high retention, adherence confirmation.

Limiting factor: Blood pressure is a surrogate; clinical events and durability were not tested.

Would change: Lower for claims about cardiovascular outcomes; higher only with long-term outcome evidence.

External validity
7/10

Rationale: Demographic diversity is a strength, but controlled feeding is unlike routine food access and adherence.

Evidence basis: Four centers, 57/56% Black enrollment and 59/54% women; both hypertension and nonhypertension.

Limiting factor: Selected untreated adults, exclusions for major disease/medications, and only 30 days per exposure.

Would change: Higher with pragmatic long-term replication across broader comorbidity and treatment contexts.

Implementation value
9/10

Rationale: The trial provides concrete dietary patterns, sodium exposure levels, achieved urinary sodium, and expected BP magnitudes.

Evidence basis: High/intermediate/low exposure structure and extreme-comparison effects are easily translated into counseling concepts.

Limiting factor: The source itself highlights processed-food sodium and long-term adherence as barriers.

Would change: Higher with scalable real-world implementation and sustainability data.

Main strengths

  • Controlled feeding sharply reduces exposure misclassification and confounding by uncontrolled diet composition.
  • Within-person randomized sodium crossover improves precision for sodium effects.
  • Parallel DASH-versus-control allocation allows diet-pattern contrasts at each sodium level.
  • Urinary sodium/potassium and stable weight corroborate adherence and isolate the intended exposures.
  • Repeated blood-pressure measurements and high completion reduce random measurement noise and attrition concerns.
  • Substantial Black and female enrollment supports clinically relevant heterogeneity assessment.

Main limitations and safety of interpretation

  • Short duration: each sodium exposure lasted 30 days; sustained adherence and durability were not established.
  • Surrogate endpoint: the study measured blood pressure, not myocardial infarction, stroke, mortality, renal outcomes, or other long-term patient-centered outcomes.
  • Controlled-food environment: efficacy under food provision may exceed effectiveness in routine practice.
  • Selected population: important comorbidities, antihypertensive medication use, and other conditions were excluded.
  • Missing-data handling: 22 missing intervention BP measurements were replaced with baseline values; this is not equivalent to a modern model-based missing-data analysis.
  • Subgroups: interaction analyses were prespecified, but multiplicity adjustment used for the total cohort was not applied to subgroup P values.
  • Non-additivity: the combined effects were large but statistically less than strict arithmetic additivity, so independent effect estimates should not simply be summed.
Do not overclaim: This trial does not demonstrate that dietary sodium reduction and DASH prevent cardiovascular events, that low sodium is universally appropriate for every patient, that effects persist indefinitely, or that the intervention is equivalent to a specific antihypertensive drug regimen. Contemporary guideline targets were not externally verified in this artifact.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now forMechanistic and counseling evidence.

Use the trial to explain that sodium amount and overall dietary pattern have separable BP effects and that combining a DASH pattern with lower sodium can produce a larger short-term BP reduction.

Magnitude anchorExtreme comparison.

High-sodium control versus low-sodium DASH differed by 8.9 mm Hg systolic overall, 11.5 mm Hg among participants with hypertension, and 7.1 mm Hg among those without hypertension.

Exposure anchorAchieved urinary sodium.

Mean urinary sodium was about 142, 107, and 65 mmol/day across the high, intermediate, and low periods, showing clear exposure separation.

Implementation constraintReal-world food environment.

The authors explicitly identify processed-food sodium as a barrier; the controlled feeding context means feasibility must be evaluated separately.

Clinical boundaryHistorical efficacy evidence, not a current guideline.

Use current clinical guidelines and patient-specific assessment for present-day sodium targets, contraindications, or treatment decisions.

Read nextOutcome and implementation evidence.

For practice decisions beyond BP efficacy, pair this paper with current hypertension guidance and evidence on long-term cardiovascular outcomes and sustainable dietary implementation.

Bottom line: The trial provides unusually clean causal evidence that both a DASH dietary pattern and sodium reduction lower short-term blood pressure, and that the combination is more potent than either strategy alone. Its strongest use is as a foundational BP-efficacy study; its weakest use is as standalone evidence for long-term clinical outcomes or present-day treatment targets.
