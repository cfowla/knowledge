One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a single-center retrospective series, 10 of 14 pregnant patients prescribed an 8-week antenatal GLE/PIB course completed therapy; all completers had undetectable HCV RNA by delivery and no major maternal or neonatal safety signals were reported in the observed sample.

Why it matters: The paper fills a specific evidence gap for GLE/PIB during pregnancy and provides practical information on gestational timing, treatment completion, SVR4/SVR12 follow-up, maternal complications, and infant screening.

Main constraint: The reassuring findings come from very small denominators, incomplete follow-up, and no comparator; rare harms, comparative efficacy, and optimal timing remain unresolved.

Section condensation

Abstract / Background

Guidelines allow direct-acting antiviral treatment during pregnancy through shared decision making, but pregnancy-specific regimen data remain limited. The report addresses a prior evidence gap for glecaprevir-pibrentasvir by describing antenatal use in 14 patients.

Methods

Beginning in March 2024, infectious diseases and maternal-fetal medicine clinicians offered prenatal HCV treatment after the first trimester or postpartum deferral. The retrospective review included adults starting GLE/PIB at 14–32 weeks’ gestation from March 2024 through August 2025; the 8-week course length motivated the gestational window.

Collected outcomes included HCV RNA during treatment and by delivery, SVR4/SVR12, liver tests, adverse effects, maternal pregnancy complications, delivery outcomes, and neonatal safety/transmission outcomes. Perinatal transmission was defined as detectable HCV RNA at age ≥2 months.

Results

Fourteen patients were prescribed GLE/PIB; 10 (71%) completed therapy. Among 13 with initiation gestational-age data, mean start was 25 weeks (range 18–31). Three noncompleters discontinued in the first week and one deferred to postpartum; anxiety/fetal-well-being concerns and medication loss were documented reasons.

All 10 completers had undetectable HCV RNA at or by delivery. All 9 with available SVR follow-up achieved SVR4 or better, and all 5 tested at 12 weeks achieved SVR12. No preterm deliveries, new ICP, new GDM, obstetric hemorrhage, low birth weight, neonatal death/stillbirth, or NICU admissions were reported; six infants were screened for HCV and none had perinatal transmission. One patient had nausea managed with oral antiemetics.

Discussion / Conclusion

The authors interpret the series as the first antenatal real-world GLE/PIB outcome report and as supportive of shared decision making for pregnancy treatment. They emphasize potential practical advantages of a shorter 8-week regimen and the problem of postpartum loss to follow-up, while noting that prospective pregnancy trial data remain necessary.

Key limitations are the retrospective design, small number of completers, incomplete SVR12/infant follow-up, absence of pharmacokinetic data, no comparator, and uncertainty about optimal gestational timing. The article also contains an internal denominator inconsistency for on-treatment liver tests: Table 1 gives n=9, whereas Results text gives n=8.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon
Useful first real-world GLE/PIB pregnancy series with reassuring observed virologic and short-term maternal/neonatal outcomes, but the evidence remains small, uncontrolled, and incomplete.

Best use: Inform shared-decision-making discussions and hypothesis generation about antenatal GLE/PIB feasibility, while tracking exact denominators and follow-up gaps.

Do not use for: Do not infer comparative safety, exclude rare fetal/maternal harms, or treat 0/6 transmission and 10/10 delivery response as definitive effectiveness estimates.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Shared decision making

The data can support a discussion that antenatal GLE/PIB has emerging real-world experience, while making clear that evidence is still limited.

Follow-up design

SVR4 may be operationally valuable before delivery when SVR12 would fall postpartum, but pregnancy registries should still capture longer follow-up whenever possible.

Safety interpretation

Zero-event findings should be read with their denominators; six infants were screened for perinatal transmission and only subsets had specific maternal laboratory follow-up.

Data quality

Preserve the source’s n=9 versus n=8 liver-test discrepancy in downstream evidence stores rather than harmonizing it without source clarification.
