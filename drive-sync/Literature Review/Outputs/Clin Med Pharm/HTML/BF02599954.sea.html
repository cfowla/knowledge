Summary, Evaluation & Appraisal

Lee HN, Cook DJ, Sarabia A, et al. Inadequacy of Intravenous Heparin Therapy in the Initial Management of Venous Thromboembolism. J Gen Intern Med. 1995;10:342-345. DOI: 10.1007/BF02599954.

One-page synthesis

Bottom line. In this 63-patient retrospective DVT cohort managed with clinician-directed IV unfractionated heparin without a standardized nomogram, only 46% met the article's prose definition of adequate heparinization by 24 hours and 62% by 48 hours. Inpatients performed worse than outpatients. Higher weight, lower initial infusion intensity, and clinical service were associated with slower anticoagulation. The paper's main contribution is historical quality-improvement evidence for standardizing and weight-adjusting IV heparin initiation, not high-certainty evidence for a specific modern regimen.
  • Design: independent duplicate chart review of consecutive venography-proven DVT patients identified from October 1990 through July 1991; 63 of 99 potentially eligible patients were analyzed.
  • Primary process endpoint: time to adequate heparinization (AH), defined in the abstract/prose as first PTT >1.5× laboratory control; therapeutic heparinization (TH) was PTT 1.5-2.5× control.
  • Attainment: AH 46% at 24 h and 62% at 48 h; TH 44% and 62%, respectively.
  • Subgroups: inpatient AH was 32% at 24 h and 50% at 48 h versus 62% and 76% in outpatients (p<0.05).
  • Process: mean initial bolus 3,819 ± 199 U, initial infusion 973 ± 41 U/h, and 10.5 ± 1.0 h from bolus to physician notification of the first PTT result.
  • Interpretation: the authors argued that low initial doses and lack of weight adjustment contributed to delay and favored a nomogram or weight-based nomogram.

Section condensation

Clinical problem and objective

The paper framed delayed heparin anticoagulation as a preventable treatment-process problem in acute DVT. It asked whether routine, non-nomogram management achieved prompt PTT targets and which patient or care-process factors predicted delay.

Methods

Consecutive patients were identified from radiology venography records. Two independent reviewers abstracted demographics, thromboembolic risk factors, clinical service, consultation service, heparin administration, and PTT values during the first 48 hours. Chi-square tests and t tests were used for unadjusted comparisons, followed by multivariable modeling of log time to AH for variables meeting a p<0.10 screening threshold.

Results

Thirty-four patients were inpatients and 29 outpatients; mean age was 66.8 years and mean weight 74.1 kg. Overall AH was achieved by 29/63 at 24 hours and 39/63 at 48 hours. Inpatients achieved AH less often than outpatients. In inpatient modeling, lower infusion rate (p=0.026) and higher weight (p=0.060) were most important; among outpatients, weight (p=0.049) and consultation service (p=0.01) were most important. Three patients had high-probability ventilation-perfusion scans after heparin began, all in the setting of delayed therapeutic PTT attainment, but this observation does not establish causality.

Discussion

The authors interpreted low initial bolus, low initial infusion rate, and lack of weight adjustment as modifiable barriers. They compared their results with previously published cohorts and nomogram studies and concluded that a standardized or weight-based nomogram was likely to improve anticoagulation consistency and speed.

PICO / clinical question

  • Population: 63 consecutive venography-proven DVT patients receiving full-dose IV heparin for at least 48 hours.
  • Exposure: clinician-directed IV UFH without a standardized heparin nomogram.
  • Comparators: inpatient vs outpatient care, service type, patient/dose factors, and published historical cohort/nomogram studies.
  • Outcomes: time and proportion achieving PTT-based AH/TH; initial dosing and monitoring-process measures.

Validity

Strengths: consecutive case identification, independent duplicate abstraction, explicit PTT definitions, and direct examination of modifiable process variables. Limitations: retrospective observational design; only 63 analyzed patients; 36 potentially eligible patients excluded based on treatment duration/context; aPTT as a surrogate endpoint; no concurrent randomized nomogram comparator; unstable estimates are plausible in small inpatient/outpatient models; and an internal AH-definition inconsistency appears in Table 1's footnote.

Practice translation / current verification

The paper is best translated as a medication-use-system lesson rather than a current VTE regimen. Contemporary adult VTE guidance has shifted substantially since 1995; the American Society of Hematology's treatment guideline favors direct oral anticoagulants over vitamin K antagonists for most patients with DVT/PE, which reduces the frequency with which routine VTE care follows the historical IV-UFH-to-warfarin pathway studied here. IV UFH remains relevant in selected clinical settings, but exact contemporary dosing and monitoring should be taken from current institutional protocols and current guideline/pharmacotherapy sources rather than this paper.

Current-practice reference: Ortel TL, Neumann I, Ageno W, et al. American Society of Hematology 2020 guidelines for management of venous thromboembolism: treatment of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Blood Advances. 2020;4(19):4693-4738. doi:10.1182/bloodadvances.2020001830. ASH continues to surface this guideline as its DVT/PE treatment guidance.

Implementation / practice takeaways

  1. Protocolization is the durable lesson: unconstrained clinician-by-clinician UFH initiation produced slow and inconsistent laboratory target attainment.
  2. Weight and initial infusion intensity matter when designing an IV UFH workflow; dose selection and subsequent titration should be explicit rather than implicit.
  3. Monitoring latency is part of the medication-use system: the reported 10.5-hour interval to physician notification of the first PTT illustrates how process delay can compound dosing delay.
  4. Historical-comparator data support the rationale for a nomogram, but do not by themselves prove the superiority or safety of any modern regimen.
  5. When using this paper in a heparin evidence map, classify it as historical quality-assurance / process evidence rather than definitive efficacy evidence.
