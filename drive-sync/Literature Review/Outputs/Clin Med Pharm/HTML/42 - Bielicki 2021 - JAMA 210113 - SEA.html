One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In children with clinically diagnosed CAP who were discharged from an emergency department or after a short hospital stay and prescribed amoxicillin, lower-dose oral amoxicillin (35–50 mg/kg/day) was noninferior to higher dose (70–90 mg/kg/day), and 3 days was noninferior to 7 days, for clinically indicated systemic antibiotic retreatment for respiratory infection by 28 days, using an 8-percentage-point noninferiority margin.

Among 824 randomized children, 814 received trial medication and formed the primary analysis population; the primary endpoint was evaluable for 789 (97%). Retreatment occurred in 12.6% with lower dose versus 12.4% with higher dose (risk difference 0.2 percentage points; upper 1-sided 95% CI 4.0) and in 12.5% with 3 days versus 12.5% with 7 days (difference 0.1; upper bound 3.9). Both upper bounds were below the prespecified 8-point noninferiority margin, and dose-by-duration interaction was not significant (P=.63).

Interpretation is constrained by the noninferiority design and the margin history. The protocol initially assumed a 5% endpoint rate and a 4% margin. After an internal pilot showed substantially more retreatment, the endpoint estimate was revised and the margin changed to 8%; Supplement 2 states the 8% choice was consensus-based and acknowledged as arbitrary but conservative relative to then-available antibacterial trial guidance. Severe-disease and prior-inpatient-antibiotic subgroup estimates were less reassuring, and those analyses were not adequately powered to establish subgroup noninferiority.

Methods and clinical question

PopulationChildren ≥6 months, 6–24 kg, with clinically diagnosed CAP and planned amoxicillin at discharge. Enrollment pathways included direct ED/assessment-unit discharge and discharge after ≤48 hours of inpatient beta-lactam treatment.
Interventions / comparatorsOral amoxicillin 35–50 vs 70–90 mg/kg/day, randomized independently to 3 vs 7 days. Duration blinding used active medication/placebo after day 3.
Primary outcomeClinically indicated systemic antibiotic retreatment for respiratory tract infection, including CAP, within 28 days; adjudicated by a blinded endpoint review committee.
Noninferiority frameworkRisk-difference margin 8 percentage points; 1-sided 95% CI. Expected retreatment 15%, 15% loss to follow-up, 90% power, target sample 800.

Key eligibility boundaries

Key exclusions included >48 hours of prior beta-lactam therapy, prior non–beta-lactam therapy, severe chronic disease, amoxicillin contraindication/allergy, complicated pneumonia, and bilateral wheezing without focal CAP signs. These exclusions matter to generalizability.

Analysis population and prespecification

The primary analysis included randomized participants who received trial medication, analyzed according to assigned groups. Kaplan–Meier methods estimated cumulative incidence at day 28 while accounting for loss to follow-up. Main effects were estimated by collapsing across the other factorial randomization after checking interaction. Secondary outcomes were not adjusted for multiplicity and therefore are exploratory. Prespecified sensitivity analyses varied retreatment definition and included a severe-CAP subgroup; on-treatment and prior-inpatient-antibiotic analyses were post hoc.

Noninferiority-margin amendment. The original assumptions were a 5% endpoint rate and 4% noninferiority margin. Internal-pilot review found all-cause retreatment 20.3% (95% CI 15.0–27.1), making the original event-rate assumption a serious underestimate. The margin was changed to 8% by consensus; Supplement 2 explicitly describes the 8% choice as arbitrary but conservative relative to guidance available at the time.

Sensitivity and subgroup findings

Prespecified sensitivity analyses supported the primary result. In the prespecified severe-CAP subgroup, lower versus higher dose had retreatment 17.3% versus 13.5% (difference 3.8 points; upper 1-sided 95% CI 10.0), which crossed the 8-point margin; 3 versus 7 days was 16.0% versus 14.8% (difference 1.2; upper 7.4), which remained within the margin. Interaction P values were .18 and .73, respectively, so these subgroup findings do not establish differential treatment effects.

In the post hoc subgroup with prior inpatient antibiotics, both dose and duration comparisons crossed the 8-point margin (upper bounds 11.4 and 11.5). In children without prior inpatient antibiotics, both comparisons met the margin. The article cautions that the inpatient subgroup was underpowered for noninferiority and interaction testing.

Symptoms, adherence, safety, and microbiology

Seven of nine symptom outcomes showed no significant randomized-group differences. Cough resolved more slowly with 3 versus 7 days (median 12 vs 10 days; HR 1.2; detailed Results reports 90% CI 1.0–1.4; P=.04), and sleep disturbed by cough also favored 7 days (median 4 vs 4 days; HR 1.2; detailed Results reports 90% CI 1.0–1.3; P=.03). These secondary analyses were not multiplicity-adjusted.

Diarrhea occurred in 345 (44%), rash in 193 (24%), and thrush in 57 (7%). Forty-three children (5%) had serious adverse events, all hospitalizations; 37/43 were respiratory and there were no deaths. One serious adverse event—hospitalization for IV treatment because of vomiting on day 2 in a child assigned higher dose plus 3 days—was judged related to trial medication. Active-treatment adherence was 98% with 3 days versus 91% with 7 days (difference 7 points; 95% CI 4–10; P<.001). At day 28, pneumococcal carriage and penicillin nonsusceptibility did not differ significantly by dose or duration, and no penicillin-resistant pneumococci were identified.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Strongly relevant, practice-informing randomized evidence for antibiotic stewardship, but interpretation should remain tied to the eligibility boundaries and the chosen 8-point noninferiority margin.
Relevance: 9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses stewardship-sensitive amoxicillin dose and duration decisions in pediatric CAP.

Evidence basis: Factorial RCT with clinically meaningful retreatment endpoint and outpatient discharge population.

Principal limiting factor: Applies to a selected, mostly non–very-severe population rather than all pediatric CAP.

What would change the score: Would rise with direct alignment to a current guideline question; would fall in settings with materially different severity or resistance patterns.

Novelty: 8/10

Rationale: Tests both dose and duration in the same pediatric CAP trial, with simultaneous randomized comparisons.

Evidence basis: 2×2 factorial design and prespecified noninferiority framework.

Principal limiting factor: Short-course pediatric CAP evidence was emerging concurrently, so the conceptual question was not unique.

What would change the score: Would rise with stronger mechanistic/PK linkage or broader severity spectrum.

Method strength: 8/10

Rationale: Randomization, blinding, placebo-controlled duration assignment, blinded endpoint adjudication, and prespecified SAP strengthen internal validity.

Evidence basis: 814 treated participants; 97% primary endpoint availability; prespecified sensitivity analyses; factorial interaction check.

Principal limiting factor: Primary analysis excluded randomized children who never received trial drug; noninferiority margin changed during the trial after pilot data review.

What would change the score: Would rise with a fully intention-to-treat co-primary analysis and a margin fixed before outcome data review.

Evidence strength: 8/10

Rationale: Both primary comparisons were well inside the 8-point noninferiority margin, with consistent main sensitivity analyses.

Evidence basis: Dose difference 0.2 points, upper 1-sided 95% CI 4.0; duration difference 0.1, upper 3.9.

Principal limiting factor: Interpretation depends materially on acceptance of the revised 8% margin; severe/prior-inpatient subgroups are less certain.

What would change the score: Would rise with replication using a tighter fixed margin and adequate power in severe/inpatient strata.

External validity: 7/10

Rationale: Multicenter enrollment across 29 hospitals improves transportability within similar high-income acute-care pathways.

Evidence basis: 28 UK hospitals and 1 Irish hospital; ED and short inpatient pathways represented.

Principal limiting factor: Excludes very severe/complicated disease and major comorbidity; does not establish total treatment duration after prolonged inpatient/IV treatment.

What would change the score: Would rise with pragmatic replication across broader severity, geography, and resistance settings.

Implementation value: 8/10

Rationale: Provides a concrete regimen comparison and identifies adherence advantages of shorter treatment.

Evidence basis: Oral amoxicillin 35–50 vs 70–90 mg/kg/day and 3 vs 7 days; active-treatment adherence 98% vs 91%.

Principal limiting factor: Implementation still requires local guideline, diagnostic, resistance, and severity criteria; this appraisal does not verify 2026 practice guidance.

What would change the score: Would rise with current guideline integration and a validated bedside eligibility pathway.

Validity judgment

Strengths: randomized factorial design; blinded duration assignment with placebo; blinded endpoint adjudication; high primary-endpoint ascertainment; prespecified SAP; sensitivity analyses; multicenter enrollment; clinically meaningful retreatment outcome rather than a purely surrogate endpoint.

Principal limitations: noninferiority conclusion is margin-dependent; the margin was doubled from 4% to 8% after internal pilot review of accumulating outcome data; primary analysis is treated-participant rather than all-randomized; secondary outcomes are multiplicity-unadjusted; subgroup estimates are imprecise; the study excludes very severe/complicated CAP and major comorbidities; prolonged inpatient/IV treatment is not addressed.

Source-reported limitations

  • Children truly requiring antibiotics cannot be identified unequivocally; some retreatment may reflect self-limiting disease.
  • The trial does not establish total treatment duration for children initially admitted for longer inpatient therapy.
  • Subgroup analyses after inpatient treatment were not adequately powered for noninferiority, and interaction tests may also be underpowered.
  • Findings should not be generalized to very severe disease or major comorbidity.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Best useEvidence base for considering shorter/lower-dose outpatient amoxicillin strategies in children closely matching CAP-IT eligibility and discharge pathways.
Do not use forClaiming equivalence across all pediatric CAP, very severe disease, major comorbidity, prolonged IV/inpatient therapy, or any regimen outside the randomized comparisons.
MonitorEligibility/severity, prior inpatient antibiotics, local dosing conventions, adherence, early clinical deterioration, and retreatment criteria.
Bottom line. The primary trial result is internally persuasive under the final 8-point noninferiority criterion. The most important interpretive question is not whether the observed group rates were similar—they were—but whether an 8-percentage-point allowable loss is clinically acceptable for the intended population and setting.
