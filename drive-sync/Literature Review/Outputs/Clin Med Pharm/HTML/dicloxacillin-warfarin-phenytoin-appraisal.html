Dicloxacillin induction of warfarin and phenytoin metabolism

Critical appraisal of a prospective, fixed-sequence, within-subject drug–drug interaction study in 28 healthy volunteers.

Bottom-line verdict

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-yield interaction paper for clinical pharmacists because it directly quantifies dicloxacillin-associated increases in warfarin and phenytoin oral clearance using intensive pharmacokinetic sampling. The signal is convincing for enzyme induction, but the study does not establish a fixed empiric dose-increase rule, the optimal monitoring schedule, the offset after dicloxacillin discontinuation, or the effect during chronic therapeutic phenytoin exposure.

Scope warning. This paper establishes a pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interaction under controlled single-dose conditions. It does not directly test chronic warfarin or phenytoin dose requirements, thromboembolism, seizure recurrence, bleeding, or an operational monitoring protocol.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis. A 21-day course of dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily substantially increases apparent oral clearance of both warfarin enantiomers and phenytoin. Shorter elimination half-lives and increased p-HPPH formation clearance support hepatic enzyme induction as the dominant mechanism, while oral bioavailability effects from intestinal CYP or P-glycoprotein induction cannot be excluded.

The strongest contribution is direct measurement: prior reports linked dicloxacillin with lower INR and thromboembolic risk, but this study demonstrates large, internally consistent changes in warfarin exposure and clearance. Phenytoin findings extend the interaction beyond warfarin and are supported by a metabolic product measure, not only total drug concentrations.

The most important restraint is translation. Participants received one 20 mg warfarin dose and one 300 mg phenytoin dose before and during dicloxacillin. Phenytoin concentrations remained well below the usual therapeutic range, so the effect under saturable, nonlinear metabolism at steady state is uncertain. The paper’s recommendation to increase doses whenever dicloxacillin is initiated is more prescriptive than the data justify; intensified monitoring with individualized adjustment is better supported than an automatic preemptive increase.

Clinical question and PICO-style framing

PopulationHealthy, medication-free, non-smoking adults 18–50 years old; 15 CYP2C9*1/*1 carriers and 13 heterozygous CYP2C9*1/*2 or *1/*3 carriers.
ExposureDicloxacillin 500 mg orally four times daily for 21 days.
ComparatorEach participant’s baseline pharmacokinetics and warfarin pharmacodynamics before dicloxacillin.
OutcomesWarfarin-enantiomer and phenytoin AUC, CL/F, half-life, Cmax; INR AUC and INRmax; urinary p-HPPH and formation clearance; genotype effect modification.

Primary practical question: Does dicloxacillin produce a clinically meaningful induction signal for narrow-therapeutic-index CYP substrates, and is the magnitude large enough to justify proactive monitoring? Answer: yes for the interaction signal; no for a specific empiric dose-change magnitude.

Methods: what was done

Participants and genotype enrichment

Healthy volunteers were recruited from a university campus and required documentation of good health, absence of chronic medication use, non-smoking status, and age 18–50 years. Penicillin or cephalosporin allergy was exclusionary. Recruitment was deliberately enriched for CYP2C9 genotype: 15 CYP2C9*1/*1 participants and 13 carriers of one reduced-function allele (eight *1/*2 and five *1/*3).

The planned sample size was 26 to detect a difference greater than 55 mL/h in (S)-warfarin CL/F with 90% power and a two-sided α of 0.05; 28 completed all phases. The power calculation was oriented to the primary clearance outcome, not the small genotype subgroup comparisons.

Fixed-sequence interaction design

At baseline, participants received warfarin 20 mg once and, at least one week later, phenytoin 300 mg once. Blood was sampled intensively for 96 hours after phenytoin and 120 hours after warfarin; INR was measured serially through 120 hours. After baseline phenytoin sampling, dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily was started for 21 days. Warfarin was repeated on dicloxacillin day 10 and phenytoin on day 17, when induction was expected to be at plateau.

The morning dicloxacillin dose was omitted on probe-drug study days to reduce direct interference with absorption. The design gives strong within-person comparisons but is not a randomized crossover: every participant received baseline first and induced conditions second, so secular or period effects are not independently controlled.

Assays and endpoints

(R)- and (S)-warfarin, plasma phenytoin, and urinary p-HPPH were measured with validated HPLC methods. Noncompartmental analysis generated AUC to infinity, apparent oral clearance (CL/F), terminal half-life, and Cmax. Warfarin pharmacodynamics were represented by AUC of INR from 0–120 hours and maximum observed INR. p-HPPH formation clearance used urinary molar excretion over 96 hours divided by phenytoin AUC over the same interval.

Genotyping covered CYP2C9 exons and a VKORC1 polymorphism used to assign haplotypes. Paired tests compared pre- and post-dicloxacillin measures; independent tests compared genotype groups. The article reports means, standard deviations, and P values, but not confidence intervals for the main interaction effects and does not describe multiplicity correction for the numerous subgroup analyses.

The p-HPPH formation-clearance result is a key mechanistic strength: it supports increased metabolic formation relative to phenytoin exposure. However, the observed phenytoin Cmax was only about 4.25 μg/mL, far below the usual therapeutic concentration range discussed by the authors, so extrapolation to saturable steady-state metabolism is uncertain.

Pharmacogenetic findings

The absolute increase in (S)-warfarin CL/F was significantly larger in CYP2C9*1/*1 participants than in heterozygous *2/*3 carriers (P<0.03). p-HPPH formation-clearance induction was also larger in *1/*1 carriers (P<0.03), while the difference in phenytoin CL/F increase was borderline (P=0.058). The decline in INR endpoints did not differ by CYP2C9 group but was reported as larger in VKORC1 AA carriers.

Interpret cautiously. These genotype analyses involve very small subgroups, multiple comparisons, and supplementary tables/figures not included in the evaluated PDF. They are hypothesis-generating and are not sufficient to support genotype-specific monitoring or dose-adjustment rules.

Independent evaluation and appraisal

Relevance9.5/10

Rationale: Directly informs management of clinically consequential interactions involving two narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.

Evidence basis: Quantified PK changes, INR response, and metabolic marker.

Principal limit: Dicloxacillin is less commonly used in some settings than alternative antistaphylococcal agents.

Would raise/lower: Comparative data with flucloxacillin/nafcillin/oxacillin would raise general usefulness; limited local use would lower it.

Novelty8.5/10

Rationale: Provides the first detailed direct warfarin-enantiomer PK characterization and first human phenytoin PK assessment reported by the authors.

Evidence basis: Intensive paired sampling before and during induction.

Principal limit: The existence of dicloxacillin-mediated CYP induction was already established.

Would raise/lower: Chronic steady-state dosing and onset/offset data would materially raise novelty.

Method strength7.5/10

Rationale: Intensive within-subject sampling, validated assays, prespecified induction timing, and complete follow-up provide strong internal PK measurement.

Evidence basis: 21–22 plasma timepoints, serial INR, urine collection, genotyping, noncompartmental analysis.

Principal limit: Fixed sequence without placebo/time control; small genotype subgroups; no dicloxacillin levels.

Would raise/lower: Randomized sequence or control period, adherence/exposure measurement, and confidence intervals would raise the score.

Evidence strength8.0/10

Rationale: Large, consistent changes across AUC, CL/F, half-life, INR, and metabolite formation strongly support a real interaction.

Evidence basis: All key paired endpoints were highly significant and mechanistically coherent; Cmax remained unchanged.

Principal limit: Evidence is for surrogate PK/PD endpoints, not clinical outcomes or dose algorithms.

Would raise/lower: Chronic-treatment studies with clinical or therapeutic-control outcomes would raise it; unexplained period effects would lower it.

External validity5.0/10

Rationale: Healthy young adults receiving single doses differ substantially from older, comorbid patients on chronic warfarin or phenytoin.

Evidence basis: Exclusion of chronic medications, no disease states, low phenytoin concentrations, genotype-enriched cohort.

Principal limit: Nonlinear phenytoin kinetics at therapeutic concentrations were not tested.

Would raise/lower: Multicenter patient studies at steady state and broader genotypes/ages would raise the score.

Implementation value8.0/10

Rationale: The effect size is large enough to justify electronic interaction alerts and closer INR/level monitoring.

Evidence basis: Approximately 40–63% increases in apparent clearance with reduced PD exposure.

Principal limit: No onset/offset curve, monitoring interval, or validated dose-change percentage.

Would raise/lower: A practical prospective management protocol with post-discontinuation follow-up would raise the score.

Main strengths

Within-person controlEach participant served as their own comparator, reducing interindividual confounding for the main PK effect.
Dense samplingLong sampling windows captured elimination phases and supported noncompartmental AUC and half-life estimates.
Mechanistic triangulationLower AUC, increased CL/F, shorter half-life, and increased p-HPPH formation clearance point in the same direction.
Clinically interpretable PDSerial INR demonstrates that the warfarin PK effect has functional anticoagulant consequences.

Main limitations and threats to interpretation

  1. Single-dose, healthy-volunteer conditions. The study does not reproduce chronic disease, polypharmacy, variable adherence, nutrition, inflammation, or steady-state dosing.
  2. Phenytoin nonlinearity. Concentrations were below the usual therapeutic range; induction may behave differently near saturation, and a small dose increase can cause disproportionate concentration changes.
  3. CL/F cannot isolate clearance from bioavailability. Shorter half-life and metabolite formation support hepatic induction, but intestinal CYP/P-gp effects remain possible because no intravenous probe or absolute bioavailability assessment was used.
  4. No unbound concentrations. Protein-binding displacement is argued to be implausible, but intrinsic clearance independent of free fraction could not be calculated.
  5. No dicloxacillin concentrations or formal adherence measure. Exposure variability may partly explain heterogeneous induction magnitude.
  6. No onset, offset, or de-induction characterization. The study tested plateau induction only; it does not define when to intensify monitoring or how long risk persists after discontinuation.
  7. Genotype analyses are underpowered. Only 13 variant carriers were included, with eight *1/*2 and five *1/*3; no homozygous reduced-function groups were assessed.
  8. Statistical reporting is incomplete for clinical interpretation. Main effects lack confidence intervals, and numerous subgroup tests are presented without a stated multiplicity strategy.
  9. Safety reporting is sparse. The main text states that all participants completed the study but does not provide a structured adverse-event summary.
  10. Conclusion overreach. Broadly recommending dose increases for CYP2C9/CYP3A4 narrow-therapeutic-index substrates goes beyond the two tested probe drugs and beyond the study’s ability to define safe dose increments.

Practice and implementation translation

Reasoned clinical translation — not directly tested by the study. The evidence supports proactive monitoring and individualized adjustment, not automatic fixed-percentage dose escalation.
WarfarinTreat initiation and discontinuation as interaction transitions.
Obtain or confirm a recent INR, increase INR surveillance during dicloxacillin exposure, and continue surveillance through de-induction. Adjust from measured INR response rather than the observed clearance percentage.
PhenytoinUse therapeutic drug monitoring and clinical seizure assessment.
Avoid proportional dose increases based on the 42.4% clearance estimate because steady-state phenytoin is nonlinear. Consider total and, when clinically indicated, free concentrations.
Clinical decision supportMake the alert bidirectional.
Trigger on dicloxacillin start and stop, name the induction mechanism, request follow-up monitoring, and warn against unmonitored dose changes.
Alternative therapy reviewConsider whether an equally effective non-inducing option is available.
This choice depends on infection, susceptibility, site, allergy, and local policy; the study did not compare antibiotic alternatives.

What should change now

  • Interaction databases, order review, and handoff documentation should flag dicloxacillin as a meaningful inducer for warfarin and likely for phenytoin.
  • Clinical teams should plan monitoring before the interaction becomes apparent, including the period after dicloxacillin is stopped.
  • Genotype should not currently determine management; the subgroup signals are insufficiently precise.

What should not change based on this study alone

  • Do not apply a universal 40–60% dose increase to warfarin, phenytoin, or other CYP substrates.
  • Do not assume the interaction magnitude is identical with chronic therapy, therapeutic phenytoin concentrations, older adults, organ dysfunction, or polypharmacy.
  • Do not generalize the findings to every CYP3A4 or CYP2C9 narrow-therapeutic-index drug without drug-specific evidence.
