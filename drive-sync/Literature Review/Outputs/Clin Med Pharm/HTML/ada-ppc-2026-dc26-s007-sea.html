One-page synthesis

The 2026 ADA technology chapter treats diabetes devices as part of routine diabetes care rather than a late escalation. It favors early and individualized access to CGM and AID, continued access across payors, training that includes data use and troubleshooting, standardized reporting, and practical support in school, work, and hospital settings. CGM receives strong support for people receiving insulin. AID is preferred over MDI, conventional pumps, and sensor-augmented pumps for type 1 diabetes and for adults with type 2 diabetes, with more qualified guidance in other groups.

The chapter is unusually operational for a guideline section. It specifies BGM backup for CGM users, meter-accuracy standards, common BGM and CGM interferences, device categories, accommodations, inpatient continuation criteria, and the role of licensed virtual coaching. Its main constraint is the same problem it describes: device generations and software change faster than long clinical trials. Recommendation strength therefore varies from A to E, and local implementation still depends on current product availability, regulatory status, coverage, training capacity, and institutional protocols.

Verdict: Read first. Best use: current ADA technology policy, prescribing and education decisions, and local protocol design. Do not use it as the sole source for device-specific labeling, payer coverage, or a primary-study effect estimate.

Section condensation

General device principles

The ADA recommends that devices be offered broadly, but selection should match the user’s needs, preferences, skills, and care context. Education is not a one-time setup step. CGM and AID training should include use of device data, data sharing, troubleshooting, and reassessment over time. The chapter also treats caregiver capability, school support, workplace accommodations, uninterrupted access, early initiation, and standardized reports as parts of safe device care.

Early use is supported for CGM, CSII, and AID when it fits the person or caregiver. C-peptide, islet-autoantibody status, and duration of insulin treatment should not be prerequisites for CSII or AID. School plans should describe device use and include backup treatment for device failure.

Blood glucose monitoring

BGM remains necessary even in a CGM-centered care model. CGM users need access to a meter for confirmation, interruption, calibration when required, and rapid glucose change. Insulin users should check according to regimen and risk, including around meals, sleep, exercise, suspected hypo- or hyperglycemia, low-glucose treatment, and driving.

The chapter emphasizes meter accuracy, approved supply chains, proper strip storage, and drug or substance interference. BGM has not consistently lowered A1C in people on noninsulin therapy, but can be useful when results are tied to changes in meals, activity, or medication.

Continuous glucose monitoring

CGM is recommended from diabetes onset or later for people receiving insulin, and it also has roles with hypoglycemia-causing noninsulin treatment or whenever it improves management. Near-daily use and uninterrupted supplies matter. Pregnancy with type 1 diabetes has specific support for time-in-range and A1C goals. Periodic personal or professional CGM is an option when continuous use is not feasible.

Device choice should fit the user rather than a single preferred platform. The chapter covers real-time, OTC, and professional CGM, interpretation, skin reactions, and substance interference. The practical message is that the clinical benefit depends on reliable data, sustained access, and the ability to act on those data.

Insulin delivery and AID

Pens are preferred for most people using MDI, with syringes retained when formulation, cost, dosing, availability, or user capability favors them. Injection aids and connected pens have defined roles. For AID, the chapter makes its strongest delivery recommendation in type 1 diabetes and adults with type 2 diabetes. AID can also be considered for selected basal-insulin-treated type 2 diabetes when individualized goals are not met.

AID systems combine CGM, a pump, and an algorithm that modulates insulin delivery. The chapter synthesizes pivotal trials and large real-world cohorts, while stressing that technical understanding and troubleshooting remain necessary. Continued payer access is recommended for people already using pumps or AID.

Open-source AID, digital health, and inpatient care

Clinicians should continue to support patients who choose open-source AID rather than withdrawing diabetes management advice. The chapter supports pairing devices or diabetes apps with licensed online or virtual coaching, while warning that app recommendations should depend on validation or FDA clearance and that privacy and security require attention.

Hospitalization does not automatically require stopping personal technology. CGM can continue when clinically appropriate with confirmatory point-of-care glucose under a protocol. Pumps and AID can also continue when supplies, training, competency assessment, resources, and institutional protocols are in place.

The future

The chapter explicitly recognizes a research-timing problem. Hardware and software can change before a study on the prior version is complete. The patient remains the key selection criterion, and possession of a device alone does not improve outcomes without appropriate use and engagement.

Key evidence reported by the chapter

These are secondary reports from the guideline. They are not substitutes for reading the cited primary publications.

  • In youth with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes, an open-label multicenter randomized trial that initiated AID within 21 days of diagnosis reported 10% higher time in range at 12 months than usual care, with lower A1C.
  • In a database study of almost 27,000 children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes, each additional daily BGM check was associated with a 0.2 percentage-point lower A1C after adjustment for multiple confounders, along with fewer acute complications.
  • A systematic review and meta-analysis in type 1 diabetes reported a 0.30 percentage-point A1C advantage for pump therapy versus MDI, with a 95% CI from 0.58 to 0.02 percentage points lower.
  • Prior meter analyses reported that 14 of 18 evaluated glucose meters met minimum accuracy requirements.
  • A systematic review of 20 real-world AID studies representing 171,209 individuals with type 1 diabetes was reported to confirm benefits seen in pivotal trials.

Main strengths

  • Recommendation coverage spans the device life cycle: selection, training, data review, ongoing access, and site-specific use.
  • CGM and AID recommendations are linked to both randomized and real-world evidence.
  • Tables convert common safety problems, especially meter and CGM interference, into usable checks.
  • The chapter distinguishes strong evidence from expert opinion through printed evidence grades.

Main limitations

  • The supplied chapter delegates full guideline-development methods and evidence-grade definitions to a separate ADA methodology source.
  • Evidence maturity differs sharply by population and recommendation. Several implementation statements are C or E.
  • Technology and regulatory status can change before trial evidence catches up.
  • Access, training capacity, reimbursement, and local protocols limit general implementation.
  • Individual cited effects remain secondary reports until the cited studies are read directly.

Implementation and practice takeaways

CGM

Offer early when indicated, train on data and alarms, protect supply continuity, and keep BGM available as backup.

AID

Prefer it for type 1 diabetes and adults with type 2 diabetes when appropriate. Do not use C-peptide, autoantibodies, or insulin duration as access gates.

Safety

Build meter and CGM interference checks into education and troubleshooting. Use approved meters and reliable strip sources.

Access

Preserve continuity across payors and support use in school, work, and hospital settings when clinical and operational conditions are met.

Data

Use standardized reports and give both users and clinical teams access to interpretable device data.

Local policy

Translate the chapter through current product labels, formulary and payer coverage, hospital protocols, and staff competency requirements.
