Scope warning. This artifact evaluates the supplied 2022 paper as a source. It does not verify current guidelines, current labeling, or newer comparative evidence. The paper is secondary evidence. Its network estimates should not be treated as results generated by a head-to-head dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin trial.

One-page synthesis

The paper asks a reasonable formulary-style question: when both SGLT2 inhibitors improve heart-failure outcomes versus placebo, is there evidence that dapagliflozin 10 mg/day or empagliflozin 10 mg/day is better? The authors searched five databases through October 2021, limited inclusion to randomized trials with at least 80 participants and conventional 10 mg dosing, and constructed a frequentist random-effects network. They report 11 independent trials across 12 publications. The placebo-controlled results generally favor both agents for heart-failure outcomes. The active-agent network comparisons are much less secure because the network is not a head-to-head randomized comparison.

The central clinical claim is that dapagliflozin should be preferred for a more comprehensive prognosis benefit, driven largely by a network estimate for all-cause mortality, OR 0.80 with 95% CI 0.66-0.98 versus empagliflozin. The same paper reports empagliflozin as better for heart-failure exacerbation. That ranking rests on indirect comparison across trials whose patients differed in ejection fraction, comorbidities, functional class, follow-up, and background treatment. The authors acknowledge several of these differences and even use them to explain mortality findings. That explanation is a warning about transitivity, not support for a causal drug ranking.

Verdict: Do not use for practice as a stand-alone comparative-choice source. Use it as a historical evidence map and as a pointer to the underlying randomized trials. It supports the SGLT2 inhibitor class effect far better than it supports choosing dapagliflozin over empagliflozin.

Section condensation

Introduction

The authors frame SGLT2 inhibitors as an emerging heart-failure therapy with benefits beyond glucose lowering. They note the absence of direct dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin comparisons and present network meta-analysis as a way to compare the two agents across heart-failure prognosis outcomes.

Methods

PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to 13 October 2021. Eligible studies were randomized trials in chronic heart failure, including reduced or preserved ejection fraction, that compared dapagliflozin 10 mg/day, empagliflozin 10 mg/day, or placebo and reported at least one prognosis endpoint. The review excluded studies with fewer than 80 participants, nonconventional doses, nonrandomized designs, and unavailable full text. Two reviewers extracted data independently, a third resolved discrepancies, and the authors used Cochrane risk-of-bias tools.

The primary outcomes were hospitalization for heart failure and heart-failure exacerbation. Secondary outcomes were cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization and cardiovascular death. Tertiary outcomes were all-cause death and hypoglycemia. The authors used odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, conventional fixed- or random-effects pairwise models according to heterogeneity criteria, and frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis in Stata. They state that global and loop inconsistency and small-study effects were assessed.

Results

Screening began with 2,921 records and ended with 12 publications containing 11 independent trials. Both drugs were associated with lower HF hospitalization than placebo. The network comparison for that outcome was not significant, dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.75-1.10. For cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization, the active-agent comparison was also not significant, OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.78-1.17. For cardiovascular death, dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin was OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.69-1.08.

The paper reports dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66-0.98 for all-cause death, favoring dapagliflozin. It reports empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59-0.84 for HF exacerbation. That exacerbation value has a source-integrity problem because the same estimate is also reported for dapagliflozin versus placebo, and Table 2 has a directionality footnote that conflicts with the narrative orientation. Hypoglycemia did not differ significantly among treatments.

Discussion and recommendations

The authors interpret the network as showing broad similarity between the two agents for several endpoints, with dapagliflozin favored for all-cause death and empagliflozin favored for HF exacerbation. They discuss possible mechanisms and suggest that differences in the empagliflozin trial populations, including more HFpEF and comorbidity, may have affected mortality results.

The paper recommends dapagliflozin 10 mg/day as the overall preferred option. The authors also acknowledge dose restriction, inconsistent follow-up, differing functional-class composition, and limited literature volume. They call for further experimental studies. Those limitations matter because the comparative recommendation depends on across-trial exchangeability.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 8/10

Directly addresses the comparative dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin question in heart failure.

Evidence basis. It pools randomized SGLT2 inhibitor trials and reports active-agent network comparisons across six outcomes.

Limiting factor. The evidence is from a 2021 search and does not represent current comparative evidence.

What would change it. A current systematic review with contemporary HF trials and direct comparative data would raise the score.

Novelty: 7/10

At publication, the paper addressed a clinically useful comparison that lacked head-to-head trials.

Evidence basis. It used a three-node network to estimate dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin differences.

Limiting factor. The clinical question is valuable, but the method depends almost entirely on indirect comparison.

What would change it. Direct randomized comparison or stronger transitivity evidence would make the contribution more distinctive.

Method strength: 4/10

The review used a multi-database search, RCT-only eligibility, duplicate extraction, risk-of-bias review, and random-effects NMA, but the active-agent network is indirect and clinically heterogeneous.

Evidence basis. Methods report PRISMA/Cochrane use, frequentist random-effects NMA, inconsistency checks, and funnel plots.

Limiting factor. No head-to-head trials are shown, the supplied supplement is missing, no certainty framework is reported, and Table 2 has a directionality inconsistency.

What would change it. A registered protocol, complete supplement, transparent transitivity assessment, certainty grading, and direct active-agent evidence would raise the score.

Evidence strength: 3/10

The placebo-controlled class-effect findings are more credible than the claim that one agent is superior to the other.

Evidence basis. Several active-versus-placebo estimates are precise, but dapagliflozin-versus-empagliflozin estimates come from the network rather than direct randomization.

Limiting factor. Cross-trial differences in HF phenotype, comorbidity, follow-up, and functional class threaten exchangeability, and the authors themselves invoke case-mix differences to explain mortality results.

What would change it. Head-to-head randomized evidence or a network with strong, demonstrated transitivity and consistent endpoint definitions would raise the score.

External validity: 4/10

The review spans HFrEF and HFpEF but combines populations that differ enough to weaken a single comparative drug ranking.

Evidence basis. Table 1 includes reduced and preserved EF trials with varied diabetes prevalence, NYHA class, background therapy, atrial fibrillation, and natriuretic peptide levels.

Limiting factor. Heterogeneous case mix and follow-up make the indirect drug comparison difficult to generalize to a specific formulary population.

What would change it. Patient-level network meta-regression or direct comparative studies in a clearly defined HF population would raise the score.

Implementation value: 3/10

The paper is useful as a historical evidence map but is too weak to justify dapagliflozin-over-empagliflozin formulary preference by itself.

Evidence basis. It identifies relevant trials and summarizes endpoint-specific pooled estimates.

Limiting factor. The comparative recommendation exceeds what indirect evidence with internal reporting ambiguity can securely support.

What would change it. Current guideline alignment, direct comparative effectiveness data, and formulary-relevant safety and operational outcomes would raise the score.

Validity and evidence interpretation

The review has several sound components. It restricts the evidence base to randomized trials, searches multiple databases, uses duplicate extraction, assesses risk of bias, reports effect sizes with uncertainty, and uses random-effects network methods. Those features are enough to make the paper useful for finding trials and understanding the evidence available by late 2021.

The comparative ranking is the weak point. Figure 2 does not show a direct randomized dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin evidence base. Network comparisons therefore depend on transitivity. Table 1 shows material differences in ejection fraction, diabetes prevalence, atrial fibrillation, background therapy, NYHA class, natriuretic peptide levels, and trial context. The authors themselves point to cross-trial population differences to explain the mortality comparison. That makes the active-agent superiority claim fragile.

Reporting and reproducibility limits

The supplied main article cites supplementary search terms, detailed risk-of-bias material, direct meta-analysis plots, and additional study characteristics, but that supplement is not in the source folder. The main article does not report a certainty-of-evidence framework such as GRADE or CINeMA. A protocol or registration identifier is not reported in the supplied main article. The Table 2 directionality conflict further lowers confidence in precise reuse of the active-agent estimates.

Practice and implementation takeaways

Best use

Historical evidence map, trial-finding source, and background on early indirect dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin comparisons in HF.

Do not use for

A stand-alone decision to prefer dapagliflozin over empagliflozin on a hospital formulary or in an individual patient.

What carries forward

The paper supports a class-effect narrative more strongly than a within-class ranking. Direct comparative effectiveness, current guidelines, safety, access, and operational factors need separate evidence.

Source check before reuse

Adjudicate the Table 2 orientation and HF-exacerbation comparison against the original analysis or supplementary material before importing that specific active-agent estimate into a CDS or evidence graph.
