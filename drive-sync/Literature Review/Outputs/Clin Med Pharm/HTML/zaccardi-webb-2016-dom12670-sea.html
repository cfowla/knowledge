Design
Systematic review + frequentist network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials

Scope warning. This is a 2016 study-level synthesis focused mainly on metabolic biomarkers and selected safety outcomes. It predates most modern cardiovascular, heart-failure, and kidney outcome evidence for SGLT2 inhibitors and should not by itself establish contemporary therapeutic interchangeability.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Across 38 randomized trials (23,997 participants), licensed doses of canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and empagliflozin improved glycaemia, body weight, and blood pressure versus placebo. Canagliflozin 300 mg generally produced the largest biomarker changes, while all agents increased genital-infection risk.

Key comparative signal. At the highest doses, canagliflozin 300 mg lowered HbA1c by about 0.2 percentage points more than dapagliflozin 10 mg and empagliflozin 25 mg; lowered fasting plasma glucose by about 0.6 and 0.5 mmol/L more; lowered systolic blood pressure by about 2 mmHg more than dapagliflozin 10 mg; but increased LDL cholesterol by 0.13 and 0.15 mmol/L relative to dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, respectively.

Safety. Canagliflozin 100/300 mg showed approximately 1.4–1.6-fold higher hypoglycaemia odds in some comparisons, but the placebo difference disappeared in a sensitivity analysis excluding trials with insulin or sulphonylurea background therapy. Dapagliflozin 10 mg increased UTI odds by about 1.4 versus placebo and empagliflozin 25 mg. Genital infection increased roughly four- to sixfold versus placebo for all SGLT2 inhibitors, with no meaningful between-agent difference.

Source integrity. The main text mislabels the 1,959 UTI events as “hypoglycaemic” events; the genital-infection event count is 1,285 in the main text versus 1,312 in Supplementary Table S3; and the UTI-network denominator of 24,037 exceeds the stated total review population of 23,997. These discrepancies were preserved rather than silently corrected.

Methods

Search and eligibility

PubMed, ISI Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception through 3 November 2015 in any language. Eligible studies were randomized trials lasting at least 24 weeks in adults with type 2 diabetes and evaluating licensed doses of canagliflozin (100/300 mg), dapagliflozin (5/10 mg), or empagliflozin (10/25 mg) against placebo or another glucose-lowering treatment. Trials restricted to chronic kidney disease at baseline were excluded.

Primary outcomes were HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, and body weight. Secondary cardiometabolic outcomes were systolic/diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides. Safety outcomes were all hypoglycaemia, urinary tract infection, genital infection, diabetic ketoacidosis, and fractures.

Extraction, bias, and synthesis

Three authors independently searched and extracted data using predefined forms; disagreements were resolved by arbitration. ClinicalTrials.gov supplemented reports when needed. Risk of bias used the Cochrane tool. Pairwise random-effects meta-analysis used DerSimonian-Laird; frequentist network meta-analysis used multivariate meta-analysis with a common heterogeneity variance.

Odds ratios were used for dichotomous outcomes with a 0.5 zero-event correction. Consistency was tested by a design-by-treatment interaction model. Missing continuous-outcome contrasts in three-arm trials assumed correlation 0.5, with 0.2/0.7/0.9 sensitivity analyses yielding similar results.

Results

Study population and bias

From 2,174 records, 79 reports underwent full-text assessment and 38 unique RCTs were included. Trials enrolled 23,997 participants, were mostly multinational, and followed patients for 24–208 weeks. Weighted baseline age was 58 years, HbA1c 8.1%, diabetes duration 8 years, and 57% were male.

Risk-of-bias judgments were 89.5% low, 1.8% high, and 8.7% unclear across domains. High/unclear domain-specific risk was reported in 1.8% of canagliflozin, 10.8% of dapagliflozin, and 16.7% of empagliflozin trials.

The article reports reductions across the class of roughly 0.6–0.9 percentage points in HbA1c, 1.1–1.9 mmol/L in FPG, and 1.6–2.5 kg in body weight. The main body-weight network had significant inconsistency (p=0.0229); the 24–30 week sensitivity network did not (p=0.386).

Secondary cardiometabolic outcomes

All SGLT2 inhibitors lowered blood pressure and modestly increased HDL versus placebo. The largest reported systolic and diastolic reductions were approximately 4.9 and 2.0 mmHg, and the greatest HDL increase was about 0.07 mmol/L. Both canagliflozin doses showed small LDL increases and triglyceride reductions; total-cholesterol data were limited.

Safety

Canagliflozin 100/300 mg increased hypoglycaemia odds in some network comparisons, but in the prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding insulin or sulphonylurea background studies no SGLT2 inhibitor differed significantly from placebo. Dapagliflozin 10 mg increased UTI odds by about 1.4 versus placebo and empagliflozin 25 mg. Genital-infection odds were 4.2 (95% CI 2.7–6.3) for empagliflozin 10 mg and 5.9 (4.0–8.3) for canagliflozin 300 mg versus placebo, with no detected between-SGLT2 difference. DKA and fractures were too sparsely reported for formal network analysis.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Strong historical comparative synthesis and useful for understanding dose-specific biomarker and safety signals, but modern practice decisions require newer cardiovascular, heart-failure, renal, and direct-comparative evidence.

Relevance — 8/10

Directly informs comparative SGLT2 glycaemic, weight, blood-pressure, lipid, and safety effects.

Evidence basis: 38 RCTs with broad cardiometabolic and safety networks.

Limiting factor: Evidence predates cardiovascular/renal outcome-era SGLT2 practice.

Would change score: Would rise with modern outcome trials and contemporary direct comparisons.

Novelty — 8/10

At publication, the work addressed a clinically important head-to-head evidence gap through network meta-analysis.

Evidence basis: No direct SGLT2 comparisons were available, and the review integrated three agents across many outcomes.

Limiting factor: Novelty is historical rather than current.

Would change score: Would be lower if judged solely against the modern evidence base.

Method strength — 7/10

The review used prespecified methods, independent screening/extraction, Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment, pairwise random-effects models, and frequentist NMA with inconsistency testing.

Evidence basis: PRISMA workflow, explicit network model, sensitivity analyses.

Limiting factor: Study-level evidence, indirect comparisons, and significant body-weight inconsistency.

Would change score: Direct trials, individual-participant data, and stronger certainty grading would raise the score.

Evidence strength — 7/10

The primary biomarker effects are supported by large randomized networks, but comparative drug differences remain indirect and some safety endpoints are sparse.

Evidence basis: 38 RCTs; 23,997 participants; outcome-specific networks up to 24,037 reported participants.

Limiting factor: No direct head-to-head trials; DKA/fracture data insufficient; source-count discrepancies.

Would change score: Direct comparative RCTs and robust long-term clinical outcomes would raise the score.

External validity — 6/10

The network was multinational and broad but trial populations, follow-up, background therapy, and outcome ascertainment varied.

Evidence basis: 34 of 38 RCTs were multinational; follow-up 24–208 weeks.

Limiting factor: Trial-era T2DM populations and biomarker outcomes do not fully represent modern cardiorenal indications.

Would change score: Contemporary pragmatic and outcome-focused studies would raise the score.

Implementation value — 6/10

Useful for historical class/dose comparison and safety pattern recognition, but not sufficient alone for present-day formulary interchangeability decisions.

Evidence basis: Clear dose-specific biomarker and genitourinary safety estimates.

Limiting factor: Pre-EMPA-REG/DECLARE/DAPA-HF/EMPEROR/CKD-era evidence and no direct SGLT2 comparisons.

Would change score: Integration with modern cardiovascular, renal, and heart-failure evidence would raise the score.

Main strengths

  • Broad randomized evidence base with 38 trials and multiple clinically relevant metabolic and safety outcomes.
  • Independent search/extraction, Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment, explicit frequentist NMA methods, inconsistency testing, and multiple sensitivity analyses.
  • Supplementary data allow outcome-specific denominator and network-quality reconciliation.

Main limitations

  • No direct head-to-head randomized comparisons among the SGLT2 inhibitors.
  • Study-level rather than participant-level synthesis; heterogeneous background therapy, follow-up, and outcome ascertainment.
  • Body-weight network inconsistency; sparse DKA/fracture data; several source-count/wording inconsistencies.
  • Evidence horizon ends in 2015 and predates the mature cardiorenal outcome evidence that now drives SGLT2 use.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Useful for
Historical class-effect and dose-response context; relative metabolic efficacy; recognition of genital-infection class signal; understanding why early indirect comparisons favored canagliflozin 300 mg on biomarkers.
Do not use alone for
Current inpatient formulary interchangeability, heart-failure treatment selection, CKD treatment selection, or claims of long-term cardiovascular/renal equivalence.
Interpret carefully
Hypoglycaemia comparisons because background insulin/sulphonylurea use changes the signal; body-weight rankings because the full network was inconsistent.
