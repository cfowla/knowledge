One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper is short and mechanistically useful: dasatinib showed frequent epinephrine- and arachidonic-acid aggregation defects, with smaller signals for imatinib, while nilotinib and bosutinib appeared less disruptive in this small cohort. Its strongest use is explaining how clinically relevant primary-hemostasis defects could exist despite normal PT/aPTT and platelet counts; it is not strong enough to quantify clinical bleeding risk or dictate current TKI selection.

Core thesis. In patients with CML-CP, dasatinib exposure was associated with substantial abnormalities in platelet aggregometry, especially after epinephrine and arachidonic-acid stimulation. Among 27 dasatinib-treated patients not receiving aspirin, 85% had reduced epinephrine-induced aggregation, 70% reduced arachidonic-acid aggregation, and 59% both. Imatinib also showed arachidonic-acid impairment in 10 of 15 evaluable non-aspirin patients, whereas all 7 nilotinib patients had normal aggregation. An ex vivo PFA-100 experiment showed rapid collagen/epinephrine closure-time prolongation with dasatinib 400 nM but not imatinib.

Why it matters. The laboratory pattern supports a primary-hemostasis explanation for bleeding that may not be detected by routine coagulation studies. The evidence is strengthened by two patients whose abnormal epinephrine aggregation normalized after switching from dasatinib to bosutinib, and by the normal-donor PFA experiment. However, no participant was actively bleeding at aggregation testing, the study was nonrandomized, group sizes were small and unequal, and the PFA time-course method used blood from a single donor.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame dasatinib-associated bleeding in the absence of thrombocytopenia as evidence of a possible primary-hemostasis defect. They tested platelet aggregation in 91 CML-CP patients who were off therapy or receiving dasatinib, bosutinib, imatinib, or nilotinib. The abstract reports impaired arachidonic-acid aggregation in 70% and epinephrine aggregation in 85% of dasatinib-treated patients, with both abnormalities in 59%.

The abstract further states that 85% of bosutinib, 100% of nilotinib, and 33% of imatinib patients had normal platelet aggregation and that dasatinib 400 nM rapidly prolonged collagen/epinephrine PFA-100 closure time in normal blood. The stated conclusion is that dasatinib and, to a lesser extent, imatinib cause platelet aggregometry abnormalities.

Introduction

The rationale is that dasatinib inhibits BCR-ABL1 plus multiple other kinases and had been associated with bleeding, including mucocutaneous bleeding in patients with normal platelet counts. The authors hypothesized that defective primary hemostasis could explain this phenotype and compared agonist-induced platelet aggregation across TKI exposures.

The introduction explicitly distinguishes the new study question from prior bleeding observations: the paper is testing platelet function, not prospectively adjudicating bleeding events. The authors describe the platelet inhibition as potentially reversible and propose that it may contribute to dasatinib-associated bleeding.

Methods

Ninety-one CML-CP patients were evaluated. Routine monitoring included CBC, PT, aPTT, and fibrinogen. Platelet-rich plasma underwent light-transmittance aggregometry within 4 hours using weak/strong ADP, arachidonic acid, collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin. The paper reports predefined low-cutoff thresholds, although the published text/visual ordering of ristocetin concentrations and threshold values is not fully transparent in the extracted layout.

PFA-100 testing used citrated whole blood from normal donors; collagen/epinephrine and collagen/ADP cartridges were used. For the time-course experiment, dasatinib or imatinib 400 nM was incubated for 0, 30, 60, and 120 minutes. The Methods specify a single donor with known epinephrine-responsive platelets for these time-course tests.

Results and discussion

At testing, 4 patients were off TKI and 87 were receiving dasatinib (27), bosutinib (32), imatinib (19), or nilotinib (9). Median platelet count was 227×109/L and 74 of 91 had counts above 150×109/L. Only 3 patients had abnormal routine coagulation testing, all receiving imatinib plus warfarin with prolonged PT; the other 88 had median PT 11.1 s, aPTT 28.5 s, and fibrinogen 470 mg/dL. Twelve patients were on aspirin and had faulty AA aggregation; all 4 off-TKI patients had normal aggregation. No patient was bleeding at the time of testing.

After excluding aspirin, dasatinib was associated with frequent epinephrine and AA defects. Bosutinib was largely normal, although the source contains an internal arithmetic inconsistency: it reports 21 of 26 patients as 85% normal (21/26 = 80.8%). Imatinib showed AA impairment in 10 of 15 and epinephrine impairment in 2 of 15; all 7 nilotinib patients were normal. Two dasatinib patients normalized abnormal epinephrine aggregation after switching to bosutinib. In normal blood, dasatinib rapidly prolonged collagen/epinephrine PFA closure time, while imatinib did not. The mechanistic discussion is hypothesis-generating and invokes cyclooxygenase-1 and platelet-kinase pathways without directly testing them.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that dasatinib inhibits AA- and epinephrine-induced platelet aggregation and may thereby contribute to bleeding despite preserved platelet counts and secondary hemostasis. They also conclude that imatinib can cause AA-related platelet dysfunction, though less frequently.

The mechanism is explicitly unresolved. The source therefore provides mechanistic/phenotypic evidence of platelet dysfunction rather than a validated prediction rule for clinical bleeding or a management pathway.

Authorship and conflicts

Kantarjian and Cortes disclosed research grant support from Novartis and Bristol-Myers Squibb; the other authors declared no competing financial interests. The article reports the authors' research and writing contributions.

Evaluation and appraisal

Clinical question / PICO-like framing: In patients with CML-CP exposed to different TKIs, are platelet-function abnormalities detectable by aggregometry, and are dasatinib effects distinguishable from other TKIs or reproduced ex vivo? The study's outcomes are laboratory platelet-function measures, not adjudicated bleeding outcomes.

Relevance 8/10

Rationale: Directly relevant to TKI-associated hemostasis and interpretation of bleeding with normal routine coagulation tests.

Evidence basis: 91-patient CML-CP cohort plus platelet-function testing across four TKIs.

Principal limit: Narrow disease setting and surrogate platelet-function endpoints.

Would change score: A current, larger study linking platelet-function abnormalities to adjudicated clinical bleeding would raise the score.

Novelty 8/10

Rationale: Historically strong novelty because the authors framed this as the first demonstration of marked, reversible TKI-associated platelet aggregation inhibition.

Evidence basis: Comparative LTA findings, two switch cases, and an ex vivo PFA-100 experiment.

Principal limit: Novelty is historical; the article was published in 2009.

Would change score: Later independent replication and mechanism-specific studies would strengthen enduring novelty.

Method strength 5/10

Rationale: The laboratory strategy is coherent, but the clinical comparison is nonrandomized and cross-sectional.

Evidence basis: Standard aggregometry, aspirin-excluded subgroup analyses, off-TKI observations, and a supporting normal-donor experiment.

Principal limit: Small unequal groups, treatment-selection bias, heterogeneous doses, lack of paired baseline testing for most patients, and single-donor PFA time-course methods.

Would change score: Prospective paired testing, prespecified clinical endpoints, multiple donors, and blinded/repeated laboratory measurements would raise the score.

Evidence strength 6/10

Rationale: The source supports an association between dasatinib and abnormal laboratory platelet function more strongly than it supports clinical bleeding causation.

Evidence basis: Large within-group abnormality proportions with dasatinib, comparative Figure 1 statistics, reversible findings after switching in two patients, and ex vivo PFA-100 prolongation.

Principal limit: No confidence intervals, no contemporaneous bleeding events, surrogate endpoints, and one internal bosutinib percentage/count inconsistency.

Would change score: A prospective exposure-response study correlating laboratory defects with bleeding outcomes would raise the score.

External validity 4/10

Rationale: Generalizability is limited to a single-center, 2009-era CML-CP population with small drug-specific analytic subsets.

Evidence basis: Multiple TKIs and several dose schedules were represented.

Principal limit: Single center, selected post-imatinib-failure patients, small non-aspirin subgroups, and historical treatment patterns.

Would change score: Multicenter replication across broader contemporary TKI populations would raise the score.

Implementation value 5/10

Rationale: Useful for recognizing possible platelet dysfunction when routine coagulation tests and platelet counts are unrevealing, but insufficient alone for treatment decisions.

Evidence basis: Provides specific agonist patterns and a rapid PFA-100 dasatinib signal.

Principal limit: Does not define a validated diagnostic pathway, bleeding-risk threshold, or management algorithm.

Would change score: Current comparative safety data and validated clinical management guidance would raise the score.

Strengths

  • Direct comparison of several TKIs within one clinical disease setting.
  • Aspirin exposure was identified and excluded from the main drug-specific aggregation analyses.
  • Routine PT/aPTT/fibrinogen and platelet counts help distinguish primary from secondary hemostatic abnormalities.
  • Two switch cases provide within-person evidence of reversibility.
  • Normal-donor ex vivo PFA-100 testing provides a mechanistically supportive experiment independent of CML disease status.

Limitations and validity concerns

  • Nonrandomized, cross-sectional treatment comparisons are vulnerable to confounding by treatment history, disease response, and indication.
  • Most patients lacked paired pre-TKI platelet-function testing; the strongest causal evidence is limited to two switch cases and the ex vivo experiment.
  • Drug-specific analytic groups were small: dasatinib 27, bosutinib 26, imatinib 15, nilotinib 7 after aspirin exclusion.
  • No patient was bleeding at the time of aggregation testing, so the study does not directly estimate the probability that abnormal aggregometry predicts clinical bleeding.
  • The PFA time-course Methods specify a single normal donor, limiting reproducibility/generalizability of that experiment.
  • No confidence intervals are reported for the main proportions.
  • The article reports 21/26 bosutinib patients as 85% normal; the count corresponds to 80.8%, creating a source-level numeric inconsistency.
  • Mechanistic explanations involving cyclooxygenase-1 or SRC-family pathways are speculative in this paper.
Do not overclaim: This study does not show that imatinib or dasatinib changes venous thromboembolism risk, does not quantify bleeding risk under anticoagulation, and does not validate a TKI-switch strategy for bleeding. Those questions require separate evidence.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Useful signal

In a dasatinib-treated patient with mucocutaneous bleeding and otherwise unrevealing platelet count/PT/aPTT, drug-associated platelet dysfunction is biologically plausible based on this source.

Imatinib nuance

Imatinib was not uniformly normal: 10/15 evaluable non-aspirin patients had impaired AA-induced aggregation, although the PFA-100 experiment did not show the rapid collagen/epinephrine effect seen with dasatinib.

Comparative interpretation

Nilotinib and bosutinib appeared less disruptive in this small cohort, but the study is not adequate for a contemporary comparative safety ranking.

Decision boundary

Use the paper as mechanistic/historical support, not as a stand-alone basis for changing TKI therapy, antiplatelet/anticoagulant dosing, or bleeding management.
