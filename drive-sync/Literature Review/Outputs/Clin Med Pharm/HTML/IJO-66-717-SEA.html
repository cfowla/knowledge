One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Optic nerve aplasia can present unilaterally or bilaterally with heterogeneous ocular findings; associated CNS abnormalities are not obligatory even in bilateral disease, so systemic assessment and neuroimaging are important parts of evaluation.

The authors describe three children with optic nerve aplasia (ONA). Case 1 had unilateral right-sided ONA with no significant brain anomaly and a normal left eye. Case 2 had unilateral left-sided ONA with partial aniridia, chorioretinal atrophy and markedly elevated left intraocular pressure (50 mmHg). Case 3 had bilateral ONA with bilateral microcornea, additional anterior-segment anomalies, and MRI-confirmed bilateral absence of the optic nerves and chiasm despite no reported brain abnormality.

The paper's strongest contribution is descriptive: it shows clinically varied phenotypes documented by fundus examination, ultrasonography and neuroimaging. Its principal limitation is inherent to a three-case report series: there is no sampling frame, denominator, comparator, standardized protocol, longitudinal outcome analysis or causal inference. The authors' recommendation for systemic evaluation and neuroimaging is clinically plausible and consistent with the concern for associated anomalies, but this case series alone cannot quantify benefit or define an optimal diagnostic pathway.

Section condensation

Abstract and introduction

ONA is described as a rare congenital developmental anomaly involving absence of the optic nerve head, retinal vessels, retinal ganglion cells and optic nerve fibers in a blind eye. The article frames unilateral disease as often occurring with otherwise normal brain development and bilateral disease as more often associated with CNS malformations, while acknowledging variation. The stated purpose is to report three cases with differing presentations.

Case 1

A 6-year-old girl had apparent small size and intermittent deviation of the right eye since birth. The right eye had no light perception, pseudoptosis, total afferent pupillary defect, 50 prism diopters exotropia and relative enophthalmos (8 mm right versus 10 mm left by exophthalmometry); the left eye had 20/20 vision and normal clinical findings.

Fundus examination showed absence of the right optic nerve and retinal vessels with chorioretinal atrophy and tessellation. B-scan lacked a right optic nerve shadow. CT showed right enophthalmos; MRI showed complete absence of the orbital right optic nerve in its expected location, while no significant brain anomaly was reported.

Case 2

A 5-year-old child had no vision in a small left eye since birth; the right eye had prior trauma and surgery. Best-corrected acuity was 20/40 in the right eye and no light perception in the left. The left cornea measured 12 mm vertically by 9 mm horizontally and partial aniridia was present.

The left fundus showed complete absence of the optic nerve and retinal vessels with macular and mid-peripheral chorioretinal atrophy. Intraocular pressure was 12 mmHg in the right eye and 50 mmHg in the left; the discussion characterizes this case as unilateral ONA with ipsilateral ocular anomalies and glaucoma.

Case 3

A 5-month-old girl evaluated for absent social smile did not fix or follow light. She had bilateral microcornea, persistent pupillary membrane in the right eye, and iris plus lens coloboma in the left. Fundus examination showed bilateral absence of the optic nerve heads and retinal vessels with tessellated retina.

B-scan did not show optic nerve head shadows in either eye; axial length was 20 mm bilaterally. MRI demonstrated bilateral absence of the optic nerves and chiasm, with no brain abnormality reported.

Discussion and conclusion

The discussion reviews developmental theories and previously reported associations, including endocrine/CNS abnormalities and ocular anomalies. The authors emphasize heterogeneity across their three cases: isolated unilateral disease, unilateral disease with ipsilateral ocular abnormalities and glaucoma, and bilateral disease without a reported CNS abnormality.

The conclusion recommends thorough systemic evaluation and neuroimaging in children with ONA to assess for CNS malformations and endocrinological abnormalities. Financial support was reported as nil; the authors reported no conflicts of interest and stated that patient consent for publication was obtained.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Recognize ONA as a congenital cause of profound visual loss that may be unilateral or bilateral and may coexist with ocular anomalies.
Consider in evaluation
Fundus examination plus orbital/brain imaging can document absent optic nerve structures and assess associated anatomy.
Do not overclaim
This report cannot estimate how often CNS or endocrine abnormalities occur, nor whether any specific screening strategy improves outcomes.
Read next
The cited literature review and familial/genetic reports are higher-yield next sources for broader phenotype and genetic context.
