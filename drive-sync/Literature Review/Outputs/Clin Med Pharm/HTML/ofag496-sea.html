One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this 50-patient cohort, invasive listeriosis occurred predominantly as primary bloodstream infection or neurolisteriosis and carried 12% 90-day and 20% 1-year mortality. Host vulnerability and acute severity—especially immunocompromised status and ICU care within 24 hours—were the strongest observed 1-year prognostic signals, whereas adjunctive gentamicin showed no detectable survival benefit in these sparse, unadjusted data.
Who was affected?Median age 72; 56% immunocompromised. Primary bloodstream infection accounted for 44%, neurolisteriosis 36%, endovascular infection 12%, peritonitis 6%, and pregnancy-associated infection 2%.
Diagnostic signalBlood cultures were positive in 38/45 (84%) when obtained. In neurolisteriosis, blood culture remained positive in 6/8 cases with negative CSF culture. CSF PCR added diagnoses but was discordant with culture in half of the eight patients tested by both methods.
Treatment signal94% received beta-lactam-based definitive therapy; 46% aminopenicillin monotherapy and 30% ampicillin plus gentamicin. The source reports no ampicillin or TMP-SMX resistance among tested isolates.
Outcome signal30-day mortality 10%, 90-day 12%, 1-year 20%. Immunocompromised status (HR 9.54) and ICU care within 24 h (HR 4.65) were associated with 1-year mortality, with very wide confidence intervals.
Do not overread treatment effects. The apparent 90-day survival advantage with penicillin-based regimens was exploratory and unadjusted (log-rank p=0.0275), and the gentamicin analysis was vulnerable to confounding by indication, variable exposure duration, and few events.

Verdict: Read soon; do not use alone for practice. The descriptive data are clinically useful for recognizing invasive listeriosis and understanding diagnostic yield, but the cohort is too small and nonrandomized to resolve optimal combination therapy.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame invasive listeriosis as an uncommon but high-mortality foodborne infection with limited contemporary evidence on clinical presentation, antimicrobial strategy, and prognostic factors. They retrospectively studied adults with laboratory-confirmed invasive L. monocytogenes infection at Mayo Clinic Health System sites from 1980–2024, categorized syndromes, and evaluated 1-year mortality as the primary endpoint.

Among 50 patients, median age was 72 and 56% were immunocompromised. Bacteremia/primary bloodstream infection and neurolisteriosis predominated. Mortality was 10% at 30 days, 12% at 90 days, and 20% at 1 year. Immunocompromised status and ICU admission were associated with higher 1-year mortality; adjunctive gentamicin was not associated with better survival. The abstract appropriately calls for larger multicenter studies.

Introduction

The introduction emphasizes older age, pregnancy, and immunocompromise as major risk contexts, and notes uncertainty around adjunctive aminoglycoside therapy despite ampicillin-based treatment being conventional. The stated evidence gap is the scarcity of large contemporary cohorts capable of clarifying presentation, diagnostic patterns, treatment strategies, and outcomes.

The study objective is primarily descriptive and prognostic, with a specific interest in longer-term mortality and gentamicin exposure. This is important because the design can characterize associations but cannot resolve a causal treatment question without stronger control of confounding.

Methods

Adults with laboratory-confirmed invasive infection from a normally sterile site were identified through microbiology records from 1980 through 2024. The authors abstracted demographic, comorbidity, immune-status, clinical, exposure, diagnostic, antimicrobial, and outcome data. Cases were assigned a single predominant syndrome using explicit definitions for primary bloodstream infection, CNS infection, endovascular infection, peritonitis, and pregnancy-associated infection. The immunocompromised definition included malignancy, immunosuppressive treatment/chronic steroids, transplantation, asplenia, or HIV; diabetes and CKD alone did not qualify.

One-year all-cause mortality was primary; 90-day mortality and residual neurologic deficit at discharge were secondary. Descriptive statistics used medians/IQRs and counts/percentages; group testing used chi-square/Fisher or Kruskal-Wallis. Cox models were intentionally univariable because of few deaths; selected treatment analyses used logistic regression where sparse data constrained modeling. This is a defensible anti-overfitting choice, but it sharply limits causal interpretation. IRB approval included waiver of consent.

Results — cohort and presentation

Fifty patients were identified. Median age was 72 years, 52% were male, and 56% were immunocompromised. Fever occurred in 62%; 34% required ICU care within 24 hours, and 86% received infectious-diseases consultation. An exposure history was identified in 27 patients (52%); undercooked/processed meats and unpasteurized dairy were the most commonly described exposures, but the detailed exposure table is in unavailable supplementary material.

Primary bloodstream infection (44%) and neurolisteriosis (36%) were most common, followed by endovascular infection (12%), peritonitis (6%), and pregnancy-associated infection (2%). Median time from symptom onset to presentation was 2.5 days, and to microbiologic diagnosis was 4 days.

Results — diagnostic yield and focal disease

Blood cultures were positive in 38/45 (84%) when obtained. Among neurolisteriosis cases, CSF culture was positive in 8/17 (47%) and CSF PCR in 5/8 (63%). Of 14 with both CSF and blood culture, 10 had positive blood cultures; importantly, 6/8 patients with negative CSF cultures still had positive blood cultures. In the eight patients with both CSF PCR and culture, four were discordant, and two additional cases were diagnosed by CSF PCR alone despite negative CSF and blood cultures.

Rhombencephalitis was reported in 5/13 (39%) imaged neurolisteriosis cases in Results text. Six endovascular cases included three endocarditis and three graft infections; 5/6 had prosthetic cardiovascular material and 4/6 had prior abdominal-aortic-aneurysm repair. All three peritonitis cases were immunocompromised and had positive peritoneal-fluid cultures.

Results — treatment and outcomes

Forty-seven patients (94%) received beta-lactam-based definitive therapy. Table 1 lists aminopenicillin monotherapy in 23 (46%), ampicillin plus gentamicin in 15 (30%), and ampicillin plus TMP-SMX in 7 (14%). Treatment duration varied by syndrome: median 14 days for bloodstream infection, 21 for neurolisteriosis, 42 for endovascular infection, and 28 for peritonitis. The source contains an unresolved overall-duration IQR discrepancy between Results text and Table 1.

Mortality was 10% at 30 days, 12% at 90 days, and 20% at 1 year. Immunocompromised status (HR 9.54, 95% CI 1.07–84.95) and ICU care within 24 hours (HR 4.65, 95% CI 1.10–19.71) were associated with higher 1-year mortality. Neurolisteriosis produced residual neurologic sequelae in 8/18 (44.4%); ICU admission was associated with sequelae (OR 18.9, 95% CI 1.92–650.3). Confidence intervals are extremely wide, reflecting sparse events.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret host factors and illness severity as stronger outcome drivers than antimicrobial regimen and emphasize the potential diagnostic value of blood cultures and CSF PCR in neurolisteriosis. They also discuss the concern that standard empiric cephalosporin-heavy meningitis regimens do not provide reliable Listeria coverage, a source claim that is not independently established by this cohort itself.

The limitations are substantial: retrospective design; small cohort; few deaths; largely univariate analyses; residual confounding; nonrandom treatment allocation; variable gentamicin exposure; evolving diagnostics and clinical care across 45 years; missing specimen-timing information; binary neurologic outcome ascertainment; underpowered temporal comparisons; and 1-year all-cause mortality that may reflect competing comorbidity. Supplementary materials needed for some claims were unavailable in the supplied source set.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that immunocompromise and ICU-level illness at presentation were associated with higher 1-year mortality, that blood culture and molecular CSF testing can be complementary in neurolisteriosis, and that aminopenicillin-based therapy remained central in this cohort. They found no survival benefit from adjunctive gentamicin but explicitly call for larger multicenter prospective studies.

Strengths

  • Very long case-accrual period for a rare invasive infection.
  • Explicit clinical-syndrome and immune-status definitions.
  • Diagnostic details that show complementarity and discordance among blood culture, CSF culture, and CSF PCR.
  • Transparent recognition that sparse mortality events precluded multivariable Cox modeling.
  • Endovascular case table provides clinically reusable phenotype and management detail.

Major limitations

  • Retrospective observational design with treatment-selection bias and residual confounding.
  • Only 50 patients and 10 one-year deaths; unstable effect estimates and extremely wide CIs.
  • Forty-five years of changing diagnostics, supportive care, and antimicrobial practice introduce era heterogeneity.
  • Unresolved source discrepancies in setting description, treatment-duration IQR, gentamicin/Table 4 linkage, and Figure 1 numbers.
  • Supplementary Tables 1–5 and Supplementary Figure 1 were cited but unavailable for reconciliation.
  • Accepted-manuscript status means final production copy may differ.

Clinical / implementation translation

What the study can support. Maintain diagnostic suspicion in older or immunocompromised adults with compatible invasive infection; do not rely on fever as a universal feature; obtain blood cultures when invasive listeriosis is suspected; recognize that CSF culture and PCR may be discordant; and treat endovascular/prosthetic-material disease as a distinct high-complexity phenotype. These are source-informed implications, not independent guideline recommendations.
External currency check. CDC’s current “Caring for Patients with Listeriosis” page (updated August 12, 2024) describes standard therapy for presumptive invasive listeriosis as typically including IV ampicillin plus gentamicin for 14–21 days in nonallergic patients, while explicitly stating that its exposure-management framework is not intended as a practice guideline. That contemporary public-health framing does not establish gentamicin efficacy, but it means this small cohort’s null association should not be used by itself to remove adjunctive gentamicin from a treatment protocol.
What should not change from this paper alone. Do not infer that gentamicin is ineffective, that penicillin-based therapy is causally superior, or that the syndrome-specific durations shown here are universally optimal. Those questions require stronger comparative evidence and site-specific/current guidance.

Best use

  • Rare-disease clinical reference and teaching.
  • Support for diagnostic reasoning around blood culture and CSF molecular testing.
  • Context for designing a larger multicenter listeriosis registry or comparative-effectiveness study.
  • Hypothesis generation for which host and severity markers deserve prospective validation.

Do not use for

  • Standalone antimicrobial-policy changes.
  • Causal claims about gentamicin, corticosteroids, or penicillin-regimen survival effects.
  • Precise syndrome-specific mortality prediction.
