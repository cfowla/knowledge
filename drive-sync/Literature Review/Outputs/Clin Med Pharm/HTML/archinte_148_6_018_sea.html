One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first — for historical heparin process-of-care evidence.
The paper is unusually concrete about how ordering behavior, laboratory timing, and reactions to low/high PTT values can keep IV heparin therapy outside the study-defined target. Its strongest value is operational and historical, not as a current dosing standard.

Core thesis. In 65 patients physicians intended to anticoagulate for PE/DVT, therapeutic intensity by the paper's standard (PTT >1.5× control) was often delayed. Only 19/65 (29%) received heparin at initial clinical suspicion; initial bolus and infusion doses averaged 5861 U and 1026 U/h; the first PTT was measured a mean 11.7 hours after starting heparin; and only about 40%-41% of first PTTs were in range. The fraction of therapeutic PTTs rose slowly and exceeded 90% only around day 8.

Why it matters. The study decomposes poor target attainment into specific workflow behaviors. After 208 low-PTT episodes, no dose change was followed by a therapeutic next PTT only 22% of the time, infusion increase alone 51%, and bolus-based responses 69%. Conversely, after 45 PTTs >100 s, the next PTT was subtherapeutic in 25 episodes (56%), consistent with frequent overcorrection. These process findings support the logic of explicit, timely, standardized feedback loops.

Clinical outcomes are much less secure. Any bleeding occurred in 12/65 (18.5%; 7 major, 5 minor). The authors found no correlation of PTT level or infusion rate with bleeding in this small dataset. There were 11 combined proved/suspected recurrences (17%), but only 2 were proved, and the paper explicitly states it could not determine whether study-defined subtherapeutic anticoagulation caused clinical morbidity.

Critical limitation: the paper does not validate PTT >1.5× control as a necessary or desirable therapeutic target, and it does not establish that delayed target attainment caused recurrence. Its dose recommendations are author interpretations from a small retrospective cohort.

Section-by-section condensation

Introduction / clinical problem

The authors start from the accepted effectiveness of anticoagulation for PE/DVT but emphasize uncertainty in method and intensity and concern about hemorrhage/thrombocytopenia. Their local observation was that anticoagulation was neither rapidly achieved nor consistently sustained despite physician intent to fully anticoagulate.

The study therefore focuses on behavior and process rather than efficacy of heparin itself: when therapy was started, how it was dosed, when PTT was checked, and how physicians reacted to values below or above the study-defined target.

Patients and Methods

Radiology records at three affiliated hospitals were reviewed for six months. Patients were identified from high-probability ventilation-perfusion scans, venography showing proximal DVT, or pulmonary arteriography demonstrating PE. Inclusion required an abnormal test and physician intent to anticoagulate with heparin; chart review was independent of active care.

The investigators recorded demographics, risk factors, PT/PTT, hematocrit, platelet counts, and all heparin/warfarin orders. Major bleeding, minor bleeding, suspected/proved recurrence, subtherapeutic PTT (<1.5× control), high PTT (>100 s), and a study definition of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia were prespecified. Timing used order-entry times. Group comparisons used ANOVA and t tests with P<.05; data were means ± SEM.

Results — population and timing

The cohort comprised 65 patients: 39 men and 26 women; 38 had PE alone, 17 DVT alone, and 10 both. Symptoms preceded diagnostic testing by a mean 98.4 hours. Only 19 patients (29%) received heparin before diagnostic confirmation; among those not empirically treated, the diagnostic-test-to-heparin interval averaged 13.1 hours.

Mean hospital stay was about 17 days and mean heparin duration 8.8 days. Six patients died; the authors attributed one death to thromboembolism. They reported no detectable medical-versus-surgical difference in the physician treatment practices studied.

Results — initial heparin and PTT attainment

Initial bolus dose averaged 5861 U (range 2000-15000), with no bolus in 5/65 patients. Initial infusion averaged 1026 U/h (range 800-2000). The first PTT was obtained a mean 11.7 hours after heparin initiation. Results text reports 40% of first PTTs >1.5× control; Figure 1 caption reports 41%.

First-PTT response correlated with cumulative weight-normalized heparin dose. Above roughly 25 U/kg/h before the first PTT, only three patients remained subtherapeutic. Across therapy, the percentage of PTTs above 1.5× control rose gradually and exceeded 90% only after about eight days.

Results — physician response to low/high PTT

Across 208 low-PTT episodes, four response patterns were observed. No change was followed by a therapeutic next PTT in 22%, infusion increase alone in 51%, bolus alone in 69%, and bolus plus infusion increase in 69%. PTT rose significantly after all three forms of dose intensification compared with baseline.

There were 45 PTTs >100 s. Five responses were observed (increase infusion, no change, hold + decrease, hold + increase, hold/discontinue). Regardless of response, subsequent PTTs fell; overall 25/45 (56%) follow-up PTTs were <1.5× control. In discussion, the authors report that therapy was interrupted or decreased in 80% of high-PTT cases.

Results — bleeding, recurrence, warfarin

Seven patients had major bleeding and five minor bleeding (12/65, 18.5%). Three of the seven major bleeds occurred in patients who also met the study's HIT definition. Major-bleeding patients were older on average, but age differences were not significant; no gender difference or correlation of PTT/infusion rate with bleeding was detected.

There were 2 proved and 9 suspected recurrences (~17% combined). Six suspected recurrences were not re-imaged and three had negative repeat radiology; timing and mean heparin dose did not distinguish recurrence cases. Warfarin was given to 46 patients, begun 4.8 days after heparin on average; 15/46 (32.6%) lacked PT >1.5× baseline at discharge.

Comment / authors' interpretation

The authors argue that their observed practice patterns repeatedly delayed or reduced anticoagulation: failure to treat at clinical suspicion, low initial bolus/infusion dosing, delayed first PTT, inadequate escalation after low PTT, and excessive reductions after high PTT. They interpret these patterns as reflecting incomplete understanding of heparin kinetics and excessive fear of bleeding.

They propose that a commonly used 5000-U bolus may be too low and suggest 7500-10000 U, with higher continuous-infusion dosing than observed. These are historical author recommendations, not validated by a prospective protocol in this study. The paper explicitly acknowledges that the clinical significance of its “subtherapeutic” PTT category was not measured.

Evaluation and appraisal

9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses IV heparin initiation, monitoring, dose-response, bleeding, thrombocytopenia, recurrence, and physician response patterns in VTE.

Evidence basis: The full paper is centered on heparin management in PE/DVT.

Limiting factor: Its dosing/monitoring framework is historical rather than a current therapeutic standard.

What would raise it: Would increase with explicit linkage to validated contemporary monitoring targets and modern treatment pathways.

6/10

Novelty

Rationale: The paper contributes a detailed process-of-care audit linking specific prescribing responses to subsequent PTT behavior.

Evidence basis: It decomposes delays into initial dosing, monitoring latency, and responses to low/high PTT.

Limiting factor: Novelty is judged as a historical contribution; no external literature comparison was performed here.

What would raise it: Would increase with prospective testing of a corrective protocol and patient-centered outcomes.

5/10

Method strength

Rationale: The chart-review design captures real physician behavior across three affiliated hospitals and uses explicit operational definitions.

Evidence basis: N=65, systematic radiology-based case identification, full order/PTT review, explicit definitions, ANOVA/t tests.

Limiting factor: Retrospective, nonrandomized, selected to patients physicians chose to anticoagulate, and several clinical outcomes were weakly confirmed.

What would raise it: Would increase with prospective protocolized data collection, prespecified analyses, and stronger outcome adjudication.

5/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Descriptive process findings are well supported, but causal claims about clinical harm from subtherapeutic PTT are not established.

Evidence basis: Large numbers of PTT episodes support process observations; authors explicitly acknowledge the causal-outcome gap.

Limiting factor: Only two recurrences were proved, bleeding analyses were small, and therapeutic-target validity was not tested.

What would raise it: Would increase with adequately powered clinical outcomes tied prospectively to exposure/monitoring states.

4/10

External validity

Rationale: Three hospitals and multiple services broaden the practice sample somewhat, but all were affiliated and the data were from 1985-1986.

Evidence basis: Medical and surgical patients were included, cared for by physicians trained at various institutions.

Limiting factor: Single affiliated system, small cohort, historical practice environment, and source-specific PTT definitions.

What would raise it: Would increase with multicenter replication across independent systems and laboratories.

7/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper is highly useful for understanding why standardized heparin nomograms and timely feedback loops are operationally attractive.

Evidence basis: It identifies five concrete failure modes and quantifies consequences of several physician responses.

Limiting factor: Its specific dose suggestions should not be treated as current dosing guidance without external verification.

What would raise it: Would increase with a validated protocol, explicit current assay calibration, and prospective safety/effectiveness testing.

Validity and interpretation

Strengths: explicit case-finding period; granular order/PTT linkage; clear operational definitions; repeated characterization of low- and high-PTT response behavior; transparent acknowledgment that PTT target validity and clinical consequences were not established.

Main threats: retrospective design; physician-selected treatment; only 65 patients; measurements nested within patients without a modern repeated-measures model; three affiliated hospitals rather than independent centers; weak confirmation of recurrent events; historical HIT definition without confirmatory testing; and internal 40% vs 41% discrepancy for the first-PTT therapeutic fraction.

Do not use for: selecting a current heparin bolus/infusion dose, defining a current therapeutic aPTT/PTT target, or estimating causal bleeding/recurrence risk from target attainment.
Best use: historical evidence for why anticoagulation protocols emphasize prompt initiation, adequate initial dosing, timely laboratory reassessment, explicit dose-response rules, and avoidance of overshooting corrective actions.

Confidence in appraisal: high for source-faithful summary and process findings; moderate for overall clinical interpretation because no external/current-practice verification was requested or performed.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Design implicationHeparin management is a feedback-control problem: initiation dose, assay timing, and response magnitude interact. Protocols should make those transitions explicit.
Monitoring implicationLong delay to the first assay can extend an initially low-exposure state; the paper's mean first PTT was 11.7 hours.
Adjustment implicationSmall or absent responses to low PTT and large responses to high PTT both produced poor target continuity in this cohort.
Safety implicationBleeding and thrombocytopenia were clinically important, but this small observational dataset cannot define a safe contemporary PTT ceiling.
Evidence-graph valueThe paper supplies atomizable process claims, response→next-assay results, adverse events, limitations, and an explicit author model of failure modes.
Next evidence needFor current implementation, pair this historical source with externally verified contemporary guidance/nomograms and modern assay targets.
