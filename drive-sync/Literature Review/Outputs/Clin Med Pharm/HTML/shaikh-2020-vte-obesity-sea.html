One-page synthesis

Core thesis

In obese trauma and surgical patients, weight-adjusted or higher fixed enoxaparin dosing more often achieves the study-defined prophylactic AFXa range than standard fixed dosing, but this pharmacodynamic improvement was not consistently accompanied by fewer VTE events. High fixed-dose strategies produced a recurring bleeding signal. The review therefore supports re-examining fixed-dose prophylaxis in obesity but does not identify a clinically validated “best” regimen.

Search and selection

The authors followed PRISMA, searched PubMed, EMBASE, Google Scholar, JAMA Network, CINAHL, Cochrane, and SAGE Journals, and hand-searched references. Search terms included VTE prophylaxis, pulmonary embolism, DVT, enoxaparin, obesity, bariatric, trauma, and surgery. English-language original studies were eligible if the exact chemoprophylaxis regimen and either VTE or AFXa outcomes were reported. Outpatient studies and secondary-literature formats were excluded.

Two reviewers (S.S. and A.E.) performed searching, screening, and data extraction. Extracted fields included study design, sample, population, age, BMI/weight, prophylaxis agent/regimen, target AFXa, limitations, and outcomes. Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment was applied to RCTs; GRADE was used to assess certainty of evidence. The paper states that heterogeneity and low-quality evidence prevented subgroup analysis.

Method appraisal. The review has a broad database search, explicit inclusion criteria, two-reviewer extraction, PRISMA structure, and formal evidence grading. However, the article does not present a pooled meta-analysis, a uniform clinical endpoint definition, a uniform AFXa target, or a common follow-up window. Protocol registration and a formal publication-bias assessment are not reported in the main article.

Results by dosing strategy

Study selection and evidence quality

The review screened 5,083 records, retained 206 after duplicate/irrelevant-title removal, assessed 133 full-text articles, and included 45 studies. Four were RCTs. Two RCTs were judged to have “fair” risk of bias and two low risk. Overall GRADE quality was low; BMI-stratified and continuous-infusion evidence was rated very low.

Weight-based signals

  • Bickford et al.: obese trauma patients, enoxaparin 0.5 mg/kg q12h with adjustment; 86% reached target AFXa, while DVT incidence was 21%.
  • Ludwig et al.: obese surgical ICU (61% trauma), 0.5 mg/kg q12h with adjustment; 91% target AFXa, DVT 4%, one bleeding event.
  • Al Otaib et al.: obese surgical patients, 0.5 mg/kg daily; 88% target AFXa, no VTE or major bleeding reported.
  • Stephenson RCT: post-cesarean 0.5 mg/kg BID vs 40 mg daily; 88% vs 14% target AFXa; neither arm reported VTE or bleeding.
  • Overcash: morbidly obese cesarean patients, 0.5 mg/kg q12h vs BMI-stratified 40/60 mg q12h; 86% vs 26% target AFXa; no VTE in either cohort.
  • Parikh: one fatal gastrointestinal bleed occurred in the adjusted-dose study despite an AFXa of 0.23 IU/mL, illustrating that “in-range” AFXa did not guarantee safety.

High fixed-dose safety signal

One bariatric cohort (Scholten) reported VTE reduction from 5.4% with enoxaparin 30 mg q12h to 0.6% with 40 mg q12h (p<0.01), although operative time was longer in the lower-dose group. Conversely, a separate bariatric comparison found bleeding increasing across low-, intermediate-, and high-dose groups (4.5%, 6%, and 15%), with no corresponding VTE reduction. The review therefore judged HFD regimens to increase bleeding without a reproducible clinical VTE benefit.

Authors’ recommendations

PICO 1 — Weight-adjusted versus alternatives

Weight-based regimens may be superior to BMI-stratified and standard fixed-dose regimens for achieving target AFXa; weight-stratified regimens may also improve AFXa target attainment.

Recommendation: Weak (2B)

Crucially, superiority is framed around AFXa—not demonstrated reduction in VTE.

PICO 2 — High fixed-dose versus standard fixed-dose

High fixed-dose regimens were associated with more bleeding without a corresponding reproducible decrease in VTE incidence.

Recommendation: Weak (2B)

The signal is directionally consistent across several studies but arises from heterogeneous regimens and populations.

Claim boundary. The review does not show that targeting AFXa improves patient-centered outcomes. Its strongest evidence concerns pharmacodynamic target attainment; the clinical endpoint evidence is weaker.

Strengths, limitations, and failure modes

Strengths
  • Systematic, multi-database search with PRISMA flow.
  • Explicit dosing-regimen focus rather than generic obesity/VTE review.
  • Separates weight-based, weight-tiered, BMI-tiered, fixed-dose, and infusion strategies.
  • Captures efficacy surrogate (AFXa), clinical VTE, and bleeding.
  • Formal risk-of-bias/GRADE framework and transparent acknowledgement of low certainty.
  • Useful regimen-level tables that preserve actual doses and populations.
Limitations
  • Only four RCTs; most evidence observational and small.
  • Most studies were not powered for VTE and used AFXa as the primary outcome.
  • AFXa target range was not standardized and its link to VTE/bleeding was unproven.
  • Follow-up varied from inpatient-only to 30 or 90 days; some loss to follow-up.
  • Bleeding definitions/reporting were inconsistent; some studies did not report bleeding.
  • Population heavily weighted toward bariatric surgery; trauma-specific evidence was limited.
  • Drug, dosing, timing, and post-discharge duration varied widely.
  • English-language restriction and no reported formal publication-bias analysis.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for evidence mapping; do not use alone for practice.

This is a high-relevance paper for understanding why obesity-specific prophylaxis strategies emerged and how AFXa-guided or weight-adjusted dosing compared with older fixed regimens. Its clinical authority is limited by the quality and heterogeneity of the underlying studies, especially the reliance on AFXa rather than symptomatic VTE or major bleeding.

What would raise confidence?

Large multicenter randomized trials in obese trauma and non-bariatric surgical populations, with standardized major-bleeding definitions, protocolized timing and missed-dose accounting, prespecified AFXa targets, and clinical VTE as a primary endpoint. A patient-level analysis linking dose/exposure to both VTE and bleeding would be more informative than cross-study AFXa target attainment alone.

Practice translation

Use this paper for

  • Historical rationale for moving beyond enoxaparin 30 mg BID in heavier trauma patients.
  • Cataloging obesity-specific prophylaxis strategies.
  • Identifying anti-Xa monitoring as a dosing/PK surrogate, not a validated patient-centered endpoint.
  • Recognizing bleeding tradeoffs with empiric high fixed doses.
  • Generating protocol questions for trauma/pharmacy committees.

Do not use it for

  • Declaring 0.5 mg/kg BID universally superior.
  • Choosing a universal AFXa target range.
  • Assuming “target AFXa” guarantees lower VTE or bleeding.
  • Extrapolating bariatric surgery findings directly to all trauma patients.
  • Establishing current 2026 practice without newer guidance/evidence.

Pharmacy-specific reading

  • The most useful signal is dose–exposure mismatch with fixed dosing in obesity.
  • The most important safety counterweight is bleeding with intensified fixed regimens.
  • Any protocol should distinguish initial dose selection from subsequent anti-Xa adjustment.
  • Clinical endpoint surveillance and missed-dose processes matter at least as much as target attainment.

External currency verification

Verification performed August 10, 2026. A Western Trauma Association critical-decisions algorithm published after this review (2020; DOI 10.1097/TA.0000000000002830) states that enoxaparin 40 mg twice daily should be considered the standard initial dose for most trauma patients because 30 mg twice daily often provides inadequate prophylaxis; the algorithm also discusses higher/weight-based strategies and anti-Xa adjustment. A 2022 trauma VTE consensus conference continued to emphasize substantial knowledge gaps and the need for better evidence. These later sources are directionally consistent with Shaikh et al.’s concern that older standard fixed dosing may be inadequate, but they also reinforce that this systematic review should be treated as historical evidence synthesis rather than a current standalone protocol.
