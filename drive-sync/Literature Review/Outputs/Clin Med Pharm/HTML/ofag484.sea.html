Scope warning. This paper evaluates a clinic/national prescribing-policy change and subsequent RP-CT trends. It is not a randomized comparison of doxycycline versus azithromycin, does not separate reinfection from microbiologic persistence, and should not be used alone to infer individual-level treatment efficacy or causal effects outside this setting.

One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. The study is most useful as implementation-effectiveness evidence: after doxycycline-only rectal CT treatment was fully adopted, the quarterly RP-CT trend fell relative to RP-GC. It does not materially reopen the treatment-choice question; its value is showing delayed uptake and population-level trend change.

The authors used a controlled interrupted time series to evaluate whether the PHSKC clinic's 2016 shift toward exclusively doxycycline for rectal CT, followed by the 2021 national guideline change, was associated with fewer recurrent/persistent rectal CT infections. There were 211 RP-CT and 222 RP-GC infection episodes over 2012-2024. The primary adjusted model found no statistically significant immediate level changes, but the RP-CT trend declined significantly in the fully implemented period versus pre-implementation (0.853, 95% CI 0.765-0.951; p=.004). Relative to the RP-GC control trend, the corresponding trend-change estimate was 0.831 (95% CI 0.719-0.957; p=.011), interpreted as a 16.9% greater reduction in the quarterly RP-CT trend.

The causal case is strengthened by the control outcome, long pre/post series, seasonality/population adjustment, and sensitivity analyses, but remains limited by a single-site nonrandomized design, return-for-retesting selection, inability to distinguish reinfection from persistence, and concurrent doxy-PEP rollout. The 14-120-day sensitivity definition supported the direction and significance of the central trend result; excluding 2024 preserved a similar point estimate but lost significance. A notable manuscript inconsistency appears in the Discussion: it attributes the 16.9% relative trend reduction to fully implemented versus implementation, whereas the Results/Table 2 assign 16.9% to fully implemented versus pre-implementation and 10.8% to fully implemented versus implementation.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The structured abstract frames the study around evidence that doxycycline is more effective than azithromycin for rectal CT and the PHSKC clinic's mid-2016 move toward doxycycline-only treatment. The study uses clinic EHR data and RP-GC as a control outcome to estimate the impact of guideline implementation on RP-CT, while separately examining correlates of azithromycin prescribing.

The abstract highlights a significant 16.9% greater quarterly trend reduction in RP-CT relative to RP-GC for full implementation versus pre-implementation. It also notes that White identity and living with HIV were positively associated with azithromycin receipt after the guideline change.

Introduction

The rationale distinguishes older urogenital CT evidence—under which doxycycline and azithromycin were considered similarly efficacious—from accumulating rectal CT evidence favoring doxycycline. The clinic changed its rectal CT protocol beginning in April 2016; national guidance changed in 2021 after randomized trials strengthened the evidence base.

The primary question is whether these policy changes translated into fewer RP-CT events, not whether doxycycline is pharmacologically superior. A secondary question asks whether patient characteristics predicted continued azithromycin prescribing, which the authors discuss in relation to possible adherence assumptions and clinician bias.

Methods

The cITS includes MSM with treated rectal CT or GC from 2012-2024 and defines three segments: pre-implementation (Jan 2012-Jun 2016), implementation (Jul 2016-Sep 2021), and fully implemented (Oct 2021-Dec 2024). RP infection is a repeat positive NAAT 14-90 days after an initial diagnosis; CT/GC co-infections count in both groups. Quarterly counts are modeled with negative binomial segmented regression, quarterly seasonality indicators, and population offsets.

Potential confounders were evaluated with CT/GC-by-period interactions; age and White race were carried into the adjusted cITS. Sensitivity analyses used 14-30, 14-60, and 14-120-day RP windows, with 14-30 days ultimately not reported because only one fully implemented RP-CT event occurred. An ad hoc analysis excluded 2024 to assess doxy-PEP rollout. The prescribing-correlate analysis uses GEE with a binomial family and independent correlation structure for repeated CT cases.

Results

The primary dataset contained 211 RP-CT and 222 RP-GC infections. No level-change contrast reached statistical significance. RP-CT trend change was 0.944 for implementation versus pre (p=.065), 0.853 for fully implemented versus pre (p=.004), and 0.904 for fully implemented versus implementation (p=.050). RP-GC trends did not significantly change. The key relative RP-CT-versus-RP-GC trend estimate was 0.831 (95% CI 0.719-0.957; p=.011) for fully implemented versus pre; the relative fully implemented-versus-implementation estimate was 0.892 (p=.079).

Sensitivity results retained the same general direction. The 14-120-day definition produced significant relative trend reductions for fully implemented versus pre (0.870; p=.010) and fully implemented versus implementation (0.902; p=.030). Excluding 2024 yielded 0.838 (p=.072) for fully implemented versus pre. In the post-policy azithromycin analysis, White identity (OR 1.44; p=.023) and HIV diagnosis (OR 1.72; p=.021) were positively associated with azithromycin receipt, while Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander identity (OR 0.43; p=.017) and Hispanic/Latino ethnicity (OR 0.52; p<.001) were inversely associated; none of the before-versus-after interaction estimates was statistically significant.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the absence of immediate level changes and the later trend decline as evidence that guideline implementation required time and prescribing fidelity. They report that only about one-third of rectal CT patients received doxycycline in 2017, with prescribing rising incrementally to 93.1% during 2021. They argue that the RP-GC control increases confidence that observed CT trend changes were related to treatment-policy change rather than general secular effects.

Important limitations are explicit: return-for-retesting determines whether an RP event is observed; recurrent and persistent infections cannot be separated; doxy-PEP rollout affects CT and GC differently and overlaps the fully implemented segment; and the study is single-site. The paper therefore supports a policy-effectiveness association, not clean causal isolation of the doxycycline guideline change.

Evaluation and appraisal

Clinical question / PICO-like framing

Population: MSM treated for rectal CT or rectal GC at one Seattle/King County sexual health clinic, 2012-2024.
Intervention/exposure: clinic transition toward doxycycline-only rectal CT therapy, followed by full clinic implementation and 2021 national guideline alignment.
Comparator: earlier implementation segments; RP-GC used as a control outcome.
Outcome: quarterly recurrent/persistent rectal CT trend, defined by repeat positive NAAT 14-90 days after initial diagnosis.
Design: controlled interrupted time series using adjusted negative binomial segmented regression.
Safety: not evaluated; this is not a comparative adverse-event study.
8/10

Relevance

Directly relevant to STI pharmacotherapy and implementation effectiveness; it evaluates real-world consequences of moving from azithromycin-permissive to doxycycline-only rectal CT treatment.

Evidence basis: Longitudinal clinic data with a treatment-policy intervention and RP-GC control.

Limiting factor: It does not address a still-open treatment-selection question because doxycycline superiority is already established in the source context.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with multicenter implementation data or outcomes tied directly to contemporary stewardship decisions.

7/10

Novelty

Adds policy-implementation evidence beyond drug-efficacy trials by asking whether prescribing change altered recurrent/persistent rectal CT trends.

Evidence basis: Controlled interrupted time series across 2012-2024 plus prescribing-adoption context.

Limiting factor: The treatment comparison itself is not novel.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with clearer separation of implementation mechanisms and contemporaneous adoption measurements.

7/10

Method strength

The cITS design, control outcome, seasonality/population adjustment, confounder assessment, and sensitivity analyses are appropriate for a policy question.

Evidence basis: Negative binomial segmented regression; RP-GC control; age/race adjustment; alternative RP windows; 2024 exclusion.

Limiting factor: Nonrandomized single-site design; control is imperfect because doxy-PEP affects CT and GC differently; recurrent and persistent infections are combined.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with multiple control sites/outcomes, explicit pre-specified doxy-PEP handling, and linked adherence/treatment-completion data.

6/10

Evidence strength

The primary relative trend estimate is statistically significant and supported by some sensitivity analyses, but causal attribution remains limited.

Evidence basis: Fully implemented vs pre relative trend 0.831 (95% CI 0.719-0.957; p=.011).

Limiting factor: No significant level changes; 2024-excluded estimate loses significance; retesting selection and concurrent doxy-PEP remain; one text/table contrast is internally inconsistent.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with consistent replication, stronger negative controls, and validated differentiation of reinfection versus persistence.

4/10

External validity

The population and workflow are narrow: MSM attending one Seattle/King County sexual health clinic.

Evidence basis: Single clinic, specialized STI setting, local EHR and prescribing environment.

Limiting factor: Generalizability to women, other populations, primary care, and different STI systems is uncertain.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with multicenter or population-based replication.

7/10

Implementation value

The study clearly illustrates gradual uptake, delayed system-level effect, and the value of measuring policy fidelity rather than assuming immediate implementation.

Evidence basis: Only about one-third received doxycycline in 2017; prescribing reached 93.1% in 2021; level changes were not immediate.

Limiting factor: It offers limited operational detail on the intervention itself and cannot determine which change—evidence, clinic policy, national guideline, or time—drove uptake.

Would raise/lower: Would rise with granular implementation measures, clinician behavior data, and site-level rollout strategies.

Validity

Strengths: long time series, a plausibly informative control outcome, explicit segmentation, adjustment for seasonality and clinic denominator, confounder screening, multiple RP-window sensitivity analyses, and a doxy-PEP-oriented ad hoc analysis. Weaknesses: residual confounding remains possible; the control outcome is not immune to all concurrent STI interventions; RP ascertainment depends on retesting; repeated infections can arise from reinfection or persistence; and the full-implementation segment overlaps a major change in STI prevention.

Practice translation

The study supports the proposition that evidence-based treatment policies can take years to penetrate practice and that clinical outcomes may change gradually rather than immediately. It does not change the source-described preference for doxycycline in rectal CT; instead it adds implementation-level evidence consistent with the clinical efficacy literature. For practice or CDS work, the most reusable lesson is to measure adoption/fidelity and downstream outcomes separately, and to expect lag between guideline issuance and measurable population effect.

Bottom line: useful implementation-effectiveness evidence, but not sufficient by itself for treatment selection or broad causal claims. Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high because the full main manuscript and all displayed main-text tables/figure were available, but supplements were absent and the accepted manuscript contains one important internal numerical-contrast inconsistency.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Use as supporting evidence that a doxycycline-preferred policy can be associated with declining recurrent/persistent rectal CT trends after adoption reaches high fidelity.
Do not overclaim
Do not treat the cITS as a randomized efficacy comparison, and do not equate RP-CT with confirmed microbiologic treatment failure.
Operational lesson
Track treatment-policy uptake over time; the source's lack of immediate level changes is consistent with gradual prescribing adoption.
Monitoring lesson
When prevention programs such as doxy-PEP roll out, predefine analytic strategies because they may affect treatment-policy outcomes and control outcomes differently.
Evidence gap
Multicenter replication with better retesting capture and separation of reinfection/persistence would improve causal and external validity.
Read priority
Skim the methods and Tables 2-3 closely; Table 4 is secondary unless prescribing-bias or implementation behavior is the focus.
