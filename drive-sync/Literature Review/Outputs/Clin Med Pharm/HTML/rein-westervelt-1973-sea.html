One-page synthesis

Verdict: Skim deeply. This is a compact foundational renal pharmacokinetic study. Its main reusable finding is the strong relation between renal function and cefazolin elimination after a single intramuscular dose.

The investigators measured serum and urine cefazolin after 500 mg intramuscular dosing in healthy volunteers and patients with renal failure. In normal renal function, peak serum concentrations were 44 to 70 micrograms per milliliter and mean half-life was 1.60 hours. Half-life lengthened as renal function worsened, reaching 69.2 hours in one anuric observation. The total elimination constant correlated with creatinine clearance at r = 0.94. More than 90% of the dose was recovered in urine during the first 24 hours in normal subjects.

Three healthy volunteers were retested with probenecid. The authors reported a 20% to 40% increase in serum half-life and a 7% to 33% rise in peak serum concentration. They interpreted the combined clearance, probenecid, and urinary data as evidence that cefazolin is eliminated mainly by the kidney, mostly by glomerular filtration with a smaller tubular-secretory component.

Source-integrity issue. Participant counts do not reconcile. The abstract says 12 subjects. The methods describe five healthy volunteers plus nine renal-failure patients. Table 1 has 14 non-probenecid rows and lists C.L. twice at different creatinine clearances. The discussion also cites reference 18, but the bibliography stops at 17.

Section condensation

Introduction

The paper frames cefazolin as a new cephalosporin whose chemical structure makes renal function a likely determinant of serum exposure. The authors wanted to measure excretion across renal failure, test probenecid, and derive a simple administration guide.

Much of the background compares cefazolin with other cephalosporins and cites earlier antimicrobial, toxicity, and distribution work. Those background claims are secondary to this study and were not treated as primary findings.

Materials and methods

Five healthy volunteers and nine renal-failure patients are described in the methods. Each received cefazolin 500 mg intramuscularly. Three healthy volunteers later received probenecid 500 mg every 6 hours for four doses, with cefazolin given 1 hour after the first probenecid dose. Serum and urine samples were collected over time.

Cefazolin was measured with a modified Bacillus subtilis agar-diffusion assay. Serum standards used human serum and urine standards used water. Samples were run in triplicate. Serum values below 5 micrograms per milliliter were excluded as unreliable. Half-life came from least-squares fitting of the descending semilog concentration curve. Normal renal clearance used the 2- to 4-hour urine collection and geometric mean serum concentration.

Results

Normal subjects reached peak serum concentrations of 44 to 70 micrograms per milliliter at 30 to 60 minutes. Their half-lives ranged from 1.37 to 1.96 hours, mean 1.60 hours. Renal impairment prolonged half-life markedly. The text gives 4.83 hours near creatinine clearance 47 mL/min and 69.2 hours in an anuric observation. Probenecid increased reported half-life by about 20% to 40% and peak concentration by 7% to 33%.

The total elimination constant tracked creatinine clearance with r = 0.94 and the reported equation k2 = 0.0034 × Ccr + 0.017. Fractional cefazolin clearance ranged from 0.33 to 0.80 of creatinine clearance in normal volunteers and rose with serum concentration. Normal urinary peaks were about 1,000 to more than 2,000 micrograms per milliliter. More than 90% of the dose was recovered in urine within 24 hours in all normals.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors attribute the strong creatinine-clearance relation and high urinary recovery to predominantly renal elimination. They interpret the probenecid effect and fractional-clearance pattern as evidence for a smaller tubular-secretory component in addition to glomerular filtration. They also suggest biliary elimination may matter more in renal failure, but that part of the argument leans on cited animal and external human data rather than direct measurements in this cohort.

The practical output is a creatinine-clearance-based half-life nomogram and a dose-to-peak relation in normal subjects. These are historically informative. The small sample, single-dose design, sparse demographics, participant-accounting conflict, and lack of clinical outcomes limit direct practice use.

Figures and tables

Figure 1. Selected serum decay curves

The figure plots selected least-squares serum decay curves as percent of peak against hours after peak. Curves flatten as creatinine clearance falls. Labels include creatinine clearances from 129 and 101 mL/min/1.73 m2 down to 0 and 1.

The visual supports the paper's central observation that reduced renal function slows cefazolin elimination. It is descriptive and based on selected subjects rather than a complete cohort display.

Figure 2. Nomogram for cefazolin clearance

The left ordinate is total cefazolin elimination constant. The right ordinate converts the same relation into serum half-life. Creatinine clearance is on the horizontal axis. The central fitted line is shown with 90% confidence limits.

The reported relation is k2 = 0.0034 × Ccr + 0.017, with r = 0.94. This figure is the paper's main implementation artifact. It maps renal function to expected elimination and half-life after the studied dose.

Figure 3. Fractional cefazolin clearance versus serum concentration

Fractional cefazolin clearance is plotted against mean serum cefazolin concentration during the 2-hour clearance interval. The fitted line rises across the five normal volunteers.

The authors use this pattern to argue that renal handling is not simple fixed-fraction filtration. They discuss protein binding and an unsaturated tubular transport process as possible explanations. The dataset has only five points, so the mechanism remains tentative.

Evaluation and appraisal

7/10

Relevance

Useful historical renal pharmacokinetic evidence for cefazolin and a clear example of creatinine-clearance-based dose-exposure reasoning.

Evidence basis. Direct serum and urine measurements across a wide renal-function range.

Limiting factor. The study does not address current treatment practice or repeated dosing.

What changes the score. Current comparative PK data and practice guidance would raise direct clinical relevance.

7/10

Novelty

For its publication period, the study linked cefazolin elimination quantitatively to renal function and tested probenecid effects.

Evidence basis. Regression of elimination constant on creatinine clearance and a renal-clearance nomogram.

Limiting factor. The appraisal cannot reconstruct the 1973 evidence field beyond what the paper itself cites.

What changes the score. A verified historical literature comparison would refine this score.

5/10

Method strength

The study directly measured concentrations and used explicit assay and clearance methods, but the sample was very small and participant accounting is inconsistent.

Evidence basis. Triplicate agar-diffusion assays, serum standards, urine collections, and least-squares decay analysis.

Limiting factor. Single dose, sparse reporting, small groups, and no modern population PK model.

What changes the score. Larger prespecified sampling with complete participant accounting and model diagnostics would raise the score.

6/10

Evidence strength

The renal-function signal is large and internally coherent, but mechanistic claims extend beyond what this small study can establish.

Evidence basis. Correlation coefficient 0.94 for elimination constant versus creatinine clearance, more than 90% urinary recovery in normals, and prolonged half-life with renal failure.

Limiting factor. No robust uncertainty analysis for most estimates and no independent replication within the source.

What changes the score. Replication with larger samples and formal uncertainty estimates would raise the score.

4/10

External validity

The results come from a small, poorly characterized sample receiving one intramuscular dose.

Evidence basis. Renal function spans creatinine clearance 0 to 144 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Limiting factor. Demographics, infection states, repeated dosing, and broader clinical covariates are not reported or tested.

What changes the score. A larger diverse cohort under clinical treatment conditions would raise the score.

5/10

Implementation value

The paper gives a usable historical half-life nomogram and a dose-to-peak relation, but it should not be used alone as present-day dosing guidance.

Evidence basis. Figure 2 connects creatinine clearance to elimination constant and serum half-life.

Limiting factor. The source predates current dosing standards and does not test clinical outcomes.

What changes the score. Current validated dosing studies and guideline alignment would raise the score.

Clinical translation. The renal-elimination relationship is biologically and numerically persuasive within this small dataset. The paper should be used as historical pharmacokinetic evidence, not as a stand-alone source for present-day cefazolin dosing.

Validity

The study has direct concentration measurements, explicit specimen handling, a serum-standard assay designed around cefazolin protein binding, and clear pharmacokinetic calculations. The renal-function gradient is wide. The main weaknesses are sample size, incomplete participant accounting, sparse demographic information, single-dose exposure, and limited uncertainty reporting.

Mechanistic inference

The conclusion that the kidney is the major elimination route is well supported by high urinary recovery and the creatinine-clearance relation. The narrower claim that glomerular filtration dominates with a smaller tubular-secretory contribution is plausible from the probenecid and fractional-clearance data, but the study did not directly measure unbound filtration or tubular transport.

Confidence in appraisal

Moderate to high for the paper's internal pharmacokinetic findings. Moderate for the mechanistic interpretation. Low for using this paper alone to make contemporary clinical dosing decisions.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Keep.
Renal function strongly changes cefazolin half-life. The paper quantifies that relation rather than stating it qualitatively.
Keep.
Human-serum assay standards matter for a highly protein-bound drug. The authors explicitly connect standard matrix to measured concentration.
Do not overclaim.
The data do not establish a modern dosing regimen, repeated-dose accumulation profile, or treatment outcome.
