Scope warning: This trial establishes short-term glycemic efficacy and selected safety signals for dapagliflozin monotherapy in a relatively early, treatment-naive type 2 diabetes population. It does not establish cardiovascular, heart-failure, kidney-outcome, mortality, or long-term comparative-effectiveness benefits.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In treatment-naive adults with type 2 diabetes, once-daily dapagliflozin produced clinically meaningful reductions in A1C and fasting plasma glucose over 24 weeks with little hypoglycemia, increased glucosuria, modest weight loss, and more reports suggestive of genital and urinary infections. The effect was larger at higher baseline A1C.

The main morning-dose cohort randomized 274 participants to placebo (n=75) or dapagliflozin 2.5 mg (n=65), 5 mg (n=64), or 10 mg (n=70). At week 24, adjusted mean A1C changes were -0.23% with placebo and -0.58%, -0.77%, and -0.89% with dapagliflozin 2.5, 5, and 10 mg. The 5 mg and 10 mg groups were statistically superior to placebo (reported P=0.0005 and P<0.0001, respectively). FPG changed by -4.1, -15.2, -24.1, and -28.8 mg/dL, respectively. Body weight fell in all groups (-2.2 kg placebo vs -3.3, -2.8, and -3.2 kg with dapagliflozin), but between-group weight differences did not reach statistical significance.

Safety interpretation should remain narrow. There were no major hypoglycemic episodes, but prospectively monitored events suggestive of genital infection were more frequent in dapagliflozin arms (main cohort: 1.3% placebo vs 7.7%, 7.8%, and 12.9% for 2.5, 5, and 10 mg), while UTI-suggestive events were 4.0%, 4.6%, 12.5%, and 5.7%. The 24-week sample was too small and too selected to establish uncommon or long-latency harms. Sponsor involvement was substantial: the trial was funded by Bristol-Myers Squibb and AstraZeneca, two authors were Bristol-Myers Squibb employees, and sponsor-employed editorial support was acknowledged.

Section-by-section condensation

Background and objective

The paper frames SGLT2 inhibition as an insulin-independent glucose-lowering strategy that increases urinary glucose excretion. Dapagliflozin is described as a selective, reversible SGLT2 inhibitor, and the study objective was to test efficacy and safety as monotherapy in treatment-naive type 2 diabetes.

The background emphasizes limitations of then-available agents, including weight gain and hypoglycemia, and proposes caloric loss through glucosuria as a potentially differentiating mechanism. These mechanistic arguments are rationale, not outcome evidence.

Methods

Adults aged 18-77 years with type 2 diabetes inadequately controlled by diet and exercise were enrolled at 85 sites. Entry included BMI ≤45 kg/m² and fasting C-peptide ≥1.0 ng/mL; exclusions removed type 1 diabetes, substantial renal dysfunction, major recent cardiovascular disease, severe hypertension, marked liver abnormalities, and other significant comorbidity. Participants with A1C 7.0-10% were randomized to placebo or dapagliflozin 2.5, 5, or 10 mg in the morning, or to exploratory evening dapagliflozin cohorts. A separate high-A1C cohort (10.1-12%) was randomized to 5 or 10 mg dapagliflozin without placebo.

The primary endpoint was week-24 A1C change in the main morning cohort; FPG and weight were secondary. ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values and generated point estimates with 95% CIs. Rescue metformin was permitted at prespecified FPG thresholds, with post-rescue efficacy data excluded. Table 2 indicates last-observation-carried-forward handling for the principal efficacy measures. The paper does not report enough detail to independently assess sequence generation or allocation concealment.

Results — glycemia and weight

A1C reduction was apparent by week 4 and sustained. Week-24 adjusted mean A1C change was -0.23% (95% CI -0.43 to -0.02) with placebo, versus -0.58% (-0.80 to -0.36), -0.77% (-0.99 to -0.55), and -0.89% (-1.10 to -0.67) with dapagliflozin 2.5, 5, and 10 mg. The proportion achieving A1C <7% was 32% with placebo and 41%, 44%, and 51% with increasing dapagliflozin doses. FPG reductions were also larger at 5 and 10 mg.

Weight decreased by -2.2 kg with placebo and -3.3, -2.8, and -3.2 kg with dapagliflozin 2.5, 5, and 10 mg; differences versus placebo were not statistically significant. In the exploratory high-A1C cohort, A1C fell -2.88% with 5 mg and -2.66% with 10 mg, with corresponding FPG changes of -77.1 and -84.3 mg/dL. In the main-cohort subgroup with baseline A1C ≥9%, A1C fell roughly 1.2-2.0 percentage points with dapagliflozin versus +0.16% with placebo; this was exploratory.

Results — pharmacodynamic and physiologic findings

Urinary glucose-to-creatinine ratios increased markedly with dapagliflozin, supporting the intended SGLT2 pharmacodynamic mechanism. Pooled morning/evening data showed a weak but statistically significant association between greater fractional renal glucose excretion and greater weight reduction (r=-0.13; P=0.008). The analogous relationship with A1C did not reach statistical significance (P=0.11).

Mean hematocrit increased dose-dependently (up to +2.38 percentage points with morning 10 mg), and seated blood pressure decreased modestly. The investigators reported no clinically relevant mean changes in renal-function markers during this short trial. These physiologic findings are consistent with osmotic diuresis but do not by themselves establish long-term cardiorenal benefit.

Results — safety

Overall adverse-event rates in the main morning cohort were 60.0% with placebo and 63.1%, 57.8%, and 68.6% with dapagliflozin 2.5, 5, and 10 mg. Serious AEs were uncommon. AE-related discontinuation rose numerically to 7.1% in the 10 mg group versus 1.3% with placebo. One death from a motor vehicle accident occurred in the 10 mg group.

No major hypoglycemic episodes occurred. Prospectively monitored events suggestive of UTIs and genital infections were more common in several dapagliflozin groups. These were investigator-defined collections of signs/symptoms/preferred terms rather than necessarily culture-confirmed infections, which matters when comparing rates with later trials or labeling.

Conclusions and author interpretation

The authors concluded that dapagliflozin monotherapy meaningfully improved A1C and FPG while producing favorable changes in several metabolic parameters. They emphasized the insulin-independent mechanism and near absence of hypoglycemia.

They acknowledged that weight differences versus placebo were not statistically significant and suggested the large placebo weight loss may have reflected intensive diet/exercise counseling in motivated, newly diagnosed patients. They also recognized that longer-term data were needed for the weight effect.

Table 1 — Baseline characteristics

Main-cohort baseline A1C was approximately 7.84-8.01%, FPG 160-167 mg/dL, mean age about 51-53 years, BMI about 32-34 kg/m², and diabetes duration was short (median roughly 0.25-0.50 years). The high-A1C cohort had baseline A1C about 10.7-10.8% and FPG about 232-241 mg/dL. The treatment-naive, relatively early-disease profile limits generalizability to longstanding or comorbidity-heavy diabetes.

Table 2 — Efficacy, vital signs, and laboratory changes

Beyond A1C/FPG/weight, Table 2 documents strong dose-related glucosuria, modest blood-pressure reductions, increased hematocrit, reduced uric acid, and no clinically meaningful average change in serum creatinine or cystatin C. These are short-term physiologic measurements. The table explicitly documents analysis conventions (LOCF for key efficacy measures; Dunnett/sequential testing for selected endpoints), which are important to interpretation.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. This is a foundational efficacy/safety trial for dapagliflozin and useful for understanding early SGLT2 pharmacology, dose response, glycemic effect, glucosuria, hypoglycemia profile, and genital/urinary infection signals. It should not be used as the principal contemporary practice-changing evidence for SGLT2 therapy.
Relevance — 9/10
Rationale: Directly relevant to dapagliflozin efficacy and early safety.
Evidence basis: randomized phase 3 monotherapy trial with multiple doses.
Limiting factor: does not compare dapagliflozin with empagliflozin or address modern cardiorenal outcomes.
Would rise: direct active-comparator or outcomes data.
Novelty — 8/10
Rationale: Historically early phase 3 evidence for a new insulin-independent drug class.
Evidence basis: dose-ranging clinical efficacy plus mechanism-linked glucosuria.
Limiting factor: novelty is historical rather than current.
Would fall: if assessed only for present-day novelty.
Method strength — 7/10
Rationale: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multinational design with prespecified endpoints and 95% CIs.
Evidence basis: main cohort and prospectively monitored safety categories.
Limiting factor: incomplete reporting of sequence generation/concealment, LOCF, rescue-data exclusion, small arms.
Would rise: protocol/SAP access, modern missing-data estimands, larger sample.
Evidence strength — 7/10
Rationale: Strong short-term evidence for A1C/FPG lowering; weaker for weight and uncommon harms.
Evidence basis: consistent dose-response and CIs for primary efficacy.
Limiting factor: surrogate endpoints and 24-week duration.
Would rise: longer-term patient-centered outcomes and robust safety exposure.
External validity — 6/10
Rationale: Multinational sites help, but participants were treatment-naive with short diabetes duration and major comorbidity exclusions.
Evidence basis: 85-site enrollment across four countries.
Limiting factor: selected early-disease population and renal/CV exclusions.
Would rise: broader CKD/HF/CVD populations and pragmatic settings.
Implementation value — 5/10
Rationale: Useful for foundational pharmacology and expected glycemic/AE profile, but contemporary use depends on later cardiorenal evidence and current labeling/guidelines.
Evidence basis: clear doses, endpoints, and safety categories.
Limiting factor: does not answer class interchangeability or inpatient formulary equivalence.
Would rise: head-to-head effectiveness data or modern outcomes trials.

Major strengths

  • Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled main cohort.
  • Multiple doses with a coherent dose-response signal for A1C/FPG and glucosuria.
  • Multinational, multicenter recruitment.
  • Prospective monitoring of urinary/genital infection symptoms and explicit hypoglycemia definitions.
  • Quantitative reporting with 95% confidence intervals for central efficacy outcomes.

Major limitations

  • Only 24 weeks; insufficient for long-term safety or clinical outcomes.
  • Small individual arms and limited power for safety comparisons.
  • Surrogate glycemic endpoints, not cardiovascular, kidney, HF, mortality, or hospitalization outcomes.
  • LOCF and exclusion of post-rescue efficacy data can bias estimates under nonrandom missingness/rescue.
  • Randomization sequence generation and allocation concealment are not described sufficiently in the article.
  • Substantial sponsor involvement and industry-affiliated authors/editorial support increase the importance of independent replication.
  • The high-A1C cohort had no placebo control and should remain exploratory.

Practice translation and 2026 external verification

What later practice confirms: The SGLT2 inhibitor class is now used for much more than glucose lowering. The 2026 ADA Standards recommend SGLT2 inhibitors with demonstrated benefit for patients with type 2 diabetes and ASCVD/high cardiovascular risk, heart failure, or CKD, often irrespective of A1C. Current U.S. dapagliflozin labeling includes glycemic control in type 2 diabetes as well as heart-failure and CKD risk-reduction indications.

What this 2010 paper does not prove: It cannot be cited as evidence that dapagliflozin reduces HF hospitalization, CKD progression, cardiovascular death, or mortality; those indications depend on later outcomes programs. It also does not establish therapeutic interchangeability between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin.

External verification sources: American Diabetes Association, Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026, sections 9, 10, and 11 (DOIs 10.2337/dc26-s009, 10.2337/dc26-s010, 10.2337/dc26-s011); FARXIGA (dapagliflozin) U.S. prescribing information, Drugs@FDA, label revised June 2024.

Best use

Foundational evidence for dapagliflozin's glucose-lowering dose response, low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk, glucosuric mechanism, modest weight/BP effects, and early genital/urinary infection signal.

Do not use for

Head-to-head dapagliflozin-versus-empagliflozin equivalence, inpatient formulary interchangeability, or modern cardiorenal outcome claims.
