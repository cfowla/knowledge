One-page synthesis

Core thesis. cHL is highly curable, but modern management increasingly tries to preserve disease control while reducing late toxicity. Treatment is stage- and risk-adapted, commonly uses interim FDG-PET/CT, and now incorporates PD-1 blockade or CD30-directed therapy in selected unfavorable early-stage disease and advanced-stage disease.

cHL comprises roughly 90%-95% of Hodgkin lymphoma and most often presents with painless supradiaphragmatic lymphadenopathy. Reed-Sternberg cells are a small fraction of tumor volume but shape a large inflammatory microenvironment and exploit PD-1/PD-L1 signaling for immune evasion. Diagnosis depends on biopsy, with excisional lymph node biopsy preferred when feasible. Lugano staging with FDG-PET/CT is standard; early-stage disease is further divided into favorable and unfavorable categories using age, B symptoms, ESR, bulk, nodal burden, and extranodal involvement.

For favorable early-stage disease, ABVD with or without radiotherapy remains a central approach; PET-adapted strategies can omit radiation in selected patients at the cost of somewhat higher relapse risk but similar overall survival in cited trials. Unfavorable early-stage disease may use chemotherapy-only or combined-modality regimens including brentuximab vedotin or nivolumab. For stage III-IV disease, the review identifies nivolumab-AVD as the preferred US regimen and discusses BrECADD and brentuximab vedotin-AVD as alternatives, particularly when immunotherapy is unsuitable. Relapsed/refractory disease is generally approached with salvage therapy followed by high-dose chemotherapy and autologous stem cell rescue when eligible; allogeneic transplant is reserved for selected patients after post-autologous relapse.

Because many patients survive for decades, late effects are central to treatment selection and follow-up. Secondary cancers are the leading cause of death among survivors, while cardiovascular disease is the leading noncancer cause. Pulmonary disease, thyroid dysfunction, and fertility impairment are also important. The review highlights ctDNA-based minimal residual disease assessment and investigational CAR-T strategies as future directions.

Section-by-section condensation

Methods

The authors searched PubMed for English-language cHL articles published from January 1, 2010 through April 23, 2026 using “Hodgkin Disease” as a major MeSH term or hodgkin* in the title, excluding case reports, letters, editorials, and animal studies. The search retrieved 5145 articles. NCCN and ESMO guidelines were also reviewed, and older publications were added from references. The final review included 125 sources.

Pathogenesis

cHL is driven by Reed-Sternberg cells derived from germinal-center B cells. These cells lose typical B-cell features and survive through dysregulated NF-κB, JAK-STAT, and PI3K-AKT signaling. They make up less than 2% of tumor volume but recruit a dense inflammatory microenvironment. 9p24.1/PD-L1 alterations and PD-1 engagement on immune effector cells provide a biologic basis for the strong activity of PD-1 blockade.

Epidemiology and risk factors

The review describes a bimodal age distribution (peak 15-35 years and a smaller peak around age 70), with a median diagnosis age of 33. Associated risk factors include familial predisposition, EBV infection, immune-mediated disease, immunodeficiency, and some immunosuppressive therapies. The absolute risk after EBV remains low despite a reported relative-risk increase, illustrating the distinction between relative and absolute risk.

Subtypes and clinical presentation

WHO histologic subtypes are nodular sclerosis, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte rich, and lymphocyte depleted; nodular sclerosis predominates in the US. Most patients have painless supradiaphragmatic lymphadenopathy. B symptoms occur in roughly 30%-40%, pruritus in about 10%-15%, and isolated infradiaphragmatic disease in less than 10%. ctDNA-defined molecular clusters remain investigational and are not incorporated into consensus classification.

Diagnosis and staging

Diagnosis requires Reed-Sternberg cells in a polymorphous inflammatory background. Excisional biopsy is preferred because tissue architecture matters and malignant cells can be sparse. Typical immunophenotype is CD30+/CD15+, weak PAX5, with absent classic B-cell markers and CD45. Lugano staging uses FDG-PET/CT; routine marrow biopsy is generally unnecessary unless clinically indicated.

Risk stratification

Stage I-II disease is separated into favorable and unfavorable groups. NCCN, EORTC, and GHSG use overlapping but nonidentical criteria involving age, B symptoms/ESR, mediastinal bulk, nodal sites, extranodal disease, and bulky masses. The review emphasizes that these systems perform similarly and that no single system is clearly superior.

Early-stage treatment

Guideline-recommended therapy is chemotherapy with or without consolidative radiotherapy. Combined-modality therapy generally improves PFS but not OS compared with chemotherapy alone in cited PET-adapted trials, so radiation decisions balance relapse control against long-term second-cancer and cardiopulmonary risks. Favorable disease commonly uses 2-4 cycles of ABVD with or without RT; interim PET is central to de-escalation.

Unfavorable early-stage disease can use chemotherapy-only or combined-modality approaches. The review discusses response-adapted ABVD/AVD, BrECADD, brentuximab vedotin-AVD plus RT, and nivolumab-AVD plus RT. Choice is influenced by disease bulk, PET response, and the anticipated long-term harm of radiation.

Advanced-stage treatment

Stage III-IV disease is treated with chemotherapy plus novel agents. The review identifies nivolumab-AVD ×6 as the preferred US regimen based on SWOG S1826, which reported superior PFS over brentuximab vedotin-AVD with similar high OS. For patients who cannot receive immunotherapy, brentuximab-containing strategies such as BrECADD or brentuximab vedotin-AVD are guideline-supported alternatives.

Relapsed or refractory disease

Suspected relapse should be biopsied. About 10%-15% of patients develop relapsed/refractory disease, usually within 2 years of first-line therapy. Transplant-eligible patients generally receive salvage therapy followed by high-dose chemotherapy and autologous stem cell rescue. Multiple salvage regimens are used, but randomized head-to-head evidence is lacking. High-risk patients without prior brentuximab exposure may receive post-transplant brentuximab maintenance; allogeneic transplant is reserved for selected patients who relapse after autologous transplant.

Prognosis and survivorship

Across cited trials, OS exceeds 97% for early-stage favorable disease, 95% for early-stage unfavorable disease, and 90% for advanced-stage disease. This high cure rate makes late toxicity a primary management concern. Routine surveillance imaging has not shown a survival benefit and is not recommended without clinical concern for relapse.

Major late effects include secondary malignancies, cardiovascular disease, pulmonary toxicity, thyroid dysfunction, and fertility impairment. Secondary cancers are the leading cause of death in survivors; cardiovascular disease is the leading noncancer cause. Risks may emerge years after therapy and persist for decades, making exposure-informed survivorship care necessary.

Future directions

ctDNA is being studied for minimal residual disease detection and treatment de-escalation; persistent detectable ctDNA appears prognostic in secondary analyses and may complement or potentially outperform PET for relapse prediction. CAR-T and other novel therapies remain investigational for cHL.

Limitations and conclusions

The authors explicitly note that relevant studies may have been missed, evidence quality was not systematically assessed, and pediatric cHL was excluded. Their conclusion is that chemotherapy remains the backbone of first-line treatment, with radiotherapy for selected early-stage disease and PD-1 or CD30-directed therapy added for select unfavorable early-stage and all advanced-stage disease; relapsed/refractory patients should be evaluated for transplant.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-yield, current adult cHL review with strong implementation value, especially for stage/risk architecture, modern frontline agents, transplant pathways, and survivorship. Its main methodological limitation is that it is a narrative review without systematic evidence-quality assessment.

Validity and bias considerations

  • Selection bias in review construction: the search is described but study selection is not presented as a systematic, reproducible PRISMA-style process.
  • Evidence heterogeneity: RCTs, cohort studies, narrative reviews, and guideline recommendations are combined without a formal certainty hierarchy.
  • Surrogate/time-horizon issues: many regimen comparisons hinge on PFS over several years; late toxicity can emerge decades later.
  • Generalizability: standard trial eligibility may not reflect frail or multimorbid adults; transplant candidacy is especially individualized.
  • Conflict disclosures: one author reported grants/honoraria from several pharmaceutical companies; this should be considered when interpreting treatment synthesis, although the review includes multiple independent trials and guidelines.
Do not overclaim. The review does not establish that every patient with unfavorable early-stage disease should receive the same novel agent, nor does it prove that one salvage regimen is superior. It explicitly notes the absence of randomized head-to-head salvage comparisons.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Stage first, then risk-stratify early disease. FDG-PET/CT + Lugano stage; early stage additionally requires favorable/unfavorable features.
2. Use interim PET as a decision node. PET response can support bleomycin omission, radiation decisions, and treatment intensity changes in selected regimens.
3. Modern advanced-stage therapy is novel-agent based. The review centers nivolumab-AVD, BrECADD, and brentuximab vedotin-AVD rather than ABVD alone.
4. Relapse remains potentially curable. Re-biopsy suspected relapse; use salvage → autologous transplant when eligible; consider maintenance and later allogeneic transplant selectively.
5. Survivorship is part of treatment selection. Radiation field, anthracycline exposure, bleomycin, alkylators, and transplant history shape decades-long monitoring.
6. Trace exact regimen details to the primary source/current guideline. This review is an excellent map, but exact dosing, eligibility, prophylaxis, toxicity management, and evolving guideline status require primary/current references.
