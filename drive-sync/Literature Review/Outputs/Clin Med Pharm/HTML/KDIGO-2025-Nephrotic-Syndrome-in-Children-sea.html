KDIGO 2025 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Nephrotic Syndrome in Children

Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Nephrotic Syndrome in Children Work Group. Kidney Int. 2025;107(5S):S241-S289. DOI 10.1016/j.kint.2024.11.007. Main guideline 50 pages, data supplement 159 pages.

Executive synthesis

The 2025 update narrows several high-impact decisions. Initial steroid therapy remains 8 or 12 weeks, not longer. Routine daily prednisone during respiratory infections is now discouraged after PREDNOS2 failed to show a clinically useful reduction in infection-associated relapse. Children with steroid dependence, and frequently relapsing disease complicated by serious steroid toxicity, should move to steroid-sparing therapy. Cyclosporine or tacrolimus remains the recommended initial second-line treatment for steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome.

The guideline is unusually useful at the bedside because it couples those recommendations with explicit definitions, dosing, monitoring, biopsy and genetic-testing triggers, and treatment algorithms. The main weakness is not guideline process. It is the evidence base. Several central recommendations are supported by moderate or low certainty evidence, and many treatment comparisons in the supplement remain small, imprecise, or at risk of bias.

Definitions that control treatment classification

  • Nephrotic-range proteinuria. Spot uPCR at least 200 mg/mmol, equivalent to 2 g/g, or protein excretion at least 1000 mg/m2/day, corresponding roughly to 3+ or 4+ dipstick protein.
  • Nephrotic syndrome. Nephrotic-range proteinuria plus serum albumin below 30 g/L, or edema if albumin is unavailable.
  • Complete remission. First-morning or 24-hour uPCR at or below 200 mg/g, 20 mg/mmol, or negative/trace dipstick for at least 3 consecutive days.
  • SSNS. Complete remission within 4 weeks of standard-dose prednisone or prednisolone.
  • SRNS. No complete remission within 4 weeks of standard-dose daily prednisone or prednisolone.
  • CNI response. Partial remission by 6 months and/or complete remission by 12 months of adequate CNI treatment. No partial remission by 6 months defines CNI resistance.

Initial steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome

The Level 1B initial-treatment recommendation preserves two acceptable regimens: 8 weeks, split 4 weeks daily plus 4 weeks alternate day, or 12 weeks, split 6 plus 6. The standard daily dose is prednisone or prednisolone 60 mg/m2/day or 2 mg/kg/day, maximum 60 mg/day. Alternate-day dosing is 40 mg/m2 or 1.5 mg/kg, maximum 40 mg.

Why the guideline stops at 8 or 12 weeks

Longer first-episode courses can delay the first relapse but have not shown a reliable reduction in frequent relapses or a better subsequent disease course. In Supplement Table S4, therapy of at least 12 weeks versus 8 weeks produced a relapse RR of 0.79, 95% CI 0.65-0.95, but certainty was low. Frequent relapse had RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.59-1.06, with moderate certainty. Cushing syndrome had RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.90-1.54, with moderate certainty. The guideline therefore prioritizes avoiding unnecessary cumulative steroid exposure.

There is still no decisive evidence favoring 8 over 12 weeks. The text allows local practice, patient characteristics, and preferences to choose between them. Eight weeks may be attractive in rapid responders or children with obesity, hypertension, or type 1 diabetes.

Infection-associated relapse prevention

This is one of the clearest changes from the 2021 approach. KDIGO now recommends against routinely switching to daily glucocorticoids during upper respiratory or other infections in FRNS or SDNS, a Level 1C recommendation.

The PREDNOS2 trial is the main reason. Among 271 children at 91 UK sites, upper-respiratory-infection-associated relapse occurred in 42.7% with prednisolone and 44.3% with placebo. The adjusted risk difference was 0.02, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.10, P=0.7. Supplement Table S5 likewise reports relapse with infection RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.73-1.27, with low certainty. A narrow exception remains for children already taking low-dose alternate-day prednisolone who repeatedly relapse with infections or have substantial steroid morbidity. For them, 3 extra daily doses at 0.5 mg/kg can be considered.

Relapse and steroid-sparing therapy in SSNS

Relapse treatment remains high-dose daily prednisone or prednisolone until complete remission for at least 3 days, followed by 4 weeks of alternate-day therapy. Children with FRNS who have serious glucocorticoid toxicity, and all children with SDNS, should receive a steroid-sparing agent rather than continuing glucocorticoids alone.

Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome

Cyclosporine or tacrolimus is the Level 1C initial second-line recommendation. Figure 4 specifies an initial minimum 6-month treatment period and continuation to at least 12 months in responders. Lack of at least partial response by 6 months is a stopping point.

The supplement explains why CNI therapy remains central. Table S16 reports that cyclosporine versus placebo/no treatment increased complete remission, RR 7.66, 95% CI 1.06-55.34, based on 49 participants in 3 studies, with moderate certainty but a very wide interval. Table S17 reports complete remission at 3-6 months with CNIs versus IV cyclophosphamide at RR 3.43, 95% CI 1.84-6.41, with moderate certainty. Table S19 found tacrolimus superior to MMF for maintenance in a single 60-patient study: complete remission RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.11-2.90, low certainty, and frequent relapse RR 0.28, 95% CI 0.09-0.92, moderate certainty.

Figure 4 explicitly says cyclophosphamide is not recommended for SRNS. MMF can be used as a maintenance approach after stable CNI-induced remission, and rituximab is a later option rather than the default second-line choice.

Biopsy, genetics, supportive care, and monitoring

  • Biopsy. The default is to avoid biopsy at first presentation when the course is typical. Biopsy becomes important with age 12 years or older at presentation, steroid resistance, secondary steroid resistance, atypical systemic or laboratory features, unexplained kidney failure, or CNI-associated kidney-function decline/prolonged exposure.
  • Genetic testing. Strongly favored for SRNS, congenital/infantile disease, syndromic disease, or family history of SRNS/FSGS. This matters because genetic disease can change the value of further immunosuppression and guide extrarenal evaluation.
  • Bone health. Routine vitamin D is not required in SSNS with normal vitamin D levels. Calcium plus vitamin D becomes relevant with FRNS/SDNS, known deficiency, or prolonged steroid exposure.
  • Gastroprotection. Routine prophylactic PPI therapy is not supported in children without symptoms or gastrotoxicity risk factors.

Guideline methods and evidence architecture

KDIGO used three systematic-review questions in PICOD format and restricted the update to RCT evidence for treatment effectiveness and safety. Brown University's Center for Evidence Synthesis in Health performed the evidence review. Two reviewers performed critical appraisal with adjudication as needed. Dichotomous outcomes were synthesized as risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals, using random-effects models. Heterogeneity, publication bias, subgroup effects, and GRADE certainty were assessed.

Figure 6 shows 4548 update-search records, 4417 exclusions at screening, 131 reports sought, 126 assessed for eligibility, and 21 new RCTs in 23 articles. Combined with the 115 RCTs in the prior guideline evidence base, the current review incorporated 136 RCTs.

The supplement is a major strength. It exposes search strategies and 70 summary-of-findings tables rather than leaving the recommendation-to-evidence link opaque. It also makes the weak areas obvious: many cells contain no data for mortality, kidney failure, major GFR loss, malignancy, or long-term kidney outcomes.

Appraisal

10/10

Relevance

Directly addresses the practical pediatric nephrotic syndrome decisions that determine steroid exposure, relapse control, biopsy/genetic workup, and second-line therapy.

9/10

Novelty

The 2025 update materially changes infection-triggered relapse prophylaxis and incorporates newer rituximab, MMF-after-rituximab, and SRNS evidence while harmonizing definitions with IPNA.

9/10

Method strength

Independent evidence review, explicit PICOD questions, RCT-focused update, GRADE, public review, full search strategies, and a 159-page supplement make the process transparent and reproducible.

7/10

Evidence strength

Several central recommendations are supported by moderate evidence, but many comparisons remain low or very low certainty because of small trials, bias, imprecision, and sparse long-term kidney and safety outcomes.

8/10

External validity

The scope is global and clinically broad for ages 1-18, but many trials are small and heterogeneous. Infants younger than 1 year are outside treatment scope, and genetic SRNS increasingly requires individualized interpretation.

10/10

Implementation value

The guideline gives usable definitions, thresholds, doses, trough targets, monitoring intervals, stopping rules, biopsy triggers, genetic-testing indications, and treatment algorithms.

Main strengths

  • Clear separation of graded recommendations from ungraded Practice Points.
  • Definitions are operational enough for clinical pathways and CDS logic.
  • The update reacts appropriately to a large negative trial, PREDNOS2, instead of retaining older infection-prophylaxis practice by inertia.
  • Figure 3 and Figure 4 convert broad steroid-sparing recommendations into dose and monitoring rules.
  • The supplement exposes both positive evidence and missing outcomes.

Main limitations

  • No dedicated evidence base resolves 8 versus 12 weeks of initial steroid therapy.
  • Many drug comparisons rely on one or a few small trials, with low or very low certainty for safety and long-term outcomes.
  • Mortality, kidney failure, sustained GFR loss, malignancy, and long-term toxicity are frequently absent from SoF tables.
  • Target CNI trough ranges are borrowed largely from transplant practice; the guideline states that nephrotic-syndrome-specific target ranges are not known.
  • Several implementation decisions remain Practice Points, so a CDS implementation should not flatten them into the same certainty class as Level 1 recommendations.

Implementation takeaways

  • Encode disease-state definitions before treatment logic. The 4-week steroid-response boundary and 6-month CNI response boundary change the treatment pathway.
  • Keep 8-week and 12-week initial steroid regimens as valid alternatives rather than automatically extending treatment beyond 12 weeks.
  • Do not automate routine daily steroids for every respiratory infection. Preserve the narrow selected-patient exception.
  • For steroid-sparing therapy, store agent-specific monitoring and duration rules alongside the indication.
  • In SRNS, prompt early genetic testing and treat CNI response at 6 months as a decision point.

Research agenda

  • Compare 8 versus 12 weeks of prednisone or prednisolone for initial therapy, explore further shortening of the initial glucocorticoid regimen, and assess combination therapy with a glucocorticoid-sparing agent at disease onset.
  • Optimize treatment of steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome after relapse across different disease patterns.
  • Optimize the glucocorticoid dosing regimen used at the onset of an infection.
  • Define the optimal dosing and choice of glucocorticoid-sparing agents in frequently relapsing and steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome.
  • Evaluate the optimal duration of glucocorticoid treatment in steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome, especially when calcineurin inhibitors are initiated, and stratify patients by podocytopathy-related genetic mutations.
  • Determine mechanisms of action of glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressive therapies in steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome, including pharmacogenomics and biomarkers or genetic risk haplotypes for disease stratification.
  • Determine the long-term safety of B-cell-depleting therapies.
  • Include quality-of-life measures as endpoints in trials of treatments for both steroid-sensitive and steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome.
  • Evaluate anti-nephrin and anti-slit diaphragm antibodies as biomarkers and predictors of treatment response and outcome.
