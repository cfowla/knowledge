Scope
Topical viscous gel biomaterials applied to chronic diabetic wounds. Preformed implantable hydrogels and dressings were excluded.
Scope warning
This paper maps a biomaterials literature. It is not a systematic review, meta-analysis, guideline, or comparative clinical-effectiveness study.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Topical gels are attractive DFU delivery systems because they are easy to apply, maintain wound contact, and can control local delivery. The review finds a wide preclinical literature across growth factors, drugs, nanoparticles, cells, and plant-derived compounds, but a much smaller human evidence base.

The article first contrasts normal wound repair with diabetic chronic-wound biology, then reviews common natural and synthetic gel materials. The treatment sections are organized by therapeutic cargo. Protein and peptide approaches include growth factors, chemokines, Substance P, and talactoferrin. Drug approaches include Edaravone, Valsartan, Naltrexone, and deferoxamine. Additional sections cover nanoparticles, stem cells and exosomes, and herbal or antioxidant agents. Tables 2 through 6 concentrate the animal-model evidence. Table 7 collects six human-tested gel approaches.

The strongest practical point is the translation gap. Many animal studies report large wound-closure differences, often with near-complete closure over two to three weeks, but the review does not provide a systematic search method, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or evidence-certainty grading. Human data are sparse and heterogeneous. The authors also call out manufacturing complexity, raw-material supply, storage, stability, regulatory cost, and uncertain long-term effects of some nanoparticle and cell approaches. The paper is useful as a structured map of candidate technologies, not as a basis for choosing DFU therapy.

Clinical boundary. The article reports Regranex as the only topical gel then FDA approved specifically for DFUs. That is a 2022 source statement. No external current-practice verification was performed in this run.

Section condensation

Introduction

The review frames DFUs as a common, recurrent, high-cost diabetes complication with high morbidity, amputation risk, and mortality. Standard care includes debridement, off-loading, infection treatment, moisture management, surgery when needed, vascular restoration, oxygen therapy in selected settings, grafting, and skin substitutes. The authors argue that current options leave room for better local therapies.

The inclusion boundary is concrete. The paper covers viscous topical gel materials that can be applied through a syringe, pipette, tube, or similar method. It excludes wound dressings and highly cross-linked preformed hydrogels that require surgical implantation.

Normal and diabetic wound healing

The acute-healing discussion follows hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. Platelets and growth factors recruit cells, neutrophils clear pathogens, macrophages shift phenotypes, fibroblasts and keratinocytes rebuild tissue, angiogenesis restores perfusion, and collagen is remodeled.

The chronic-wound section emphasizes persistent inflammation. The review describes impaired immune-cell recruitment and neutrophil clearance, prolonged inflammatory macrophage activity, excess protease and MMP activity, ECM breakdown, hypoxia, reactive oxygen species, poor angiogenesis, and impaired fibroblast and keratinocyte function. Table 1 condenses these differences.

Gel materials

Natural materials discussed include collagen, gelatin, silk, chitosan, cellulose, alginate, hyaluronic acid, chitin, elastin, and decellularized ECM. Their main strengths are tissue-like structure, cell attachment, and biocompatibility. Their weaknesses are faster degradation and weaker mechanics. Synthetic materials such as PEG, polyurethane, PLA, PLGA, PCL, Pluronic F127, and zwitterionic polymers offer more control over crosslinking, mechanics, degradation, and release, with tradeoffs in bioactivity and tissue interaction.

Therapeutic cargo

Protein and peptide gels dominate the early treatment section. The review covers growth factors, QHREDGS peptide, IL-8 and MIP-3alpha, Substance P, and talactoferrin. Drug gels include Edaravone, Valsartan, Naltrexone, deferoxamine, and related delivery systems. The nanoparticle section includes silver, insulin-loaded particles, cerium oxide miR-146a conjugates, and levofloxacin nanoemulsions.

Cell and exosome gels aim to improve survival and local retention of regenerative cells or cell products. Herbal and antioxidant gels include vitamin C, curcumin, mangiferin, ferulic acid, Teucrium polium extract, and konjac glucomannan systems. Across these sections, the review repeatedly reports better closure and tissue-repair markers in diabetic animal models, but the study designs, models, wound sizes, treatment schedules, and endpoints vary substantially.

Challenges and future perspectives

The authors note that topical gels can have weak mechanics and rapid degradation. They discuss stimulus-responsive or "smart" hydrogels as one route to better control stiffness, degradation, and drug release. Their translation section is more concrete than the future-facing material. It focuses on material choice, manufacturing complexity, purification needs, raw-material cost and supply, storage temperature, stability, packaging, and the burden created by multi-component formulations.

The review also stresses that far fewer gel systems have reached human testing than animal testing. Cell survival, unknown long-term nanoparticle effects, clinical-trial cost, and regulatory time are cited as barriers. The authors favor simpler, manufacturable formulations with controlled release that can reduce application frequency.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that topical DFU gels are extensively studied in research settings but rarely tested clinically. They identify Regranex as the only topical gel then FDA approved specifically for DFUs and argue that the growing DFU burden supports continued development.

Table 1. Acute versus chronic wound healing

Hemostasis: acute wounds form a platelet plug and release recruitment signals; chronic wounds have impaired cell recruitment and growth-factor release.
Inflammation: acute wounds clear pathogens and transition macrophage states; chronic wounds retain neutrophils, prolong inflammatory macrophage activity, and increase ECM-degrading proteases.
Proliferation: acute wounds form granulation tissue, angiogenesis, ECM, and epithelium; chronic wounds show poor vascularization, hypoxia, ROS, and excessive ECM degradation.
Remodeling: acute wounds strengthen collagen and regenerate epidermis; chronic wounds have impaired collagen formation and reduced epidermal development.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper is useful for its organized tables and biomaterial translation discussion. It should not be used alone for current DFU treatment decisions or to infer comparative efficacy across gel platforms.
Relevance 7/10
Useful for DFU wound-care technology and local delivery research.
Limit: much of the evidence is preclinical.
Novelty 7/10
The review brings many gel platforms into one DFU-focused taxonomy and includes clinical examples.
Limit: it synthesizes existing studies rather than testing a new intervention.
Method strength 4/10
The paper defines its material scope and organizes the field clearly.
Limit: no reproducible search strategy, selection flow, risk-of-bias method, or formal evidence grading is reported.
Evidence strength 4/10
Many cited studies report large wound-closure effects and the review preserves useful numbers.
Limit: heterogeneous animal models dominate, human data are sparse, and no pooled or bias-adjusted synthesis is provided.
External validity 4/10
The review spans several diabetic animal models and a small set of human studies.
Limit: model differences, wound geometry, dosing schedules, and endpoints make cross-study generalization weak.
Implementation value 6/10
The manufacturing, storage, simplicity, release, and regulatory discussion is useful for product design.
Limit: it does not supply a clinical implementation pathway or comparative cost-effectiveness analysis.

Why the evidence should be handled cautiously

  • Most efficacy examples come from STZ rats or mice, db/db mice, diabetic rabbits, or diabetic pigs.
  • Table values are not directly comparable because wound size, splinting, treatment frequency, study duration, and endpoint timing differ.
  • Some results are reported as approximate or threshold values such as "near full," "about 90 to 95%," or ">95%."
  • The review does not perform systematic risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis.
  • The human table contains only six gel approaches and includes small studies alongside larger trials.

What would raise the scores

A reproducible search and selection method, formal study-quality assessment, standardized outcome definitions, clearer separation of animal and human evidence, pooled estimates where appropriate, and newer externally verified clinical evidence would raise method, evidence, external-validity, and implementation scores.

Implementation and practice takeaways

Use the paper for
A quick map of gel materials, therapeutic cargo, wound-closure benchmarks, and translational design constraints.
Do not use the paper for
Comparative treatment ranking, current regulatory status, or stand-alone clinical treatment selection.
Product-development lesson
The authors repeatedly favor simpler materials, stable storage, scalable synthesis, controlled local release, and fewer applications.
