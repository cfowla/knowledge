One-page synthesis

Core thesis. A prescriptive UFH nomogram rapidly minimized prolonged subtherapeutic APTT. Simultaneous warfarin substantially increased the frequency of prolonged “supratherapeutic” APTT while lowering heparin dose requirements, without an observed increase in bleeding; this challenged the assumption that every high APTT represented excessive heparin exposure.

Load-bearing findings

  • Persistent subtherapeutic APTT for ≥24 h occurred in only 2/99 patients with simultaneous warfarin and 1/100 with heparin alone initially.
  • Persistent supratherapeutic APTT occurred in 69/99 (69%) with simultaneous warfarin versus 24/100 (24%) with heparin alone initially; difference 46 percentage points, P<.001.
  • Recurrent symptomatic VTE was essentially identical: 7/99 (7.1%) versus 7/100 (7.0%).
  • Bleeding occurred in 9/99 (9.1%) versus 12/100 (12.0%). Among patients with versus without persistent supratherapeutic APTT, bleeding was 8.6% versus 12.3% (observed RR 0.7; P=.49); major bleeding was 3.2% versus 9.4% (RR 0.3; P=.09).
  • Mean heparin doses diverged after warfarin initiation; by day 5 they were 21,845±7,400 U/day with simultaneous warfarin versus 30,800±9,400 U/day with heparin alone initially.
  • APTT showed a significant diurnal pattern with a 9.5-s mean peak-to-trough variation (95% CI 5.9-13.2), peaking around 3:23 AM.
Why this matters for a heparin evidence graph. The paper separates three concepts that are often collapsed: the heparin dose, the measured APTT, and clinical bleeding. Warfarin and circadian variation changed APTT independently of a parallel increase in heparin dose, making a single upper APTT threshold an imperfect proxy for pharmacologic heparin excess.

Section-by-section condensation

Background / rationale

The authors frame UFH dosing as a quality-assurance problem: ad hoc dosing often leaves patients subtherapeutic, and earlier prospective evidence linked failure to reach the lower APTT target with recurrent VTE. In contrast, they argue that the evidence defining a dangerous upper APTT limit was much weaker and largely customary.

The paper therefore tests a prescriptive approach intended to reach adequate anticoagulation rapidly while also examining how warfarin-related APTT prolongation should influence heparin dose reduction.

Patients and randomization

Consecutive patients had proximal DVT confirmed by venography. Exclusions included active bleeding/contraindication to anticoagulation, more than 24 h of previous IV heparin, geographic inability to follow up, or refusal of consent. Patients were stratified by prior VTE and bleeding risk before computer-derived assignment to 5 versus 10 days of heparin.

All 199 enrolled patients completed initial therapy and 3-month follow-up. The paper states that treatment groups were comparable on age, sex, presentation status, prior DVT, underlying disorders/surgery, and bleeding risk, but does not reproduce a detailed baseline table in this article.

Regimens and blinding

All patients received a 5000-U IV heparin bolus followed by continuous infusion. The protocol used approximately 40,320 U/day for low-bleeding-risk patients and 29,760 U/day for high-risk patients. The short-course arm started warfarin 10 mg on day 1; the long-course arm received matching placebo through day 4 and started warfarin 10 mg on day 5. Warfarin continued for 12 weeks with an INR target of 2.0-3.0.

APTT and prothrombin time were withheld from the chart and reported only to a clinical pharmacist, who issued protocol-based dose recommendations and was not involved in outcome assessment. This design reduced treatment-group awareness among clinical assessors while allowing standardized titration.

Monitoring, outcomes, and statistics

APTT was checked 4-6 h after initiation and dose changes, repeatedly during the first 24 h, then at least daily; subtherapeutic values triggered additional 4-6 h testing. APTT 55-85 s defined the protocol target. Persistent subtherapy was <55 s not corrected within 24 h; persistent supratherapy was >85 s not corrected within 24 h.

Recurrent DVT/PE required objective confirmation. Bleeding was classified with previously published major/minor criteria. Repeated-measures ANOVA compared APTT and heparin doses, Fisher exact testing compared clinical event frequencies, and longitudinal regression assessed clock-time periodicity.

Results

The protocol nearly eliminated prolonged subtherapy: only 3/199 patients remained below target for 24 h or more. Simultaneous warfarin markedly increased persistent supratherapeutic APTT (69% vs 24%) while heparin doses became lower, supporting the authors’ premise that APTT reflected both heparin and warfarin effects.

Clinical events did not differ materially between randomized arms: recurrent VTE was ~7% in both and bleeding ~9%-12%. Within the nonrandomized achieved-response comparison, persistent supratherapeutic APTT was not associated with more bleeding. APTT also varied by clock time, with a mean 9.5-s peak-to-trough swing despite no comparable diurnal change in heparin dose.

Comment / authors’ interpretation

The authors argue that the prescriptive protocol prevented subtherapy without increasing clinical complications and that high APTT values should not automatically be equated with excessive heparin, particularly when warfarin is contributing to clotting-factor reduction. They emphasize rapid, incremental protocol-driven correction rather than intuitive dose ordering.

They also explicitly restrict portability: the Table 1 nomogram reflects their laboratory’s APTT reagent, and a clinically useful APTT range should be calibrated to a heparin level of 0.2-0.4 U/mL by protamine titration. They note that more frequent testing would have been needed to eliminate supratherapeutic APTTs on later days.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best used as a historical/foundational study of UFH nomogram design, APTT calibration, and the danger of treating APTT as a pure heparin-concentration surrogate. Do not use the numeric nomogram as a current protocol without independent contemporary validation.

Validity and interpretation

Strengths
  • Consecutive objectively diagnosed proximal DVT cohort.
  • Randomized assignment with double-blind treatment timing.
  • Pharmacist-mediated protocol execution insulated from outcome assessment.
  • Objective recurrent VTE confirmation and complete 3-month follow-up.
  • Explicit nomogram and monitoring workflow allow reproducibility.
Major limitations
  • Persistent supratherapeutic APTT was not randomized; the “no bleeding association” comparison is observational.
  • Event counts are small, limiting precision for bleeding and recurrence.
  • APTT thresholds are reagent-specific; the authors explicitly warn against uncalibrated transfer.
  • Warfarin itself prolongs APTT, so high APTT in the combined group cannot be interpreted as high heparin exposure without a heparin-specific measure.
  • The population, fixed-dose strategy, assay methods, and clinical context are from 1992.
Claim boundary. The data support “no observed increase in bleeding among patients with persistent high APTT in this cohort.” They do not establish that deliberately higher heparin exposure is safe, because achieved APTT status was not randomized and APTT was affected by warfarin and time of day.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Preserve
  • Protocolized early titration to avoid prolonged subtherapy.
  • Assay-specific calibration of any APTT therapeutic range.
  • Explicit hold/restart rules and repeat-test timing.
  • Recognition that APTT can reflect factors other than UFH dose.
  • Pharmacist protocol stewardship as a quality-control layer.
Do not copy directly
  • The 55-85 s target.
  • The fixed 40,320/29,760 U/day starting doses.
  • The assumption that an upper APTT threshold is universally safe.
  • The 1992 regimen as a current DVT treatment standard.
Evidence-graph hooks
  • subtherapeutic APTT → recurrence risk context
  • warfarin → APTT prolongation
  • APTT reagent calibration
  • heparin nomogram
  • diurnal APTT variation
  • pharmacist-directed titration

Bottom line: This paper is most valuable for the architecture of safe UFH dosing: reach the lower therapeutic boundary promptly, use a prescriptive titration algorithm, calibrate the assay to a heparin-specific reference, and avoid assuming that a high APTT necessarily means excessive heparin exposure. The exact numeric protocol is historical and source-specific.
