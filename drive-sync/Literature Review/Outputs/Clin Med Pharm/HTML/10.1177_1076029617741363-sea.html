One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Anti-Xa-based titration reached therapeutic UFH targets faster and required fewer rate adjustments per treatment day than aPTT-based titration across four indication-specific nomograms, but the study did not establish better clinical outcomes.

The prospective anti-Xa cohort reached therapeutic range at a median 16 hours versus 24 hours in historical aPTT controls (P<.01), and required 1.2 versus 1.5 dose adjustments per 24 hours (P=.01). Other utilization measures were not significantly different. Paired testing in the anti-Xa cohort showed 49% assay discordance; aPTT was therapeutic only 35% of the time anti-Xa was therapeutic.

Clinical events were uncommon and statistically inconclusive: VTE 1% vs 1%, bleeding 11.9% vs 4% (P=.07), and mortality 0% vs 4% (P=.06). The historical-control design, absence of power for clinical outcomes, incomplete control-era capture risk, and unmeasured baseline confounders materially limit causal inference.

Section condensation

Abstract

The article compares anti-Xa and aPTT monitoring for IV UFH across multiple indication-specific nomograms. It reports a prospective anti-Xa cohort (n=101) versus historical aPTT controls (n=100), with paired anti-Xa/aPTT sampling in the prospective cohort but dosing based on anti-Xa.

Anti-Xa was associated with faster therapeutic attainment and fewer adjustments per 24 hours, with 49% assay discordance and no significant between-group clinical outcome differences.

Introduction / background

The authors frame UFH as useful but pharmacodynamically variable, requiring monitoring and frequent adjustment. They emphasize that aPTT is an indirect assay influenced by reagent, analytic, and biologic factors and cite prior reports of substantial aPTT/anti-Xa discordance.

The stated evidence gap is comparative performance across a broad population managed under multiple indication-specific UFH nomograms rather than a single protocol.

Methods

All prospective cardiology-unit patients receiving continuous IV UFH during March 30-June 30, 2015 were evaluated with simultaneous anti-Xa and aPTT sampling; anti-Xa alone guided titration. Historical patients from the immediately preceding period were titrated using aPTT. Patients were excluded for UFH exposure <24 h, interruption >10 h, or ≥25% noncompliance with the nomogram.

Four nomograms used total body weight, dose caps, indication-specific aPTT and anti-Xa targets, and matched adjustment logic. Testing was performed 6 hours after initiation/rate changes until 2 consecutive therapeutic results, then daily. Wilcoxon and Fisher exact tests compared cohorts; subgroup analyses examined nomogram and bolus status.

Results

Baseline measured characteristics and nomogram distributions were not significantly different. The primary process endpoint favored anti-Xa: 16 vs 24 hours to therapeutic range (P<.01); dose adjustments per 24 hours were 1.2 vs 1.5 (P=.01). Total tests, tests per 24 hours, total adjustments, total heparin duration, and length of stay did not differ significantly.

Paired assays were discordant 49% of the time, and aPTT was therapeutic only 35% of the time when anti-Xa was therapeutic. VTE was 1% vs 1%; bleeding 11.9% vs 4%; mortality 0% vs 4%; none demonstrated a statistically significant between-group difference.

Discussion / limitations / conclusion

The authors interpret the roughly 8-hour faster therapeutic attainment and lower adjustment burden as favoring anti-Xa process performance, consistent with earlier studies. They describe operational implications for nursing, phlebotomy, and laboratory work and note their own discordance experience contributed to system-wide conversion to anti-Xa monitoring.

Major limitations are the single-center historical-control design, potential missing/incomplete retrospective data, unmeasured confounding, inability to tie clinical events to contemporaneous anticoagulation status or dose changes, and no clinical-outcomes power calculation. The authors therefore call for larger controlled studies before claiming clinical-outcome benefit.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for UFH protocol/monitoring design; do not use alone to infer patient-outcome superiority.
Best use: operational evidence for anti-Xa implementation, assay discordance, and nomogram design.
Do not use for: proving reduced thrombosis, bleeding, or mortality.
9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses UFH monitoring strategy and indication-specific nomograms.

Evidence basis: Prospective anti-Xa implementation versus historical aPTT cohort; detailed nomograms and outcomes.

Limiting factor: Single-center 2015 practice and modest sample size.

Would raise/lower: Multicenter contemporary comparative outcomes data.

7/10

Novelty

Rationale: At publication, evaluated anti-Xa versus aPTT across four indication-specific UFH nomograms rather than a single protocol.

Evidence basis: Four nomogram families and paired assay data.

Limiting factor: Concept of anti-Xa monitoring and discordance was already established.

Would raise/lower: Randomized or multicenter comparative implementation evidence.

5/10

Method strength

Rationale: Prospective intervention cohort is useful, but nonrandomized historical control design leaves important bias and temporal-confounding risk.

Evidence basis: Same cardiology units, similar measured baseline characteristics, explicit endpoints and tests.

Limiting factor: Historical control, convenience sampling, unmeasured confounders, no blinding/randomization.

Would raise/lower: Concurrent randomized or well-adjusted prospective comparison.

5/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: Supports process outcomes (time to therapeutic range and dose-adjustment burden) more strongly than clinical outcomes.

Evidence basis: Significant 8-hour difference in median time to therapeutic range and fewer adjustments/24 h.

Limiting factor: Underpowered for VTE, bleeding, and mortality; clinical events were not temporally attributed to anticoagulation status.

Would raise/lower: Adequately powered patient-centered outcomes with concurrent controls.

5/10

External validity

Rationale: The multi-nomogram design broadens within-center applicability, but transferability depends on assay, laboratory, and local nomogram characteristics.

Evidence basis: Four indication-specific protocols spanning DVT/PE, ACS, Afib/post-op, and high-risk settings.

Limiting factor: Single academic center and local target ranges/reagents.

Would raise/lower: Multisite validation using varied assays and contemporary populations.

8/10

Implementation value

Rationale: Provides concrete nomogram targets, adjustment rules, monitoring intervals, and discordance findings useful for protocol comparison.

Evidence basis: Tables 1-2 are operationally detailed; process outcomes are directly measured.

Limiting factor: Should not be copied unchanged without local assay validation and current-guidance review.

Would raise/lower: Local validation plus prospective implementation outcomes.

Principal validity threat: anti-Xa was measured prospectively while the comparator was a prior historical cohort. Even with similar measured baseline characteristics, calendar-time changes, data-capture differences, and unmeasured severity/coagulation factors can explain part of the observed process difference.

Clinical-practice translation and external currency check

What aged well: The paper's process-level signal is directionally consistent with 2026 ISTH SSC guidance, which suggests anti-Xa as the optimal UFH monitoring assay, considers aPTT acceptable, and suggests an anti-Xa target of 0.30-0.70 U/mL for most indications.
What should not be copied literally: The study's four local target ranges and titration thresholds were tied to its institutional protocols, reagents, populations, and 2015 implementation. Current ISTH guidance also emphasizes local range establishment when aPTT is used and recognizes anti-Xa assay limitations/interferences. This article supports local protocol design but does not validate universal adoption of every table threshold.

Current verification source: Gouin-Thibault I, Frere C, Castellucci LA, et al. How to monitor and manage unfractionated heparin in practice (Part 1): Guidance from the SSC of the ISTH. Journal of Thrombosis and Haemostasis. Published online May 2026. DOI: 10.1016/j.jtha.2026.05.013.

Additional current caveat: A 2025 ISTH SSC communication addresses interference from oral factor Xa inhibitors when UFH-calibrated anti-Xa is used after switching from apixaban, rivaroxaban, or edoxaban (DOI: 10.1016/j.jtha.2025.02.026).
