One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In this selected neurocritical-care cohort, a lower-intensity UFH nomogram targeting aPTT 40–65 s was associated with substantially fewer major bleeding events than a standard aPTT 60–100 s nomogram, while VTE progression did not differ significantly between the two UFH strategies. The data support prospective evaluation of lower-intensity UFH, not routine replacement of standard treatment.

What the source supports

The strongest signal is safety: major bleeding occurred in 15/81 standard-intensity patients versus 2/50 lower-intensity patients. In Firth penalized logistic regression, standard-intensity UFH remained associated with greater odds of major bleeding (aOR 4.63, 95% CI 1.18–18.10; p=0.03); a sensitivity model incorporating bolus use, concomitant antiplatelet therapy, and supratherapeutic aPTT burden yielded aOR 6.65 (95% CI 1.62–27.35; p<0.01). The crude absolute difference was 14.5 percentage points, but it should not be interpreted as a causal risk difference because treatment assignment was nonrandom.

For efficacy, untreated patients had the highest symptomatic VTE progression (29/105, 27.6%). Both lower-intensity UFH (aOR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.87) and standard-intensity UFH (aOR 0.15, 95% CI 0.05–0.43) were associated with less progression than no therapeutic anticoagulation. The direct UFH-versus-UFH comparison remained inconclusive: progression was numerically higher with lower-intensity UFH (6/48, 12.5%) than standard-intensity UFH (3/79, 3.8%), p=0.08, and the adjusted comparison was imprecise (reported aOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.21–3.52; p=0.82).

Why this paper matters

It addresses a common but poorly standardized bedside compromise: using a deliberately lower heparin target in patients with acute intracranial pathology or recent neurosurgery who nevertheless have acute extracranial DVT/PE. The study gives concrete target ranges and clinically meaningful bleeding/VTE outcomes, making it directly useful for protocol discussion and research design.

Do not overread the result. The two UFH strategies were clinician-selected, the groups differed in neurological diagnoses and heparin bolus exposure, follow-up imaging was symptom-triggered rather than protocolized, and event counts were small. The wide confidence intervals are compatible with considerable uncertainty around the magnitude of both safety and efficacy effects.

Methods

Design and treatment assignment

All consecutive eligible Neuro ICU patients with acute DVT/PE during the study period were identified from a prospective ICU database, but the analysis itself was retrospective. UFH intensity was not assigned by protocol or randomization. Treating teams selected standard intensity, lower intensity, or no therapeutic anticoagulation based on hemorrhagic risk, thrombotic risk, stability of intracranial pathology, timing from neurologic injury/surgery, and overall clinical status.

Patients without therapeutic anticoagulation received mechanical prophylaxis at admission and heparin chemoprophylaxis 24–48 h after brain injury, intervention, or surgery. This means the three cohorts represent different clinical decisions and baseline risk profiles rather than exchangeable treatment arms.

Outcome ascertainment

VTE progression required a quantifiable increase in thrombus burden on imaging or a new PE after a prior DVT. Repeat CT pulmonary angiography or duplex ultrasonography was obtained when clinically indicated rather than on a fixed surveillance schedule. Major bleeding followed ISTH criteria and included critical-site bleeding, ≥2 units pRBC transfusion outside the operating room, hemorrhagic shock, or a ≥2 g/dL hemoglobin decrease attributable to hemorrhage.

Intracranial events were adjudicated with explicit radiographic/clinical thresholds, including SDH expansion, hemorrhagic transformation, tumor-bed bleeding, and intracerebral hematoma expansion criteria. Outcomes after hospital discharge were not captured. Three investigators performed chart review with additional lead-reviewer review.

Statistics

Baseline comparisons used standard categorical and continuous-data tests. Multivariable models reported adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals; covariates were selected a priori for clinical relevance. Firth penalized logistic regression was used for the main outcome models, an appropriate strategy for sparse events. Sensitivity analyses added clinically relevant covariates.

aPTT target attainment was assessed at the patient level by classifying all measured values as below, within, or above each assigned nomogram range, calculating each patient's percentages, and comparing group medians with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Results

Study population

Among 10,883 Neuro ICU admissions, 443 patients were diagnosed with acute DVT or PE; 236 met criteria for the safety cohort. The final groups were 81 standard-intensity UFH, 50 lower-intensity UFH, and 105 no therapeutic anticoagulation. The efficacy analysis excluded two distal-DVT patients from each UFH group.

Age, sex, race, BMI, PESI class, GCS, and NIHSS in the stroke subset were similar. Clinically important imbalances remained: the lower-intensity cohort contained more patients with intraparenchymal hemorrhage (18.0% vs 4.9%) and postoperative/interventional patients (44.0% vs 21.0%), whereas the standard-intensity cohort contained more acute ischemic stroke (44.4% vs 32.0%). Heparin bolus exposure differed sharply: 46.9% with standard intensity versus 2.0% with lower intensity.

Compared with no therapeutic anticoagulation, lower-intensity UFH was associated with lower odds of VTE progression (aOR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.87; p=0.03), as was standard-intensity UFH (aOR 0.15, 95% CI 0.05–0.43; p<0.01). Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame therapeutic anticoagulation for acute VTE in neurocritical care as a balance between thrombotic progression and catastrophic hemorrhage. In 236 eligible patients, major bleeding was more frequent with standard-intensity than lower-intensity UFH, and both UFH strategies were associated with less VTE progression than no therapeutic anticoagulation.

The abstract appropriately concludes that lower-intensity UFH may provide a safer balance in selected brain-injured patients and calls for prospective validation. The data do not establish noninferiority for efficacy.

1. Introduction

The introduction identifies immobilization and inflammation as drivers of VTE in neurologically critically ill patients and highlights the danger of precipitating or worsening intracranial hemorrhage with therapeutic anticoagulation. UFH is attractive because it is rapidly reversible, and clinicians sometimes use lower aPTT targets despite limited evidence.

The study objective is explicit: compare lower-intensity UFH with standard-intensity UFH and with no therapeutic anticoagulation for safety and efficacy in patients at risk of intracranial hemorrhage.

2. Methods

The study is a retrospective cohort based on a prospective ICU database. Eligibility required adult acute DVT/PE plus intracranial/intraspinal pathology; treatment strategy was selected by clinicians. The co-primary outcomes were symptomatic VTE progression and ISTH major bleeding during hospitalization.

Follow-up imaging was clinically triggered, not systematic. The analysis used multivariable/Firth penalized logistic regression and sensitivity analyses. aPTT attainment was summarized at the patient level against each assigned target range.

3. Results

The safety cohort comprised 81 standard-intensity, 50 lower-intensity, and 105 untreated patients. Major bleeding was lower with lower-intensity UFH (4.0% vs 18.5%); the association persisted after adjustment. Specific intracranial hemorrhagic events were numerically fewer but not statistically different.

VTE progression was highest without therapeutic anticoagulation. Lower- and standard-intensity UFH were each associated with less progression than no anticoagulation; direct comparison of the two UFH strategies was imprecise and nonsignificant despite a numerical difference favoring standard intensity. Lower-intensity patients had more aPTT measurements within their assigned range.

4. Discussion

The authors interpret the data as supporting the plausibility of lower-intensity UFH in high-bleeding-risk neurocritical-care patients, while emphasizing the clinical heterogeneity of DVT/PE and the difficulty of balancing intracranial bleeding risk against thrombus extension. They contextualize their findings with prior reports of therapeutic anticoagulation after neurological injury/surgery and smaller lower-dose heparin studies.

They acknowledge that the absence of a statistically significant difference in intracranial hemorrhage may reflect limited sample size and clinician selection. They also note frequent IVC-filter use as a possible explanation for the low PE-progression rate and identify BMI as an independent predictor of VTE progression.

4.6 Limitations and future directions

The authors explicitly identify the single-center design, small sample, clinician-selected treatment, confounding by indication, residual confounding, and nonprotocolized VTE imaging as major limitations. These factors can distort both safety and efficacy comparisons.

They classify the findings as hypothesis-generating and call for larger prospective multicenter validation to determine which neurocritical-care subgroups may benefit from different anticoagulation strategies.

5. Conclusion

The source concludes that lower-intensity UFH was associated with fewer major bleeds than standard-intensity UFH and no significant difference in VTE progression between UFH strategies. It proposes lower-intensity UFH as a potentially reasonable balance for selected brain-injured patients.

The conclusion remains appropriately conditional and reiterates the need for prospective validation because of retrospective treatment selection and absence of protocolized follow-up imaging.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first for anticoagulation / critical-care practice, but do not use alone to change a therapeutic UFH target.
Best use: protocol discussion, identifying a plausible lower-intensity strategy for high-hemorrhagic-risk patients, and designing prospective validation. Do not use for: claiming noninferior VTE efficacy or a definitive causal reduction in bleeding.
9/10 Relevance

Directly addresses UFH nomogram intensity, VTE treatment, bleeding, and neurocritical-care decision-making.

Evidence basis: clinically specific population, dosing targets, and outcomes. Limiting factor: narrow setting. Would rise: multicenter pragmatic validation.

8/10 Novelty

Provides comparative data for a lower therapeutic aPTT target in a high-risk population with limited direct evidence.

Evidence basis: direct 40–65 s vs 60–100 s comparison. Limiting factor: retrospective practice variation. Would rise: prospective randomized or protocolized comparison.

5/10 Method strength

Well-defined outcomes and penalized regression improve a difficult observational dataset, but treatment allocation is highly confounded.

Evidence basis: predefined criteria, explicit bleeding definitions, sensitivity analyses. Limiting factor: nonrandom assignment, small events, clinically triggered imaging. Would rise: prospective allocation and standardized surveillance.

5/10 Evidence strength

The safety association is compelling enough to generate a hypothesis; efficacy equivalence is not demonstrated.

Evidence basis: 18.5% vs 4.0% major bleeding with adjusted association; wide CIs and 3.8% vs 12.5% VTE progression. Limiting factor: residual confounding and imprecision. Would rise: adequately powered prospective outcomes.

4/10 External validity

Results may apply to similar tertiary Neuro ICU patients but are difficult to generalize across institutions, diagnoses, and heparin protocols.

Evidence basis: single center with heterogeneous neurologic diagnoses. Limiting factor: center-specific selection and management. Would rise: multicenter replication across defined subgroups.

7/10 Implementation value

The article gives actionable target ranges and outcome definitions, useful for protocol review and local data collection.

Evidence basis: clear aPTT targets, safety/efficacy outcomes, attainment analysis. Limiting factor: insufficient evidence for an autonomous practice change. Would rise: protocolized dosing details plus prospective effectiveness data.

Validity assessment

Strengths
  • Clinically consequential, under-studied decision.
  • Explicit lower and standard aPTT targets.
  • ISTH-based major bleeding definition plus neurologic imaging criteria.
  • Consecutive eligible patients and standardized abstraction process described.
  • Firth penalized logistic regression suited to sparse outcomes.
  • Sensitivity analyses address several plausible confounders.
  • Authors appropriately frame conclusions as hypothesis-generating.
Threats to validity
  • Confounding by indication: clinicians selected intensity based on hemorrhagic/thrombotic risk.
  • Marked bolus imbalance (46.9% standard vs 2.0% lower), making “intensity” partly a treatment-bundle comparison.
  • Neurologic diagnosis and operative status differed between groups.
  • Clinically triggered repeat imaging can miss asymptomatic progression and create differential ascertainment.
  • Small number of major bleeds and VTE events yields wide intervals.
  • Excluding UFH <24 h and patients who failed to reach target may omit clinically important early failures/intolerance.
  • No post-discharge outcomes.
  • Supplemental analyses were referenced but not available in the evaluated PDF.
Interpretive hinge: the paper supports “lower-intensity UFH deserves prospective testing” more strongly than “lower-intensity UFH is as effective as standard UFH.” The direct efficacy estimate is too imprecise to exclude clinically meaningful differences.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Potential use now

Use the study as evidence supporting individualized discussion when full-intensity anticoagulation is judged unusually hazardous, especially when a team is already considering a reduced UFH target.

What should not change from this paper alone

Do not infer that aPTT 40–65 s is a validated therapeutic standard, noninferior for VTE prevention of progression, or appropriate across all brain injuries/neurosurgical states.

Protocol-design value

If an institution evaluates a lower-intensity pathway, this article supplies candidate aPTT ranges, safety endpoints, VTE-progression definitions, and target-attainment metrics for prospective audit.

Monitoring implication

Protocol evaluation should separately track intracranial and extracranial major bleeding, bolus exposure, antiplatelet exposure, time to therapeutic range, percentage below/within/above target, DVT/PE progression, and HIT.

Research priority

Prospective multicenter studies should stratify by neurological pathology, recent surgery, VTE phenotype/severity, bolus strategy, and timing from injury/procedure, with standardized imaging and post-discharge follow-up.

Pharmacy relevance

The study is particularly useful for pharmacist-led nomogram review because it highlights that target range, bolus policy, and target attainment are separable protocol components that may influence both safety and efficacy.

Key limitations and unresolved questions

  1. Causal attribution is weak. Lower-intensity patients were selected because clinicians perceived different risks; regression cannot fully reconstruct the counterfactual treatment decision.
  2. The bolus imbalance is substantial. Standard-intensity care included heparin boluses in nearly half of patients versus almost none in the lower-intensity group. Even though a sensitivity model adjusted for bolus use, sparse events limit confidence that the target range itself explains the bleeding difference.
  3. “No difference” in VTE progression is not evidence of equivalence. The direct comparison is underpowered, with wide confidence bounds; raw progression was 12.5% lower-intensity versus 3.8% standard-intensity.
  4. Outcome surveillance was not uniform. Repeat vascular imaging was symptom-triggered, so asymptomatic progression could be missed and detection could vary by clinical status.
  5. Selection from exclusions may matter. Excluding UFH courses shorter than 24 hours and patients who failed to reach a therapeutic target may preferentially remove early discontinuations, unstable patients, or difficult-to-anticoagulate cases.
  6. Single-center protocol ecology. The balance of neuro diagnoses, IVC-filter practices, laboratory reagents, heparin nomogram details, and clinician thresholds may not transport directly to other hospitals.
  7. PE-specific efficacy is largely unanswered. Only two PE-progression events occurred across all three groups.
