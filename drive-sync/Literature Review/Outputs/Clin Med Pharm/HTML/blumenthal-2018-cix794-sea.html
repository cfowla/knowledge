One-page synthesis

Core finding. In 9004 procedures, reported penicillin allergy was associated with higher adjusted odds of SSI, aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.22. The mediation model attributed the association to receipt of beta-lactam alternative prophylaxis rather than a direct effect of the allergy label.

The clinical signal is specific. Procedures associated with a reported penicillin allergy used cefazolin far less often, 12.2% versus 92.4%, and used clindamycin, vancomycin, gentamicin, and fluoroquinolones much more often. Timing was also less favorable for vancomycin. Among cefazolin or vancomycin recipients, 97.5% of vancomycin recipients missed the recommended 60 to 120 minute preincision start window, compared with 1.7% of cefazolin recipients outside their recommended timing window.

The paper is useful because it links an allergy label to a modifiable prophylaxis pathway and a patient-centered outcome. Its strongest weakness is causal inference. The exposure and prophylaxis choices were not randomized. The authors adjusted for major measured confounders and used a marginal structural mediation model, but residual confounding and model assumptions remain. The 112 to 124 number needed to evaluate is therefore a model-based estimate, not an observed trial result.

Section condensation

Background and objective

The authors framed reported penicillin allergy as common and frequently inaccurate, with the practical consequence that surgical patients may receive non-beta-lactam prophylaxis instead of cefazolin. The study objective was to estimate SSI risk associated with a reported penicillin allergy and examine perioperative antibiotic use as a possible pathway.

Methods

The cohort included hip and knee arthroplasty, hysterectomy, colon surgery, and CABG procedures from 2010 through 2014. Reported penicillin allergy was defined from the allergy record at surgery. SSI ascertainment used prospective NHSN surveillance by the MGH Infection Control Unit with review of every procedure. Perioperative antibiotics were measured from 120 minutes before surgery through surgery end, with separate preincision and vancomycin timing windows.

The adjusted SSI model used generalized estimating equations to account for repeated procedures and included surgery type, age, sex, race, ASA class, procedure duration, and wound class. A marginal structural model estimated direct and indirect effects through receipt of only beta-lactam alternative prophylaxis. The NNE calculation assumed 90% to 100% of reported-allergy patients were not truly allergic and would receive first-line beta-lactam prophylaxis after evaluation.

Results

The study included 8385 patients and 9004 procedures. There were 922 patients with a reported penicillin allergy and 978 procedures in the reported-allergy exposure group. A total of 241 SSIs occurred. Unadjusted SSI rates were 3.5% versus 2.6%. The fully adjusted odds ratio was 1.51, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.22. The mediation model estimated an indirect-effect aOR of 2.05, 95% CI 1.40 to 3.00, while the direct effect was 1.00, 95% CI 0.36 to 2.78.

Antibiotic choice differed sharply by allergy status. Cefazolin use was 12.2% versus 92.4%; clindamycin 48.8% versus 3.1%; vancomycin 34.7% versus 3.3%; gentamicin 24.0% versus 2.8%; fluoroquinolone 6.8% versus 1.3%. The supplement showed the same pattern when limited to the preincision window. The modeled NNE was 112 to 124 allergy evaluations per SSI prevented.

Discussion and conclusions

The authors interpreted the higher SSI odds as a consequence of second-line prophylaxis rather than a direct effect of the allergy label. They argued that most reported reactions were compatible with direct cefazolin use or formal penicillin allergy evaluation. Only five documented reactions were considered potential beta-lactam contraindications, while 953 of 1042 reactions were described as amenable to standard allergy evaluation.

The authors acknowledged the retrospective design, limited number of SSI events, inability to perform adequately powered surgery-specific analyses, and single-center setting. Their conclusion favored systematic preoperative allergy clarification, but the paper did not prospectively test such a program.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Strong implementation signal and a clear, clinically relevant pathway, but the evidence remains observational and single-center.

Relevance: 9/10

Directly relevant to perioperative antibiotic stewardship and penicillin-allergy labels.

Evidence basis. Five common surgery groups, antibiotic selection, timing, and SSI outcomes.

Main limit. The article does not address every surgical population or current practice updates.

Would change the score. A multicenter study tied to present-day pathways would raise this score.

Novelty: 8/10

At publication, the study linked a common allergy label to SSI risk through a specific prophylaxis pathway.

Evidence basis. Mediation analysis connected allergy status, alternative antibiotics, and SSI.

Main limit. The mechanism remained observational rather than experimentally tested.

Would change the score. Prospective intervention evidence would strengthen novelty and causal interpretation.

Method strength: 7/10

The study used standardized SSI surveillance, repeated-procedure methods, prespecified confounders, and a mediation model.

Evidence basis. NHSN outcome definitions, 100% procedure chart review, GEE, multivariable adjustment.

Main limit. Retrospective single-center design and only 241 SSI events limited confounder control and subgroup analysis.

Would change the score. Prospective multicenter design with stronger control of treatment selection would raise the score.

Evidence strength: 6/10

The association is quantitative and internally coherent, but causal claims depend on observational modeling assumptions.

Evidence basis. Adjusted OR 1.51 with 95% CI 1.02-2.22; modeled indirect effect OR 2.05; direct effect OR 1.00.

Main limit. Residual confounding, exposure misclassification, and mediation assumptions remain plausible.

Would change the score. Randomized or strong quasi-experimental delabeling evidence with SSI outcomes would raise the score.

External validity: 6/10

The cohort covered five major procedure types but came from one academic center with ready allergy-specialist access.

Evidence basis. 8385 patients and 9004 procedures across orthopedic, gynecologic, colorectal, and cardiac surgery.

Main limit. Local prophylaxis practices and specialist access may differ elsewhere.

Would change the score. Replication across diverse hospitals and current prophylaxis protocols would raise the score.

Implementation value: 8/10

The study identifies a concrete preoperative workflow target and shows large differences in antibiotic selection and timing.

Evidence basis. Cefazolin 12.2% versus 92.4%, alternative-agent use was much higher, and NNE was modeled at 112-124.

Main limit. The paper does not test an implemented delabeling program or provide a current guideline pathway.

Would change the score. Prospective implementation data with safety, throughput, and SSI outcomes would raise the score.

Key appraisal issue. The phrase "entirely mediated" comes from a marginal structural model in observational data. It is a model result, not proof that alternative prophylaxis is the only causal pathway.

Practice translation

Best use

Support the case for structured preoperative review of penicillin-allergy labels when the label changes recommended prophylaxis.

Do not use for

Do not treat the study as proof that every reported penicillin allergy should be removed or that every patient can safely receive cefazolin without individual assessment.

Workflow signal

The actionable failure point is visible before surgery: allergy label, prophylactic agent choice, and administration timing. That makes the study useful for stewardship and perioperative quality design.

Current-practice boundary

This appraisal evaluates the supplied 2018 article. It does not verify 2026 guideline recommendations, allergy-testing pathways, or local perioperative protocols.
