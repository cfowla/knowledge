One-page synthesis

Core finding. Across 18 observational studies, higher CCL5 was associated with lower odds of unfavorable disease progression (OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.71-0.85; I²=0%) and mortality (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.55-0.76; I²=23.1%). The authors interpret higher CCL5 as a marker of a more competent antiviral response.

The review used a broad multisource search through October 1, 2025, dual screening/extraction, Newcastle-Ottawa Scale risk-of-bias assessment, GRADE certainty assessment, DerSimonian-Laird random-effects pooling, subgroup analyses, meta-regression, publication-bias tests, and several sensitivity analyses. The progression evidence included pediatric and adult cohorts and multiple viruses; mortality evidence was restricted to adults with coronavirus infections.

The most consequential robustness result is not the low I² but the adjustment-stratified analysis: the progression association was significant in unadjusted models (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.70-0.85) but not in multivariable-adjusted models (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.63-1.39). For mortality, the adjusted evidence was a single study (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.55-0.89). This materially limits causal or practice-oriented interpretation of the headline pooled effects.

Internal source discrepancy. The main article reports 11 studies/1214 participants for unfavorable progression; the supplement GRADE eTable reports 11 studies/n=1241 for the same pooled OR. Table 2 subgroup counts (609 + 605) sum to 1214. This makes 1241 look like a likely supplement typographical error, but the discrepancy is preserved rather than silently corrected.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract / question

The review asks whether elevated CCL5 is associated with disease progression and mortality in confirmed respiratory viral infections. Eighteen observational studies (2376 patients) were included. The primary pooled results favored elevated CCL5 for both progression and mortality.

The authors frame CCL5 as a potential prognostic biomarker and as evidence that an intact CCL5 response may reflect competent host antiviral immunity rather than harmful hyperinflammation.

Introduction

The rationale is the clinical heterogeneity of respiratory viral infections and the need for early prognostic biomarkers. CCL5/RANTES is presented as an interferon-stimulated chemokine connecting innate and adaptive immunity through recruitment and survival of antiviral effector cells.

The motivating controversy is whether CCL5 is protective or pathogenic: prior studies linked higher CCL5 to mild disease in some settings but to severe inflammatory injury in others. The meta-analysis was designed to resolve the direction and magnitude of clinical associations across viruses and populations.

Methods

PRISMA-guided, PROSPERO-registered systematic review. Databases were searched through October 1, 2025, without language or geographic restrictions. Eligible reports were observational cohort/case-control studies with confirmed respiratory viral infection, CCL5 measurement in a biological sample, a clinically relevant severity/mortality comparison, and effect-size data. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted, and rated studies, with third-reviewer resolution.

Outcomes were harmonized into unfavorable disease progression and all-cause mortality. OR was the primary pooled metric; HRs/RRs for rare outcomes were treated as OR approximations. Random-effects pooling, I²/Q, interaction tests, REML meta-regression with Knapp-Hartung adjustment, funnel/Begg/Egger tests, trim-and-fill, leave-one-out analysis, and effect-measure/adjustment-status sensitivity analyses were performed.

Results

2787 records yielded 18 studies. The review included 2376 patients; 11 studies assessed progression and 7 assessed mortality. Progression studies spanned pediatric/adult populations and several respiratory viruses; mortality studies were all adult coronavirus cohorts. CCL5 was measured in blood/systemic or respiratory samples using multiple protein or gene-expression assays.

Pooled ORs were 0.78 for progression and 0.65 for mortality, with a significant between-outcome interaction (P=.047). Virus and age subgroup results remained below 1 without significant interactions. OR-only sensitivity analyses were similar. However, adjustment-stratified progression results attenuated to OR 0.94 (95% CI 0.63-1.39) in multivariable-adjusted models. NOS ratings were 12 high and 6 moderate; GRADE was reported as moderate certainty for both outcomes.

Discussion and conclusions

The authors interpret the association as evidence that higher CCL5 generally marks a competent antiviral response across the viruses studied. They propose possible use for early risk stratification and discuss future biomarker-guided immunomodulation hypotheses.

They explicitly call for prospective multicenter validation, standardized assays, and clinical cutoffs. Limitations include few studies in some subgroups, heterogeneous assays and severity definitions, mixing OR/HR/RR and different regression models, variable covariate adjustment, inability to exclude publication bias, residual confounding, and lack of causal inference.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. The paper has a useful pooled signal and a clearly framed mechanistic question, but it is not sufficient to support routine CCL5 testing or CCL5-directed treatment. The adjustment sensitivity and assay/outcome heterogeneity are the most important reasons to avoid practice-level overinterpretation.
Relevance7/10

Useful infectious-disease biomarker synthesis with plausible translational value.

Evidence basis: 18 studies across several respiratory viruses and clinically important outcomes.

Limiting factor: biomarker is not yet operationalized for routine care.

Would rise: validated assay thresholds tied to decisions.

Novelty7/10

Authors describe this as the first quantitative synthesis of CCL5 prognosis across respiratory viruses.

Evidence basis: cross-virus pooling and outcome comparison.

Limiting factor: contribution is synthesis rather than new prospective data.

Would rise: individual-patient meta-analysis or prospective validation.

Method strength6/10

Broad search, dual review, prespecified protocol, risk-of-bias assessment, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses are strengths.

Limiting factor: mixed OR/HR/RR pooling, heterogeneous regression adjustment, and small study counts.

Would rise: harmonized effect measures and stronger adjusted-data synthesis.

Evidence strength5/10

The pooled associations are precise and directionally consistent, but all underlying evidence is observational.

Limiting factor: progression effect loses significance in the multivariable-adjusted subset; mortality adjusted evidence is one study.

Would rise: prospective cohorts with standardized measurement and robust adjustment/external validation.

External validity5/10

Multiple viruses and pediatric/adult progression cohorts improve breadth.

Limiting factor: mortality is adult coronavirus-only; assays, sample types, and severity definitions vary.

Would rise: standardized multicenter cohorts across additional viruses/settings.

Implementation value3/10

The paper defines a research direction but not an executable clinical pathway.

Limiting factor: no validated cutoff, timing rule, standardized assay, or intervention trial.

Would rise: prospective decision-impact study with reproducible assay/cutoff and management algorithm.

Strengths

  • Protocol registration and PRISMA-aligned reporting.
  • Broad database search and dual-review workflow.
  • Detailed sensitivity analyses, including effect-measure restriction, adjustment-status stratification, trim-and-fill, and leave-one-out analysis.
  • Explicit risk-of-bias and certainty framework with supplement-level detail.
  • Authors acknowledge major methodological limitations rather than presenting the pooled ORs as causal.

Critical limitations

  • Confounding: the adjusted-only progression result is null (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.63-1.39), making the overall progression headline vulnerable to confounding.
  • Mortality adjusted evidence: only one multivariable-adjusted mortality estimate is reported in the adjustment-stratified sensitivity analysis.
  • Composite outcome heterogeneity: ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, ARDS, hospitalization, and study-defined severity are merged under progression.
  • Measurement heterogeneity: systemic vs respiratory samples, protein assays vs qPCR, and varying CCL5 definitions/cutoffs.
  • Effect-measure heterogeneity: ORs, HRs, and RRs are pooled under an approximation framework.
  • Internal reporting discrepancies: 1214 vs 1241 participants for progression and inconsistent Supplement 1/2 cross-references.

Claim discipline

Supported: elevated CCL5 is associated with better outcomes in the included observational evidence, and the pooled signal is robust to OR-only and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses.
Not established: elevated CCL5 is causally protective; a specific CCL5 threshold should be used clinically; routine CCL5 testing improves outcomes; boosting CCL5 or blocking CCR5 improves outcomes in biomarker-selected patients.

Clinical / implementation translation

Clinical question (PICO-like)

P: patients with confirmed respiratory viral infections.
E: elevated CCL5 in biological samples.
C: low/normal CCL5.
O: unfavorable progression and all-cause mortality.

Use now?

No practice change from this review alone. The evidence is prognostic association, not a validated diagnostic/therapeutic pathway.

Best use

Background for biomarker research, host-response phenotyping, and designing prospective multicenter studies.

Do not use for

Routine admission CCL5 ordering, a treatment threshold, or CCL5/CCR5-directed therapy selection without separate supporting evidence.

Most useful next evidence would be a prospective, multicenter cohort using a standardized assay at a predefined time point, a prespecified CCL5 cutoff, externally validated prediction performance, adjustment for major clinical confounders, and a decision-impact analysis showing whether CCL5 adds information beyond routine severity markers.
