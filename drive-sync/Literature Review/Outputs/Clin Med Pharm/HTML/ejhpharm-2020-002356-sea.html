One-page synthesis

Core thesis. In this single-centre retrospective cohort, DRPs were frequent: 174/209 patients (83.3%) had at least one, with 274 DRPs and a mean 1.31±1.00 per patient. The dominant PCNE problem was Other/P3 (62.8%), driven by unnecessary drug treatment; the dominant cause domain was Drug selection/C1 (81.8%). Dose-timing instructions were a notable safety signal, and traditional Chinese medicine injections ranked highest among medication categories associated with DRPs.

The study is useful as a structured description of medication-review targets in hospitalised PD, especially unnecessary therapy, contraindicated/no-indication prescribing, and administration timing around levodopa. It is not an intervention trial. Its main inferential value is local burden and pattern recognition rather than proof that pharmacist review reduces harm, length of stay, or cost.

Critical source-integrity issue. Severity results conflict across sections: the abstract reports 88.7% B-D / 11.3% E-H; Results/Table 3 reports 85.4% (234/274) / 14.6% (40/274); Discussion reports 82.5% / 17.5%. Additional denominator and table inconsistencies are present. These are preserved rather than silently corrected.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors identify a gap in Chinese inpatient PD data on DRP type and severity and retrospectively review 209 neurology inpatients. DRPs were classified with PCNE v8.03 and severity with NCC MERP. The abstract reports 274 DRPs, 1.31±1.00 per patient, and 83.3% of patients with at least one DRP.

The abstract’s severity percentages (88.7% B-D; 11.3% E-H) conflict with the Results/Table 3 and Discussion values. Its conclusion that high DRP prevalence warrants subtype identification is descriptive and does not establish that identification itself improves outcomes.

Introduction

The rationale is clinically coherent: PD commonly requires complex medication regimens in older adults with comorbidity, creating opportunities for adverse reactions, inappropriate prescribing, dosing problems, interactions, and other DRPs. The paper defines DRPs as medication-related events or circumstances that actually or potentially interfere with desired health outcomes.

The authors frame the work as filling a lack of inpatient PD DRP studies in China and as preliminary information for future work. Background claims about pharmacist benefit derive from cited literature rather than this study.

Methods

This was a retrospective study in one tertiary neurology department. Inclusion required idiopathic PD diagnosed by a neurology specialist under cited Chinese criteria and at least one anti-PD medication daily; exclusions included atypical/secondary parkinsonism, uncertain diagnosis, stay under 48 hours, and missing relevant data. Data came from EMR/HIS records. A Raosoft calculation indicated at least 197 patients.

PCNE v8.03 classified DRPs. Neurology-trained clinical pharmacists identified DRPs and a neurologist confirmed them; two pharmacists independently assessed DRP/severity and resolved disagreements by discussion. Because pharmacists did not contact patients or provide medication appraisal to prescribers, Dispensing, Drug use process, and Patient-related cause codes were excluded. Analyses were largely unadjusted descriptive/group comparisons, with no reported multivariable model or inter-rater statistic.

Results

Of 209 patients, 174 (83.3%) had at least one DRP; 274 total DRPs were identified, averaging 1.31±1.00 per patient. P3/Other accounted for 172/274 (62.8%), P1/Treatment effectiveness 53/274 (19.3%), and P2/Treatment safety 49/274 (17.9%). Unnecessary drug treatment was 162/274 (59.1%). Cause domains were C1/Drug selection 224/274 (81.8%), C3/Dose selection 40/274 (14.6%), and C4/Treatment duration 10/274 (3.6%). C3.5 wrong/unclear/missing dose-timing instructions accounted for 34/274 (12.8%).

Table 3 gives 234/274 (85.4%) B-D and 40/274 (14.6%) E-H. Of the 40 E-H events, 34 (85.0%) were C3.5 timing problems. Figure 1 ranks traditional Chinese medicine injections highest among medication categories causing DRPs, followed by neurotrophic agents and anti-PD agents. Table 4 reports 30 levodopa-associated DRPs, 6 selegiline, 4 trihexyphenidyl, and 3 entacapone; the table’s percentage denominator is not stated clearly and is not the number of users for each drug.

Discussion

The authors compare their 1.31 DRPs/patient and 83.3% patient prevalence with earlier studies but acknowledge that methods and populations differ. They argue for enhanced clinical pharmacy services and discuss local medication patterns, including traditional Chinese medicine injections, neurotrophic agents, trimetazidine, and flupentixol/melitracen. They emphasize dose timing for levodopa and report that 12.4% of prescriptions lacked clear administration-time instructions.

The discussion itself contains numerical and terminology inconsistencies: it reports 82.5% B-D / 17.5% E-H rather than Table 3’s 85.4%/14.6%, and refers to “Treatment duration, C3” even though Table 3 labels C3 as Dose selection and C4 as Treatment duration. The authors appropriately acknowledge retrospective under-ascertainment, missing adherence/PD-severity data, limited generalisability, and partial use of PCNE.

Conclusion

The authors conclude that DRPs are frequent and that pharmacist awareness and early identification may improve prevention and management. This is a reasonable hypothesis-generating implication but was not tested as an intervention.

The conclusion also says PD patients were more likely to be non-adherent, particularly to levodopa. That claim is not supported by a reported adherence endpoint and conflicts with the Methods statement that medication compliance could not be comprehensively identified; it should not be treated as a study result.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. Best used as a hypothesis-generating, local inpatient medication-safety map. Do not use it as a national prevalence benchmark or as proof that pharmacist intervention improves outcomes.
7/10

Directly relevant to inpatient clinical pharmacy and medication safety in Parkinson’s disease.

Evidence basis
209 inpatient PD cases, PCNE-coded DRPs, medication-class and timing findings.
Principal limit
Single-centre descriptive scope.

Implementation value

Useful for forming PD inpatient medication-review checks, especially dose timing and unnecessary therapy, but not sufficient to prove an intervention improves outcomes.

Evidence basis
C3.5 timing issues, P3.2 unnecessary therapy, medication-category ranking.
Principal limit
No tested pharmacist intervention, no post-intervention outcomes, source inconsistencies.
Would raise/lower
Prospective intervention study with acceptance, harm prevention, LOS, and patient-centered outcomes.

Validity and bias

Selection/ascertainment: retrospective records and missing PD-severity/adherence information may undercount DRPs. Classification: PCNE use was partial because several cause domains were excluded. Measurement reliability: two pharmacists independently assessed DRPs/severity, but no kappa or other agreement statistic is reported. Statistics: mostly descriptive/unadjusted; no causal model. Reporting integrity: multiple incompatible severity percentages, impossible-looking Table 1 totals, mixed denominators, and a conclusion-level adherence claim not grounded in measured adherence.

Clinical interpretation

This study supports a practical review hypothesis: inpatient PD medication review should pay particular attention to unnecessary/non-indicated therapy, contraindicated agents, and administration timing, especially around levodopa. It does not quantify the effect of implementing such a review, and some medication patterns (for example traditional Chinese medicine injections) may be specific to the study setting.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use now
Use the reported DRP categories as prompts for medication reconciliation/review: indication, contraindication, duplicate/unnecessary therapy, dose timing, and duration.
High-value PD-specific check
Verify timing instructions for levodopa-containing therapy relative to meals; the study’s high-severity events were heavily concentrated in the C3.5 timing category.
Do not overclaim
Do not cite this paper as evidence that pharmacist intervention reduces harm, costs, or length of stay; no intervention outcome was tested.
Research next
Prioritise prospective multicentre studies with complete PCNE capture, explicit denominators, adjudication reliability, and measured clinical outcomes after pharmacist intervention.
