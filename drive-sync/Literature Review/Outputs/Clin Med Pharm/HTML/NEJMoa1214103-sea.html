One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In carefully selected, mechanically ventilated adults with persistent severe ARDS, early prolonged prone positioning added to lung-protective ventilation produced a large mortality reduction compared with supine management.

PROSEVA randomized 466 analyzable patients (237 prone, 229 supine) after a 12-24-hour stabilization period. Severe ARDS required PaO2:FiO2 <150 mm Hg with FiO2 ≥0.6, PEEP ≥5 cm H2O, and tidal volume near 6 mL/kg predicted body weight. Prone patients were turned within 1 hour and kept prone for at least 16 consecutive hours per session, generally daily until improvement or day 28. Both arms used a low-tidal-volume ventilation strategy with plateau-pressure limitation.

The primary result was 28-day mortality of 16.0% with prone positioning versus 32.8% with supine positioning (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.25-0.63; P<0.001). Ninety-day mortality was 23.6% versus 41.0% (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.29-0.67; P<0.001). The supplementary appendix reported an NNT of 6 based on day-28 mortality and adjusted analyses remained strongly favorable. Successful extubation and ventilation-free days also favored prone positioning. Cardiac arrests were less frequent in the prone arm, while other measured adverse events were not significantly different.

Why it matters: The trial combined severity enrichment, early intervention, long proning sessions, lung-protective ventilation, and experienced centers. Those features are central to interpreting why its result differed from earlier proning trials.

Section condensation

Abstract / clinical question

Earlier randomized trials had improved oxygenation with prone positioning without clear survival benefit. PROSEVA asked whether applying prone positioning early and for prolonged sessions in severe ARDS would improve outcomes.

The abstract accurately previews the decisive findings: 28-day and 90-day mortality were both markedly lower in the prone arm, without an overall excess in complications.

Methods — population and design

Adults with ARDS required invasive ventilation for less than 36 hours and persistent severe hypoxemia after a 12-24-hour stabilization period. Randomization was centralized, computer-generated, and stratified by ICU. Outcome assessors were reported as unaware of treatment assignment, while bedside data collectors necessarily knew positioning assignment.

The protocol excluded important contraindications to proning and several clinical contexts that could increase procedural risk or confound prognosis, including unstable fractures, intracranial hypertension, recent sternotomy/tracheal or major facial surgery, pregnancy, ECMO before inclusion, chronic respiratory failure requiring oxygen/NIV, and a life expectancy under one year.

Methods — intervention and cointerventions

Prone positioning began within 1 hour of randomization and lasted at least 16 consecutive hours per session. The supplement specified a coordinated 3-4-person turning process and standardized protection of tubes, lines, pressure points, head/neck position, and patient alignment. Proning was repeated daily until oxygenation-based stopping criteria, major deterioration/complication, or day 28.

Both groups were treated with volume-controlled lung-protective ventilation targeting approximately 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, PEEP guided by a PEEP-FiO2 table, and plateau pressure ≤30 cm H2O. This reduces the likelihood that the survival difference was simply an artifact of grossly dissimilar ventilation strategies.

Methods — outcomes and statistics

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality at day 28. Secondary endpoints included 90-day mortality, successful extubation, time to extubation, ICU stay, complications, noninvasive ventilation, tracheotomy, organ-dysfunction-free days, and respiratory mechanics. The planned sample size assumed 60% control mortality and 90% power to detect an absolute 15-point reduction.

Analysis used Kaplan-Meier survival curves, log-rank comparison, and Cox proportional-hazards regression stratified by center. The trial used a planned interim analysis and no futility rule. Reported P values were two-sided and not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Results

Among 474 randomized patients, 466 were analyzed. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, although SOFA score, vasopressor use, and neuromuscular-blocker use differed statistically. Day-28 mortality was 16.0% versus 32.8%; day-90 mortality was 23.6% versus 41.0%, both strongly favoring prone positioning. Adjusted analyses in the supplement remained favorable.

Successful extubation by day 90 was 80.5% in the prone group versus 65.0% in the supine group. Ventilation-free days were higher at days 28 and 90. Seventeen control-assigned patients (7.4%) crossed to prone positioning as rescue therapy. Cardiac arrest was lower in prone patients (6.8% vs 13.5%); other listed adverse events were not significantly different.

Discussion / limitations

The authors attributed the difference from prior trials to selection of more severely hypoxemic patients, confirmation of severity after stabilization, early and prolonged proning, and modern lung-protective ventilation. They proposed reduced overdistention and more homogeneous stress/strain as a plausible mechanism, while acknowledging that alveolar recruitment was not directly measured.

Generalizability is constrained by implementation expertise: participating centers were experienced in turning patients prone, and the authors explicitly note the procedure's technical complexity and need for coordinated teamwork. Because patients were returned supine at least daily, the specific effect of the prone position cannot be fully separated from repeated repositioning.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-impact landmark critical-care RCT with a large patient-centered mortality effect and unusually strong implementation relevance.
Relevance — 10/10
Directly addresses a high-stakes ICU intervention with mortality, ventilation, and safety outcomes.
Limit: applicability depends on matching the severity/implementation context.
Novelty — 9/10
Demonstrated a survival benefit where earlier proning trials had generally not done so, using a distinct severity-enriched, early, prolonged-session strategy.
Would be lower if the trial merely reproduced prior effects without design changes.
Method strength — 9/10
Randomized multicenter design, prespecified primary mortality endpoint, strong follow-up, standardized ventilation/proning, and adjusted analyses.
Limits: open bedside intervention, baseline imbalances, post-randomization exclusions, and a one-sided design assumption in sample-size planning.
Evidence strength — 9/10
Large, precise, internally consistent mortality effect at 28 and 90 days with robustness to covariate adjustment and clinically coherent secondary outcomes.
Would be lowered by loss of effect in adjusted analyses, major missing outcome data, or safety excess; these were not observed.
External validity — 8/10
Multicenter ICU population supports generalizability to severe ARDS, but centers were experienced in proning and eligibility excluded several high-risk contexts.
Would rise with similarly strong effectiveness data across less-experienced routine-care settings.
Implementation value — 10/10
The intervention timing, duration, stopping rules, ventilator strategy, team requirements, and rescue criteria are unusually concrete and operationalizable.
Implementation still requires trained staff and local safety processes.

Validity

Strengths: patient-centered primary endpoint; randomized multicenter design; complete reported 90-day follow-up for analyzed participants; standardized cointervention strategy; strong effect size; supplement-supported robustness analyses; consistent survival curve and secondary outcomes.

Constraints: post-randomization exclusions complicate a strict interpretation of intention-to-treat, although a supplementary sensitivity analysis including the eight excluded patients produced nearly identical mortality results. Bedside blinding was impossible. Baseline SOFA, vasopressor use, and neuromuscular-blocker use differed, but covariate-adjusted mortality estimates remained strongly favorable. Technical expertise at participating centers limits simple transfer to teams without proning experience.

Do not overclaim: the trial does not establish that any duration, timing, or severity threshold for proning is equivalent to the studied strategy. It also does not directly prove the proposed mechanistic explanation of reduced lung stress/strain.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Population fidelity
The strongest direct evidence is for persistent severe ARDS after stabilization, not indiscriminate proning of all mechanically ventilated patients.
Timing and dose of intervention
Early initiation and long sessions (≥16 h) are part of the tested package, not incidental details.
Co-intervention
Interpret the benefit within a lung-protective ventilation strategy with low tidal volume and plateau-pressure limitation.
Operational safety
Successful implementation requires a coordinated team trained to protect airway devices, vascular lines, pressure points, and hemodynamics during turns.

Best use: landmark evidence for prone positioning as a mortality-reducing strategy in severe ARDS and as a reference for protocol design, patient selection, session duration, and staffing requirements.

Do not use for: extrapolating without qualification to mild hypoxemia, non-intubated patients, or settings unable to reproduce safe proning workflows.

Confidence in appraisal: high, because the primary article, supplied protocol, and supplementary appendix were all reviewed and the principal mortality, safety, subgroup, and implementation claims reconcile across them.
