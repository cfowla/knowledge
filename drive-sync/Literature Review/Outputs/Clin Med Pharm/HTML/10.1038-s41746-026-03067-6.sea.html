Core thesis. A locally deployed, open-source LLM can use a frozen snapshot of the current physician roster plus expert-authored clinical routing rules to assign one department–subspecialty destination from unstructured referral letters. The evaluated configuration was prompt-based rather than fine-tuned and returned a constrained JSON object for downstream workflow integration.

What the evidence shows. Strict concordance with historical coordinator assignments was moderate-to-high, and post-hoc review accepted additional nonmatching predictions as clinically reasonable. Performance was materially lower for subspecialties with fewer than five test examples. Remaining errors clustered at clinically adjacent boundaries—especially cardiothoracic surgery versus pulmonology and neurosurgery versus neurology—rather than being uniformly random. Six of 104 post-adjudication discordant referrals contained insufficient narrative detail for a unique assignment.

What matters most. The roster-as-context design is the paper’s strongest implementation idea because it separates dynamic operational availability from model weights. The study also exposes a genuine evaluation problem: a single historical destination is an imperfect reference when several adjacent services could reasonably manage the referral.

Main constraint. The paper does not compare against alternative models, simpler baselines, or prospective human workflow performance. Post-hoc adjudication is clinically sensible but subjective; blinding, interrater reliability, adjudicator disagreement, and prespecified equivalence criteria are not reported. Data, code, supplementary analyses, latency, cost, calibration, abstention behavior, and safety-monitoring procedures are not available in the supplied manuscript.

Section condensation

Abstract · page 4

Problem, intervention, and headline result

The manuscript frames manual tertiary-hospital referral triage as resource-intensive and inconsistent. It evaluates an on-premises Qwen-2.5-32B model using roster-embedded prompting on 6,624 electronic referral letters. In the 680-referral hold-out set, strict accuracy against historical coordinator assignments was 75.4%, increasing to 84.7% after post-hoc expert review of discordant cases.

The abstract claims that the system can complement human referral workflows and reduce administrative burden. The reported study, however, measures classification agreement rather than administrative time, burden, safety, cost, or prospective operational outcomes.

Introduction · pages 5–6

Rationale and claimed gap

The introduction contrasts manual review of unstructured narratives with prior rule-based, conventional NLP, and machine-learning approaches. The authors argue that detailed subspecialty triage requires contextual interpretation and adaptation to changing clinician availability, which static rules or retrained classifiers may handle poorly.

The proposed contribution is a real-world, privacy-preserving alternative to cloud-API and synthetic-ground-truth evaluations: an open-source model hosted inside the hospital, evaluated against actual operational assignments, and constrained by a point-in-time clinician roster.

Results · pages 8–9

Triage performance

Prompt tuning was cumulative: Prompt 1 contained persona and instruction hierarchy; Prompt 2 added primary specialty criteria; Prompt 3 added subspecialty differential logic. The manuscript states that concordance increased from Prompt 1 to Prompt 3 in the most frequently referred group, but the numerical tuning results are confined to unavailable Supplementary Figure S1.

On the hold-out set, Prompt 3 achieved 75.4% strict accuracy and 84.7% post-hoc adjudicated accuracy. The long-tail subgroup had markedly worse performance: 61.0% strict and 74.4% adjudicated for 82 referrals from subspecialties with fewer than five cases, versus 77.4% and 86.1% for 598 referrals from more frequent subspecialties.

Results · pages 8–9

Discordant assignment patterns

After adjudication, 104 cases remained discordant. Fifty (48.1%) preserved the historical department but differed at the subspecialty level; 54 (51.9%) crossed departments. Errors concentrated at adjacent clinical boundaries, with cardiothoracic surgery → pulmonology described as the most common cross-department direction and pulmonology overrepresented among predictions.

Within-department errors were common in cardiology, pulmonology, and gastroenterology. Six of 104 discordant cases (5.8%) contained brief, nonspecific referral narratives without enough context to support one unique destination. The cited case-level and confusion details are in unavailable Supplementary Tables S2–S4.

Discussion · pages 9–14

Authors’ interpretation

The authors argue that strict single-label concordance underestimates clinical validity because real referral scopes overlap and the operational record captures only one finalized destination. They interpret the 9.3-percentage-point increase after adjudication as evidence that many mismatches were acceptable rather than clinically wrong.

They also interpret the error topology as a preference for diagnostic over interventional services when procedural intent is absent. The proposed system is explicitly positioned as human-in-the-loop decision support, not an autonomous replacement.

Discussion · pages 12–14

Limitations and conclusion

The manuscript acknowledges lower performance in rare subspecialties, single-center and institution-specific data, evaluation of only one model and one deterministic configuration, and the absence of prospective operational outcomes. External validation, model comparisons, decoding studies, processing-time measurement, and long-term cost analysis are deferred to future work.

The conclusion describes the roster-aware architecture as practical, scalable, and privacy-preserving. “Scalable” is an implementation claim rather than an empirically tested outcome: the study does not report throughput, latency, infrastructure utilization, maintenance workload, or multi-site deployment.

Methods · pages 14–16

Study design and data preparation

The IRB-approved study initially collected 12,246 referrals from 2023. It excluded 5,057 Partners Portal referrals to preserve standardized formatting and 565 referrals assigned to unavailable subspecialties. The remaining 6,624 HIRA referrals were split 90:10 into a 5,944-referral prompt-tuning set and a 680-referral hold-out set, stratified by historical department assignment and calendar quarter.

The split preserves broad clinical and temporal distributions but is not a forward temporal validation. The exclusion of portal referrals narrows format variability and may improve internal consistency at the cost of external realism.

Methods · pages 18–19

System implementation

Qwen-2.5-32B ran in the hospital’s secure infrastructure with external network access disabled. The system prompt contained a frozen point-in-time snapshot of active departments and subspecialties plus optional descriptive tags; the user prompt contained the de-identified referral narrative.

The evaluated system returned one structured JSON object containing the predicted department and subspecialty. Although the architecture is intended to adapt by replacing the roster snapshot, the roster was frozen during evaluation to avoid performance drift.

Methods · pages 19–21

Prompt design

No model weights were updated. The final prompt combined: a referral-coordinator persona and instruction hierarchy; primary criteria based on diagnosis, anatomy, pathology, and symptoms; expert-authored differentials for adjacent subspecialties; and roster/output constraints. Rules included pediatric routing, procedural keywords favoring surgery, thyroid medical-versus-surgical distinctions, and central-versus-peripheral dizziness routing.

This is best understood as a hybrid LLM-plus-rule architecture embedded in natural-language instructions. Its behavior depends on prompt maintenance, ontology quality, and institutional rule ownership even though it avoids model retraining.

Methods · pages 21–23

Performance evaluation

The primary metric was exact assignment accuracy against historical coordinator labels. Every mismatch underwent post-hoc review by referral coordinators; predictions judged clinically equivalent or acceptable were reclassified as correct. Remaining cases were analyzed at department and department–subspecialty levels, with a qualitative review for low-information referrals.

The adjudication strategy addresses label ambiguity but introduces a second subjective reference standard. The manuscript does not report prespecified acceptance criteria, number of reviewers per case, blinding to the historical label or model output, consensus procedures, or interrater reliability.

Methods and disclosures · pages 23–24

Implementation, availability, funding, and conflicts

Inference and evaluation used Python 3.10.12, pandas, and scikit-learn on two NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPUs. The referral-level dataset is restricted for privacy and ethical reasons. Deployment and integration code are not public because of institutional security and proprietary infrastructure constraints, though qualified researchers may request access.

The work was funded by Korean government technology and health R&D programs. The authors declared no competing financial or non-financial interests.

Data leakage and tuning boundaries

The manuscript reports a held-out 10% test set and prompt development on the remaining 90%, which is an appropriate basic separation. It does not report a locked prompt-development protocol, number of tuning iterations, analyst exposure to test outcomes, or whether any test-set inspection informed later prompt changes. No evidence of leakage is reported, but the available methods are insufficient to audit the boundary fully.

Reference-standard validity

Historical assignments are operationally authentic but not necessarily a unique clinical truth. Post-hoc adjudication directly addresses this limitation, yet it may also inflate performance if acceptance criteria are permissive or reviewers are influenced by the original and predicted labels. A stronger design would use multiple blinded specialist reviewers, prespecified acceptability categories, interrater reliability, and a three-way outcome: correct, acceptable alternative, and unsafe/inappropriate.

Safety and human factors

The authors appropriately recommend a human-in-the-loop role. The paper does not define which cases should bypass automation, what confidence or information threshold should trigger manual review, how roster mismatches are detected, how invalid output is handled, or whether automation changes coordinator vigilance. These are deployment-critical questions rather than minor implementation details.

Implementation and practice takeaways

1. Treat the roster as versioned contentStore a timestamped roster snapshot with each inference. Validate department–subspecialty combinations before model use and preserve the exact roster version in the audit record.
2. Separate structural and clinical validityFirst validate JSON and roster membership; then evaluate whether the clinical destination is acceptable. A syntactically valid label can still be clinically inappropriate.
3. Add abstentionThe source forces exactly one label. A safer implementation should support “insufficient information,” “multiple acceptable destinations,” and “manual review required.”
4. Use hierarchical outputsEvaluate department correctness separately from subspecialty correctness and retain acceptable-alternative labels. This aligns better with overlapping clinical scope than exact strings alone.
5. Protect the long tailRare services need targeted test sets, minimum-case thresholds, error-severity review, and possibly deterministic routing rules rather than reliance on aggregate accuracy.
6. Predefine adjudicationUse blinded independent reviewers, explicit equivalence criteria, disagreement resolution, and interrater reliability. Preserve “acceptable alternative” separately from exact correctness.
7. Measure workflow outcomesTrack coordinator time, queue time, acceptance/override rate, downstream rerouting, delay, unsafe assignment, availability, latency, and total infrastructure cost.
8. Version prompts as clinical contentPrompt rules function as a clinical routing policy. They require ownership, review dates, change control, validation fixtures, rollback, and drift monitoring.
