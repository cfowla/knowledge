One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a small randomized ACS cohort, weight-adjusted IV UFH produced higher early APTT values and fewer subtherapeutic values at selected timepoints than a fixed-dose standard-care nomogram, but it also produced marked early supratherapeutic APTT values and was subsequently revised by the authors.

The trial randomized 64 patients with acute coronary syndromes who were not treated with thrombolysis. The tested weight-based regimen began with 80 U/kg IV followed by 18 U/kg/h; the standard regimen used 5000 U followed by 1000 U/h. APTT was checked at baseline and 6, 12, 18, 24, and 48 hours, targeting 60-90 seconds. Exact median APTT values reported in the Abstract were 150 vs 83 seconds at 6 hours (p=0.001), 100 vs 79 at 12 hours (p=0.09), 66 vs 61 at 18 hours (p=0.005), 63 vs 56 at 24 hours (p=0.09), and 64 vs 56 at 48 hours (p=0.11), favoring higher anticoagulation intensity with the weight-based arm.

Interpretation is limited by a surrogate endpoint, n=64, one center, no power calculation, incomplete reporting of allocation safeguards and missing observations, and several internal numeric inconsistencies. No major bleeding events were reported, so safety cannot be meaningfully compared. The historical contribution is strongest as evidence for weight-based UFH dosing logic and protocolized titration, not for the exact 80/18 regimen or its APTT target.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The Abstract frames uncertainty about optimal UFH dosing and reports a randomized comparison of fixed-dose and weight-adjusted nomograms. It states that the weight-adjusted strategy achieved higher median APTT values early and concludes that it improved anticoagulation in the first 24 hours.

A key source inconsistency appears here: the Abstract reports 18-hour subtherapeutic APTT rates of 11% versus 26%, while the body Results reports 23% versus 48%. This appraisal preserves the conflict rather than choosing one version as correct.

Introduction / rationale

The paper links heparin effectiveness to achieving APTT above a minimally therapeutic threshold while avoiding excessive prolongation associated with bleeding. It notes prior heparin nomograms in venous thromboembolic disease and argues that heavier patients were more often subtherapeutic after ACS, motivating weight adjustment.

The authors position the trial as a prospective evaluation of a weight-based regimen in ACS and explicitly connect it to the then-recent unstable-angina guideline context.

Methods

Patients admitted to a coronary care unit or medicine ward from July 1994 through February 1995 and intended to receive heparin for coronary artery syndromes were randomized by envelope to one of two nomograms. Nurses were aware of assignment and executed preprinted dosing rules; post-study reviewers were blinded to assignment when reviewing complications. APTT was measured at baseline and 6, 12, 18, 24, and 48 hours with a target of 60-90 seconds.

APTT used an automated reagent/coagulation system and plasma heparin concentration was measured with a chromogenic assay. Major/minor hemorrhage and thrombocytopenia definitions were prespecified, although some printed units are unusual. Continuous variables used Wilcoxon rank-sum testing and discrete variables Fisher exact testing; no sample-size calculation is reported.

Results

The cohort contained 64 patients (31 male; mean age reported as 60 ± 11.4 years), with unstable angina the largest indication. Baseline characteristics were described as similar between groups. Weight-adjusted dosing produced markedly higher median APTT at 6 hours (150 vs 83 seconds, p=0.001) and a smaller but statistically significant difference at 18 hours (66 vs 61 seconds, p=0.005); other timepoints did not reach p<0.05 in the Abstract comparisons.

The body Results reports fewer subtherapeutic APTT values in the weight-adjusted arm at 6 and 18 hours, but exact percentages and p values are not consistently aligned with the Abstract/Table 2. Figure 2 shows correlation between APTT and heparin concentration at 12 hours (r=0.76, p=0.0002, n=18). No major complications occurred; one minor event was reported in the weight-based group.

Discussion

The authors interpret the findings as evidence that weight-based dosing gives more effective early anticoagulation in ACS, while acknowledging uncertainty about the optimal therapeutic APTT range. They compare their results with earlier thromboembolic-disease work and note a decline over 24 hours in the percentage of weight-based patients above 60 seconds.

Importantly, the authors did not treat their tested nomogram as final: because early APTT values were high, they report reducing the initial bolus from 80 to 70 U/kg and modifying subsequent adjustment steps. Those post-study changes were not tested in this randomized trial and should not be conflated with trial evidence.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin deep dive; do not use for current dosing.
Best use: historical evidence for weight-based UFH dosing, explicit nomogram architecture, and early APTT attainment. Do not use for: direct contemporary ACS dosing, bleeding-risk quantification, or proof of improved ischemic outcomes.
Relevance
9/10

Directly addresses weight-based UFH nomogram performance in ACS and is highly relevant to a heparin dosing evidence history.

Evidence basis: Randomized comparison with explicit dosing/monitoring protocol.

Limiting factor: Historical population and obsolete practice context.

Would change score: Higher if the question is specifically the origin of ACS weight-based UFH nomograms; lower for contemporary bedside dosing.

Novelty
7/10

For its 1995 context, it prospectively tested weight-based heparin dosing specifically in acute coronary syndromes.

Evidence basis: Authors position it as the first prospective evaluation of the guideline-advocated weight-based regimen in this setting.

Limiting factor: Prior weight-based heparin work already existed in thromboembolic disease.

Would change score: Higher with evidence of unique clinical outcome benefit; lower when judged only by current-day novelty.

Method strength
5/10

Randomization and prespecified serial APTT assessment strengthen inference, but the trial is small and incompletely reports allocation methods and missing observations.

Evidence basis: Prospective randomized design, objective laboratory endpoint, blinded complication review.

Limiting factor: n=64, no power calculation, unblinded treatment, unclear allocation concealment, varying denominators.

Would change score: Higher with concealed randomization, prespecified power, complete follow-up accounting, and multicenter execution.

Evidence strength
5/10

The study supports better early laboratory anticoagulation but not patient-centered efficacy or comparative safety.

Evidence basis: Large early APTT separation and lower subtherapeutic proportions at selected timepoints.

Limiting factor: Surrogate endpoint, no confidence intervals, no major bleeding events, internal numeric inconsistencies.

Would change score: Higher with clinical ischemic/bleeding outcomes, effect estimates with uncertainty, and internally consistent reporting.

External validity
4/10

Generalizability is limited by one center, 1994-1995 ACS care, assay-specific targets, and exclusion of thrombolytic recipients.

Evidence basis: Broad ACS labels and practical ward/CCU workflow.

Limiting factor: Major changes in ACS antithrombotic therapy, invasive care, and UFH dosing/monitoring since publication.

Would change score: Higher with replication across contemporary centers, assays, body weights, and ACS treatment strategies.

Implementation value
6/10

The paper provides an unusually explicit nomogram and adjustment workflow, useful for historical protocol design analysis but not as a current dosing template.

Evidence basis: Table 1 fully specifies bolus/infusion rules and serial monitoring.

Limiting factor: Authors themselves revised the regimen after observing excessive early APTT values; current guidelines supersede it.

Would change score: Higher if paired with modern evidence and local assay calibration before implementation.

Main strengths

  • Prospective randomized comparison with an explicit protocolized dosing algorithm.
  • Objective, frequent laboratory measurements across the first 48 hours.
  • Directly tests the core question of weight-adjusted versus fixed-dose initiation in ACS.
  • Reports the complete adjustment nomogram and a heparin-assay/APTT correlation check.

Main limitations

  • Small, single-center study with no reported power calculation.
  • Surrogate APTT endpoint; no clinical efficacy endpoint and too few bleeding events for safety comparison.
  • Randomization/concealment methods incompletely described and treatment unblinded.
  • Varying denominators across timepoints without a clear missing-data accounting.
  • Several internal numeric/reporting inconsistencies in the Abstract and Table 2.
  • Historical ACS care and assay-specific targets sharply limit present-day transportability.

Clinical practice translation and external verification

Current-practice boundary: The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI ACS guideline has replaced the 1994 unstable-angina guidance cited by this trial. Modern UFH dosing is context-specific, and an official 2025 correction updated the fibrinolytic-therapy UFH dosing row and therapeutic APTT note. Therefore the trial’s 80 U/kg + 18 U/kg/h regimen and 60-90-second APTT target should not be copied into current ACS protocols without contemporary guideline/local-lab reconciliation.

What remains reusable: weight-normalized initial dosing as a design principle; explicit titration rules; early scheduled reassessment; laboratory-method awareness; and separating initiation rules from adjustment rules.

What should not be carried forward uncritically: the exact bolus/infusion values, the exact 60-90-second target, the source’s unusual printed safety units, or the assumption that improved APTT attainment implies improved patient outcomes.
