One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-utility operational guideline chapter for glycemic monitoring, individualized targets, hypoglycemia prevention/treatment, sick-day medication management, and outpatient DKA/HHS prevention.

Core thesis. Glycemic management should combine A1C with CGM/BGM as appropriate, target clinically useful default ranges without rigidly applying them, and continuously trade glycemic benefit against hypoglycemia, functional status, comorbidity, treatment burden, and life expectancy.

Highest-value content. Recommendations 6.1–6.22; CGM targets (Table 6.2); individualized goals (Figure 6.1); hypoglycemia definitions/risk/prevention (Tables 6.4–6.7); intercurrent-illness medication handling; SGLT2-associated DKA risk and prevention; hyperglycemic-crisis risk factors (Table 6.8).

Main limitation. This chapter is a secondary evidence synthesis and does not reproduce the full methodology, trial denominators, or bias assessments behind every claim. The included glucagon pricing is explicitly as of 1 July 2025 and was not externally updated in this source-processing pass.

Section condensation

Assessment of glycemic status

A1C, BGM, and CGM are complementary. A1C remains the primary longitudinal marker and reflects roughly 2–3 months of glycemia, but it does not capture variability, real-time glucose, or hypoglycemia. The chapter recommends A1C and/or CGM metrics for routine assessment, at least twice yearly and usually about every 3 months when goals are unmet, therapy changes, severe/frequent hypo- or hyperglycemia occurs, health changes, or children/adolescents are growing rapidly.

A1C interpretation is vulnerable to altered red-cell turnover, kidney failure, pregnancy, transfusion, erythropoiesis-stimulating drugs, and some hemoglobin variants. Fructosamine/glycated albumin cover a shorter 2–4-week window but have a weaker clinical-trial evidence base. For CGM, 10–14 days with ≥70% wear is presented as clinically useful; standardized TIR/TAR/TBR targets make treatment changes more actionable.

Glycemic goals and complications

For many nonpregnant adults, A1C <7% and TIR >70% are the default reference goals, with strict TBR limits. The chapter repeatedly stresses individualization: lower goals can be appropriate with good health/function and low treatment burden, whereas A1C up to about 8% or non-A1C symptom-focused goals may be appropriate with complex health, frailty, cognitive/functional limitations, or limited life expectancy. Severe/frequent hypoglycemia is a strong reason to modify treatment, including deintensification of insulin, sulfonylureas, or meglitinides.

The evidence synthesis links lower glycemia to durable microvascular benefit (DCCT/EDIC, Kumamoto, UKPDS) but is more nuanced for cardiovascular outcomes. ACCORD raised a mortality safety signal with its intensive strategy; later meta-analysis and long-term follow-up suggest some reduction in myocardial infarction/MACE without uniform stroke, HF, or mortality benefit. Modern SGLT2 and GLP-1–based organ benefits are emphasized as not contingent on A1C lowering.

Hypoglycemia assessment, treatment, and prevention

The chapter defines level 1 (<70 to ≥54 mg/dL), level 2 (<54 mg/dL), and level 3 hypoglycemia (severe impairment requiring assistance). It directs clinicians to review hypoglycemia at every encounter, screen at least annually for impaired awareness and fear when appropriate, use CGM in high-risk people, and reassess/deintensify after any level 2 or 3 episode. High risk is operationalized through Table 6.5, combining recent events, intensive insulin therapy, kidney/cognitive disease, metabolic-surgery history, social instability, and other clinical factors.

Treatment is pragmatic: conscious people generally receive 15 g fast glucose-containing carbohydrate, recheck after 15 minutes, with 5–10 g often sufficient in automated-insulin-delivery users; acarbose users need pure glucose. Glucagon should be prescribed to insulin users and other high-risk people, with ready-to-use formulations preferred and close contacts trained. Prevention is multicomponent: structured education, technology, treatment-plan reevaluation, glucagon access, and awareness training.

Intercurrent illness

Illness, trauma, and surgery can destabilize glucose in both directions and can precipitate hyperglycemic crises. The chapter recommends more frequent glucose monitoring and ketone monitoring in ketosis-prone people, explicit sick-day plans, and individualized medication adjustment.

Medication-specific cautions include holding metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors when oral intake cannot be maintained or acute kidney injury is a concern; holding thiazolidinediones during HF exacerbation/hypervolemia; and reducing or stopping sulfonylureas when interacting antimicrobials substantially raise hypoglycemia risk.

Hyperglycemic crises

DKA and HHS are treated as life-threatening emergencies with prevention and outpatient recognition emphasized here while formal diagnosis/inpatient treatment is deferred to section 16. The chapter highlights SGLT2-associated DKA risk: 5–17-fold higher in type 1 diabetes users; in type 2 diabetes, 0.6–4.9 events/1,000 patient-years, with pooled RR 2.46 in randomized trials and 1.74 in observational studies. Pregnancy can produce euglycemic DKA below 200 mg/dL, and the reported DKA incidence is up to 2% with pregestational diabetes but <0.1% with gestational diabetes.

Prevention requires early symptom recognition, ketone access, sick-day guidance, continuation of basal insulin during fasting/illness for intensive-insulin users, and rapid care escalation when hydration, vomiting, mental status, glucose, or ketones worsen. One 6-month randomized trial summarized by the chapter found blood ketone monitoring reduced hospitalization/ER management by about 50% versus urine monitoring. Recurrent DKA warrants behavioral/social trigger assessment; Table 6.8 explicitly includes insulin rationing and adverse social determinants of health.

Table 6.2 — CGM metrics

Valid wear: ≥14 days and ≥70% active time.
Most adults: TIR 70–180 >70%; TAR >180 <25%; TAR >250 <5%; TBR <70 <4%; TBR <54 <1%; CV ≤36%.
Older adults with complex/intermediate health: TIR >50%; TAR >180 <50%; TAR >250 <10%; TBR <70 <1%; TBR <54 <1%.
Mean glucose/GMI: no standardized goals listed.

Table 6.3 — Glycemic goals for many nonpregnant adults

A1C: <7.0% (<53 mmol/mol).
Preprandial capillary plasma glucose: 80–130 mg/dL (4.4–7.2 mmol/L).
Peak postprandial: <180 mg/dL (<10.0 mmol/L), generally assessed 1–2 h after meal start.
Interpretation: targets require individualization.

Figure 6.1 — Individualized A1C/CGM goals and decision framework

Good health/function, low treatment risk/burden: A1C <6.5%.
Most adults: A1C <7%; TIR >70%; TBR <70 <4%; TBR <54 <1%; TAR >180 <25%; TAR >250 <5%.
Healthy older adults: A1C <7.5%.
Complex/intermediate older adults: A1C <8%; TIR >50%; TBR <70 <1%; TBR <54 <1%; TAR >180 <50%; TAR >250 <10%.
Very complex/poor health or limited life expectancy: no A1C goal; minimize hypoglycemia and avoid symptomatic hyperglycemia.
Modifying factors: diabetes duration, hypoglycemia risk, treatment burden, nonglycemic medication benefits, cardiovascular complications, comorbidity burden.
Governance: framework—not rigid rule; use shared decision-making and account for resources/support.

Table 6.4 — Hypoglycemia classification

Level 1: glucose <70 and ≥54 mg/dL.
Level 2: glucose <54 mg/dL.
Level 3: altered mental/physical status requiring assistance, irrespective of measured glucose.

Table 6.5 — Hypoglycemia risk

Applies to insulin, sulfonylurea, or meglitinide users.
High risk: ≥1 major factor or multiple other factors using clinical judgment.
Major clinical: recent level 2/3 event, intensive insulin therapy, impaired awareness, kidney failure, cognitive impairment/dementia, metabolic-surgery history.
Major social: food insecurity, low income, housing insecurity, religious/cultural fasting, underinsurance.
Other factors include age ≥75, high variability, CKD/CVD, neuropathy/retinopathy, mental illness, gastroparesis, beta-blockers, low health literacy, and substance use.

Table 6.7 — Hypoglycemia-prevention components

At initial visit: history, awareness, cognitive/risk assessment, education, technology needs, treatment-plan review, glucagon access/training, and awareness training when indicated.
Every follow-up: history, technology needs, education when indicated, treatment-plan reevaluation when indicated.
Annually: repeat core assessments and indicated education/interventions.
Trigger notes: recurrent events/high-risk meds for education; level 2/3 events, illness, or interacting drugs for plan reevaluation; impaired awareness for awareness training.

Table 6.8 — Hyperglycemic-crisis risk factors

Risk factors include absolute insulin deficiency/type 1 diabetes, younger age, prior hyperglycemic or hypoglycemic crises, diabetes complications, chronic illness, behavioral-health conditions, alcohol/substance use, high A1C, insulin rationing, SGLT2/SGLT1/2 inhibitors, and adverse social determinants of health.

Evaluation and appraisal

Bottom line: Use as a current practice reference and implementation source, but read cited P0 primary studies before reusing trial-level results as primary evidence. Confidence in appraisal: high for chapter content and operational recommendations; moderate for underlying study-level certainty without primary-source review.

Relevance — 10/10

Rationale: Directly governs glycemic assessment, targets, hypoglycemia prevention/treatment, sick-day medication handling, and outpatient crisis prevention.

Evidence basis: The chapter provides explicit practice recommendations and operational thresholds.

Principal limiting factor: Some populations (pregnancy, pediatrics, older adults, inpatient care) are partly delegated to other Standards chapters.

What would change the score: Full linked-chapter reconciliation would raise contextual completeness, not the chapter’s direct relevance.

Novelty — 7/10

Rationale: The value is primarily an updated synthesis and current recommendation set rather than a novel study.

Evidence basis: Annual Standards update integrates modern CGM targets, deintensification, ready-to-use glucagon, and current DKA-risk framing.

Principal limiting factor: Many core glycemic and hypoglycemia principles derive from established evidence.

What would change the score: A line-by-line comparison with the 2025 chapter would determine exact novelty.

Method strength — 7/10

Rationale: Recommendations use the ADA evidence-grade framework and synthesize major randomized, observational, meta-analytic, and consensus evidence.

Evidence basis: Recommendation grades A/B/C/E are provided throughout.

Principal limiting factor: The full search, evidence-selection, conflict-management, and grading methodology is in the separate Introduction and Methodology and was not supplied.

What would change the score: Reviewing the governing methodology chapter and evidence tables would strengthen method appraisal.

Evidence strength — 8/10

Rationale: Several central recommendations rest on landmark RCTs, long-term follow-up, meta-analyses, and clinical trials, while other operational recommendations use lower-grade or expert evidence.

Evidence basis: The chapter explicitly grades recommendations and preserves major effect magnitudes such as DCCT/EDIC benefits and SGLT2-associated DKA risk.

Principal limiting factor: Secondary summaries often omit full denominators, detailed bias assessments, and trial-level uncertainty.

What would change the score: Direct reading of P0 primary sources would increase claim-level certainty.

External validity — 9/10

Rationale: The recommendations target broad diabetes populations and explicitly individualize goals by age, function, comorbidity, hypoglycemia risk, and treatment burden.

Evidence basis: Figure 6.1 and Tables 6.2–6.5 define multiple common clinical phenotypes and risk strata.

Principal limiting factor: Special populations are partly handled in other Standards sections, and some evidence derives from older landmark treatment eras.

What would change the score: Cross-chapter reconciliation and direct study appraisal would refine subgroup applicability.

Implementation value — 10/10

Rationale: The chapter is highly operational: it supplies thresholds, visit frequencies, risk tables, treatment rules, glucagon options, medication-holding advice, and DKA prevention triggers.

Evidence basis: Recommendations 6.1–6.22, Tables 6.1–6.8, and Figure 6.1 can be translated directly into protocols and CDS logic.

Principal limiting factor: Local formulary, device access, pricing, and policy constraints still require local adaptation.

What would change the score: Local policy mapping and current formulary/cost verification would improve deployment readiness.

Strengths

Explicit recommendation grades; broad clinical applicability; actionable thresholds and visit frequencies; strong linkage between monitoring, hypoglycemia risk, treatment burden, and deintensification; unusually useful risk/prevention tables; preservation of landmark effect magnitudes and modern therapy context.

Limitations and uncertainty

Guideline methodology is external; many cited studies are summarized without full denominators or bias information; some landmark evidence reflects older treatment eras; special-population detail is delegated to other chapters; product prices are point-in-time U.S. values; evidence grades range from A to expert-opinion E.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Monitoring

Build workflows that accept A1C plus CGM metrics; alert on TBR/TAR thresholds and insufficient CGM wear; flag A1C-discordance conditions.

Goals

Use default A1C/TIR targets only after individualized health/function, hypoglycemia risk, treatment burden, and life-expectancy assessment. Treat Figure 6.1 as decision support rather than a rigid rule.

Hypoglycemia

At every encounter, capture event severity/frequency, awareness, and treatment. Any level 2/3 event should trigger plan reevaluation; high-risk people should have CGM, glucagon access, education, and appropriate deintensification.

Illness / DKA

Provide explicit sick-day instructions, ketone access, basal-insulin continuation guidance, drug-holding rules, and clear escalation criteria. Screen recurrence for behavioral and social barriers, including insulin rationing.

Do not overclaim: The chapter’s reports of DCCT/EDIC, ACCORD, SGLT2 meta-analyses, and other studies are secondary evidence summaries. Use the linked reference queue to acquire and atomize primary publications before treating those results as primary-study evidence.
