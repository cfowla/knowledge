Design

Retrospective multicenter cohort study using an integrated clinical data warehouse across 8 Catholic University of Korea hospitals.

Population

Adults with heart failure receiving dapagliflozin or empagliflozin and with transthoracic echocardiography. Matched cohort: 4,930.

Scope warning

This appraisal evaluates comparative outcomes reported in this cohort. It does not establish randomized equivalence, therapeutic interchangeability, formulary cost equivalence, or current regulatory-indication parity.

One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first for an empagliflozin-vs-dapagliflozin formulary evidence review. The study is directly relevant and methodologically useful, but observational.

The matched cohort contained 2,465 patients per treatment group with a median 16-month follow-up. The primary composite of cardiovascular death or heart-failure hospitalization occurred in 9.8% with dapagliflozin and 9.3% with empagliflozin (adjusted HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.83-1.19; P=.95). Secondary cardiovascular outcomes were also statistically nonsignificant. The same overall pattern held across HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF; interaction P=.32.

Two sensitivity/extension analyses reinforce the main direction without converting it into equivalence evidence: HFimpEF showed adjusted HR 1.01 (0.68-1.50), and the IPTW primary sensitivity analysis showed HR 0.93 (0.79-1.10). Exploratory safety event frequencies were mostly similar, while diabetic ketoacidosis was reported in 9 vs 2 patients (0.4% vs 0.2%; P=.04). That sparse, exploratory signal is hypothesis-generating rather than a robust comparative safety estimate.

Interpretive boundary. “No statistically significant difference” is not the same as demonstrated equivalence. The study did not use a randomized design or a prespecified equivalence/noninferiority margin.

Methods condensation

Patients diagnosed with heart failure from January 2021 through November 2023 were identified across 8 tertiary hospitals sharing a standardized EMR/CDW. Adults had to have a dapagliflozin or empagliflozin prescription and transthoracic echocardiography. The first prescription was the index date, and at least one prescription counted as treatment. HFrEF was LVEF ≤40%, HFmrEF 41%-49%, and HFpEF ≥50%.

One-to-one propensity-score matching used baseline variables with greedy matching and a 0.1-SD caliper; SMD <0.1 defined balance. Cox proportional-hazards models, Kaplan-Meier analysis, and time-dependent treatment handling were used. A full-cohort IPTW sensitivity analysis, subgroup analyses, and an exploratory HFimpEF analysis supplemented the primary matched comparison. Missing baseline data were reported as under 4% before matching and absent in the matched cohort.

Exploratory safety

Any adverse event was 5.8% in both groups. UTI, fracture, amputation, and hypoglycemia showed no statistically significant between-group difference. Diabetic ketoacidosis was reported in 9 dapagliflozin patients (0.4%) vs 2 empagliflozin patients (0.2%), P=.04. Because this was exploratory, sparse, and not accompanied by an adjusted comparative effect estimate, it should not be interpreted as definitive comparative safety evidence.

Sensitivity and exploratory analyses

HFimpEF: primary outcome 53/408 vs 47/369, adjusted HR 1.01 (0.68-1.50; P=.96). IPTW: primary outcome 315/3003 vs 237/2515, HR 0.93 (0.79-1.10; P=.420), with secondary outcomes also statistically nonsignificant. Follow-up creatinine, NT-proBNP, LVEF, and body-weight changes showed no significant between-drug differences in matched patients.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance9/10

Rationale: Directly informs empagliflozin-vs-dapagliflozin heart-failure comparative effectiveness and formulary reasoning.

Evidence: Multicenter head-to-head routine-care cohort with all EF phenotypes.

Limit: Not a randomized interchangeability trial.

Would change: A randomized head-to-head effectiveness trial would raise confidence.

Novelty7/10

Rationale: Adds a direct multicenter comparison across the full LVEF spectrum and HFimpEF.

Evidence: Few prior direct within-class HF comparisons existed.

Limit: Observational design and overlapping questions with prior cohort/meta-analytic work.

Would change: Prospective randomized comparison or mechanistic explanation would increase novelty.

Method strength7/10

Rationale: PSM, time-to-event modeling, LVEF stratification, and IPTW sensitivity are appropriate safeguards for a retrospective cohort.

Evidence: 8-center standardized EMR/CDW, 1:1 PSM, balance checks, Cox models, IPTW sensitivity.

Limit: Residual confounding, sparse adherence/exposure detail, and some unmeasured comorbidities remain.

Would change: Active-comparator new-user design with adherence and richer confounder capture would improve method strength.

Evidence strength6/10

Rationale: The overall null comparative result is internally consistent across matched, subgroup, and IPTW analyses, but causal certainty is limited.

Evidence: Primary AHR 0.99 (95% CI 0.83-1.19); sensitivity HR 0.93 (0.79-1.10).

Limit: No randomized assignment or equivalence/noninferiority margin; subgroup CIs are wide.

Would change: Randomized head-to-head evidence with prespecified equivalence margins would raise this score.

External validity6/10

Rationale: Routine-care multicenter sampling supports practice relevance, but the network and population are geographically concentrated.

Evidence: 8 tertiary hospitals in South Korea, broad HF EF spectrum.

Limit: Primarily Korean participants and one integrated health-system network.

Would change: Replication in other countries, health systems, ethnic groups, and non-tertiary settings would improve generalizability.

Implementation value8/10

Rationale: Useful evidence against assuming a large HF-outcome advantage of one agent over the other in similar patients.

Evidence: No significant difference in primary or secondary outcomes, consistent across EF strata and IPTW sensitivity.

Limit: Does not establish therapeutic equivalence, interchangeability, inpatient substitution policy, cost parity, or indication parity.

Would change: Concordant randomized and broader real-world evidence plus current label/cost analysis would increase implementation value.

Strengths

  • Direct within-class comparator question.
  • Large matched sample and multiple participating hospitals.
  • Explicit propensity-score balance checks and time-to-event methods.
  • Consistent primary direction across LVEF strata, HFimpEF, and IPTW sensitivity analysis.
  • Both effectiveness and exploratory safety outcomes reported.

Limitations

  • Retrospective observational design with residual confounding.
  • Exposure counted after at least one prescription and adherence was unavailable.
  • Some clinically important confounders were unavailable or incompletely captured.
  • Primarily Korean tertiary-care population limits transportability.
  • No equivalence/noninferiority margin; null significance testing cannot prove interchangeability.
  • Exploratory safety counts are sparse and vulnerable to multiplicity and coding limitations.
Source-integrity finding: the reported CV-death adjusted HR 0.94 (95% CI 0.67-1.32) is paired with P=.94 in the article. That P value is not numerically consonant with the HR/CI combination under the usual Wald relationship. This appraisal preserves the source value and does not silently repair it.

Implementation / practice translation

Use now

Use as supportive real-world evidence that a large heart-failure effectiveness difference between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin was not observed in this Korean multicenter cohort.

Do not overclaim

Do not cite this study alone as proof of therapeutic equivalence or an unrestricted hospital formulary interchange.

Formulary relevance

Pair this study with randomized placebo-controlled outcome trials, direct comparative observational evidence, current indications/labels, safety, renal criteria, operational switching issues, and acquisition cost.

Read next

Prior direct comparator cohort studies and network meta-analyses in references 11-16 and 19 are the highest-yield follow-up sources.
