One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In patients receiving intravenous thrombolytic therapy for acute MI and subsequent heparin, recurrent ischemic events clustered at lower PTT values and sometimes followed heparin discontinuation; bleeding was observed mainly in the first 24 hours after thrombolysis.

The investigators prospectively followed 75 consecutive thrombolysis-treated AMI patients; 72 formed the analyzed cohort. Heparin was started after thrombolysis and titrated to a PTT target of 90-120 seconds. Definite recurrent ischemia required chest pain plus reversible ECG changes, while a probable category used clinical chest-pain features without the qualifying ECG changes. Seventeen definite episodes occurred in 13 patients; combining definite and probable events yielded 52 episodes in 28 patients. Across all patients, definite ischemia was reported as six times more likely to be associated with PTT <50 seconds than PTT >100 seconds. Eight patients developed definite or probable ischemia within 24 hours after heparin discontinuation, on average 10.1 ± 1.8 hours after stopping the infusion.

The signal is clinically interesting but methodologically fragile. PTT intensity was not randomized, PTT categories were arbitrary, repeated measurements/events occurred within individuals, and no multivariable or clustered analysis is described. The combined definite/probable association was not statistically significant in the full 72-patient cohort. Nine hemorrhagic complications occurred, all within 24 hours after thrombolysis; 4 were considered major and 2 required transfusion. The authors attributed the timing of bleeding more to thrombolytic-associated hemostatic disturbance than to heparin, but that attribution is not causally testable from this uncontrolled series.

Best value of the paper: historical mechanistic evidence linking low anticoagulation intensity and heparin interruption to recurrent ischemia after thrombolysis, with unusually detailed PTT/event timing for a small early cohort.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract / question

The paper asks whether the level of heparin anticoagulation after intravenous thrombolysis for AMI is associated with recurrent ischemia and bleeding. The abstract reports 17 definite ischemic episodes in 13 patients, 52 definite/probable events in 28 patients, 8 patients with ischemia within 24 hours after heparin discontinuation, and 9 hemorrhagic complications.

The authors conclude that lower anticoagulation levels are associated with more recurrent ischemia, heparin discontinuation is frequently followed by ischemia, and the observed hemorrhagic complication rate with heparin was low.

Introduction

Reocclusion, reinfarction, and recurrent ischemia were recognized problems after successful thrombolytic reperfusion. Existing reports suggested anticoagulation could help maintain arterial patency, but the effect on recurrent clinical complications was uncertain.

The stated prospective objective was to relate recurrent ischemic events to the level of anticoagulation in 75 consecutive patients receiving intravenous streptokinase or urokinase for AMI.

Methods

Patients presenting within 4 hours of AMI onset were considered for thrombolysis. Major bleeding-risk or recent neurologic/surgical events and vigorous CPR excluded thrombolysis. AMI diagnosis required at least 30 minutes of typical chest pain plus at least 2 mm ST elevation in at least 2 leads. Seventy-one patients received streptokinase and 4 urokinase. A 3,000-unit IV heparin bolus preceded thrombolysis; the thrombolytic was given as two 750,000-unit doses 10-15 minutes apart, and continuous heparin 1,000 units/hour began immediately after thrombolytic completion.

PTT was measured every 6 hours for 48 hours, then every 12 hours until stable, then at least daily, with extra sampling during chest pain; heparin was titrated to PTT 90-120 seconds. Definite ischemia required chest pain plus reversible ECG change; probable ischemia required typical angina with a >15 mm Hg blood-pressure change or nitroglycerin relief. Events were observed from 24 hours after thrombolysis until death, revascularization, or 24 hours after heparin cessation. Statistical methods included 2-sample t tests, chi-square testing, and Mann-Whitney analysis of four arbitrarily chosen PTT ranges; p<0.05 defined significance.

Results

Three patients were excluded, leaving 72. Seventeen definite ischemic episodes occurred in 13 patients from 2-16 days after AMI (median 5). With probable ischemia included, 52 events occurred in 28 patients from 2-16 days (median 4). Eight patients had ischemia within 24 hours after stopping heparin; heparin had been stopped on average 7.8 ± 1.7 days after AMI and ischemia followed an average 10.1 ± 1.8 hours later. Definite ischemia across all patients was sixfold more likely to be associated with PTT <50 seconds than PTT >100 seconds; the combined definite/probable trend was not significant in the full cohort. Among the 28 patients with ischemia, the lower-PTT association was significant for both definite and combined events, with nearly a fourfold contrast between <50 and >100 seconds.

Patients with recurrent ischemia received thrombolysis earlier (146.3 ± 9.4 vs 174.0 ± 7.1 minutes, p=0.02) and reached peak CK earlier (13.1 ± 0.8 vs 15.6 ± 0.7 hours, p=0.02). Nine patients had hemorrhagic complications, all within 24 hours of thrombolysis. Four had major bleeding requiring heparin discontinuation; 2 gastrointestinal bleeds required transfusion, 2 venipuncture-site hematomas did not. Five minor events occurred without heparin alteration.

Discussion

The authors interpret recurrent ischemia after heparin reduction or interruption as a potential sign of reperfusion/myocardial salvage and argue that heparin discontinuation requires careful timing. They note that PTT >100 seconds was associated with the fewest recurrent ischemic events, while acknowledging that therapeutic anticoagulation did not prevent all ischemia.

They attribute early bleeding primarily to the thrombolytic-related hemostatic disturbance because no new bleeding developed later, and they view anticoagulation intensity as only one contributor to recurrent ischemia. They also speculate that adding antiplatelet therapy or mechanically reducing residual stenosis could improve prevention, but these strategies were not tested in this study.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Skim deeply. High historical relevance to heparin pharmacotherapy, but limited causal and practice-changing strength. Preserve the methods, Figure 1, heparin-discontinuation signal, and bleeding timing; do not treat the PTT target or safety attribution as validated contemporary practice.

Clinical question / PICO-style framing

Population: Consecutive patients with acute MI treated with IV streptokinase or urokinase; 72 analyzed.
Exposure/intervention: Post-thrombolysis IV heparin, titrated to PTT 90-120 seconds; observed PTT intensity and episodes of heparin discontinuation.
Comparator: Lower versus higher observed PTT categories; patients with versus without recurrent ischemia. No randomized heparin/no-heparin comparator.
Outcomes: Definite and probable recurrent myocardial ischemia; recurrent AMI signals; hemorrhagic complications.

Validity

Strengths
  • Prospective observation of consecutive patients.
  • Explicit heparin dosing and PTT monitoring schedule.
  • Prospectively defined definite/probable ischemia categories.
  • Event-time PTT sampling and clear heparin-cessation timing.
  • All main tables and figures are internally consistent with the main narrative.
Major limitations
  • No randomized heparin comparator or randomized PTT target.
  • Small single-center cohort (72 analyzed).
  • PTT categories were arbitrarily selected.
  • Repeated PTT values and recurrent events within patients are not described as cluster-adjusted.
  • Probable ischemia is less specific than the ECG-defined endpoint.
  • Figure 2 conditions on already having ischemia.
  • Bleeding attribution to thrombolysis versus heparin is based on timing, not controlled comparison.

Ratings

9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses heparin anticoagulation intensity and interruption after thrombolysis for AMI, making it highly relevant to a heparin evidence cluster.

Evidence basis: Prospective observation of PTT, recurrent ischemia, heparin cessation, and bleeding in the same cohort.

Principal limiting factor: The clinical setting is historical and AMI-specific.

What would change the score: Would rise with direct applicability to current anticoagulation decisions across contemporary reperfusion settings; would fall if the review question excludes historical thrombolysis.

6/10

Novelty

Rationale: The paper links serial PTT intensity and heparin discontinuation to recurrent ischemia in a prospective series, an informative mechanistic/historical contribution.

Evidence basis: Serial PTT sampling, event-time sampling, and explicit comparison of PTT strata.

Principal limiting factor: It is an association study without a randomized anticoagulation strategy.

What would change the score: Would rise with randomized PTT targets or a prespecified causal comparison; would fall if equivalent prior evidence already established the same relationship.

4/10

Method strength

Rationale: Prospective consecutive enrollment and prespecified event definitions are strengths, but causal inference is limited by the uncontrolled design and repeated observations within patients.

Evidence basis: Consecutive cohort, prospective ischemia classification, defined PTT target, statistical testing, two tables and two PTT-event figures.

Principal limiting factor: No randomized heparin comparator or target assignment; arbitrary PTT ranges; no described adjustment for within-patient correlation or multivariable confounding.

What would change the score: Would rise with randomized anticoagulation intensity, patient-level clustered analysis, prespecified exposure cutoffs, and adjusted modeling.

4/10

Evidence strength

Rationale: The study demonstrates a consistent association between lower PTT and definite recurrent ischemia, but the association cannot establish that higher-intensity heparin prevents events.

Evidence basis: Definite ischemia was reported as sixfold more frequently associated with PTT <50 s than >100 s; heparin cessation preceded 8 recurrent ischemic events within 24 h.

Principal limiting factor: Combined definite/probable events were not significant in the full cohort; bleeding attribution and cessation effects are observational.

What would change the score: Would rise with replicated prospective controlled evidence showing reduced clinical ischemic outcomes without unacceptable bleeding.

2/10

External validity

Rationale: Generalizability is narrow because the cohort was small, single-center, and treated in a 1987 streptokinase/urokinase era.

Evidence basis: 72 analyzed patients from Northwestern Memorial Hospital; thrombolytic regimens and PTT monitoring are explicitly described.

Principal limiting factor: Single center, small sample, assay-specific PTT target, historical reperfusion context.

What would change the score: Would rise with multicenter validation using contemporary reperfusion and anticoagulation practice.

3/10

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper is useful for historical rationale and for recognizing ischemic events around heparin interruption, but it is insufficient to set a modern heparin target or interruption policy by itself.

Evidence basis: Detailed heparin dosing, PTT monitoring, event definitions, cessation timing, and bleeding counts.

Principal limiting factor: No controlled comparison of alternative heparin targets or interruption strategies and no contemporary validation.

What would change the score: Would rise with current-practice trials or guidelines that reproduce the signal and specify actionable targets and hold/restart rules.

Key overclaim to avoid: The paper does not show that targeting PTT >100 seconds causes fewer ischemic events, nor that stopping heparin causes recurrent ischemia. It shows associations in an observational cohort.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Useful now as evidence history

Use the paper to document an early clinical observation that recurrent ischemia after thrombolysis was associated with lower PTT measurements and sometimes occurred soon after heparin interruption.

Do not operationalize directly

Do not derive a modern therapeutic PTT range, mandatory heparin duration, or hold/restart rule from this paper alone. The study did not randomize anticoagulation intensity or interruption.

Safety interpretation

The observed major-bleeding burden was low in this small series (4 patients; 2 transfused), but attribution between thrombolytic therapy and heparin is unresolved by the design.

Best extraction targets for synthesis

Preserve the 6-fold low-PTT association for definite ischemia, the 8 events within 24 hours of heparin cessation, the 10.1-hour mean delay after cessation, and the early timing of all 9 hemorrhagic complications.

Bottom line: This is a useful historical association study for a heparin deep dive, especially for understanding why post-thrombolysis anticoagulation intensity and interruption attracted attention. Its small uncontrolled design is not adequate to establish causal efficacy, bleeding safety, or a current therapeutic target.
