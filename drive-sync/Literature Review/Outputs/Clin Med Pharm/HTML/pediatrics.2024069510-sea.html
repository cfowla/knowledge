One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In a large integrated-health-system cohort of healthy term infants, nirsevimab receipt was associated with substantially lower incidence of PCR-confirmed RSV LRTD, hospitalized RSV LRTD, and all PCR-confirmed RSV, plus lower health-care utilization among breakthrough cases.

The cohort included 31,900 healthy term infants, 15,647 (49.1%) of whom received nirsevimab. The primary adjusted effectiveness estimate against first PCR-confirmed RSV LRTD was 87.2% (95% CI 81.7–91.1; adjusted HR 0.128), with incidence rates of 6.10 versus 58.51 per 1,000 person-years. Effectiveness against any PCR-confirmed RSV was 71.0% (95% CI 65.3–75.8), and a post hoc estimate against hospitalized RSV LRTD was 98.0% (95% CI 85.1–99.7).

Among infants who developed RSV LRTD, nirsevimab recipients had fewer encounters (adjusted mean difference −0.86; 95% CI −1.36 to −0.36; P=.001), lower adjusted odds of hospitalization (OR 0.110; 95% CI 0.014–0.853), and lower adjusted odds of an ED visit (OR 0.297; 95% CI 0.132–0.667). Sensitivity analyses using 2- and 7-day protection lags were consistent, while the influenza negative-control analysis was null. Key limits are residual confounding, possible differential testing, exclusion of preterm/high-risk and maternally vaccinated infants, and single-system generalizability.

Internal source inconsistency: Main-text Results state that post hoc RSV episodes had 0.12 fewer mean encounters with P=.001. Supplemental Table S7 identifies −0.12 (95% CI −0.34 to 0.10) as the unadjusted difference, whereas the adjusted difference paired with P=.001 is −0.37 (95% CI −0.60 to −0.15). The discrepancy is preserved rather than silently corrected.

Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames RSV LRTD as a major infant burden and evaluates nirsevimab under real-world conditions after the 2023 ACIP recommendation. It identifies a healthy-term, nirsevimab-eligible cohort, excludes maternally RSV-vaccinated and high-risk infants, and uses adjusted Cox models to estimate effectiveness.

The headline findings are large: 87.2% effectiveness against RSV LRTD, 98.0% against hospitalized RSV LRTD, and 71.0% against any PCR-confirmed RSV, with fewer encounters and lower hospitalization odds among treated breakthrough cases.

Introduction

The authors emphasize that RSV burden extends beyond hospitalization: most medically attended RSV disease occurs in outpatient settings, including among otherwise healthy infants. Nirsevimab is positioned as a long-acting prophylactic antibody that removes the monthly-dosing limitation of palivizumab.

Prior randomized evidence established efficacy against medically attended RSV LRTI and hospitalization, but the paper argues that outpatient real-world effectiveness and utilization effects remained less well described. That gap motivates the KPNC cohort.

Methods

KPNC's integrated EHR enabled cohort construction, immunization ascertainment, PCR outcomes, diagnoses, and utilization capture. Healthy term infants were defined as ≥37 weeks without prespecified high-risk diagnoses. Maternal RSV vaccination, prior RSV positivity, preterm birth, and high-risk conditions were exclusions. Routine nirsevimab began Oct 19, 2023.

Exposure was time-varying. The primary RSV LRTD definition required a positive RSV PCR plus an LRTD diagnosis within a −7/+10-day window. Cox models used calendar time, birth-month stratification, and adjustment for sex and race/ethnicity; utilization analyses used adjusted linear/logistic regression. Missing data were not imputed. Sensitivity analyses varied the protection lag and age censoring.

Results

Of 49,680 births, exclusions yielded 31,900 healthy term infants: 15,647 nirsevimab recipients and 16,253 nonrecipients. Among 5,056 infants with at least one PCR test, 1,114 were RSV positive. Primary RSV LRTD occurred in 35 treated versus 462 untreated infants; adjusted effectiveness was 87.2%. Any PCR-confirmed RSV occurred in 158 versus 956 infants; effectiveness was 71.0%.

Hospitalized RSV LRTD effectiveness was 98.0% in post hoc analysis. Breakthrough RSV LRTD cases in the treated group generated 75 total encounters versus 1,241 in the untreated group. Adjusted encounter difference was −0.86 per episode; hospitalization and ED adjusted ORs were 0.110 and 0.297. Two- and seven-day lag analyses remained near 87.5%–87.7%; influenza negative control was null.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the study as confirming strong real-world nirsevimab performance not only against severe disease but also against milder PCR-confirmed RSV and downstream utilization. They highlight broad nirsevimab uptake, cohort-based follow-up rather than tested-only sampling, calendar-time/birth-month control, and the influenza negative control.

Important constraints remain: few cases immediately after dosing prevented onset-of-protection estimation; the proportion of symptomatic infants tested was unknown; residual confounding from socioeconomic status, siblings, and daycare may persist; high-risk and preterm infants were excluded; and the single integrated system limits external validity. The main-text/Supplement S7 utilization discrepancy is an additional reporting-quality concern not highlighted by the authors.

Conclusion

The source supports nirsevimab as an effective RSV-prevention strategy for the specific population studied: healthy term infants entering a first RSV season who were not maternally RSV vaccinated. The strongest reusable findings are the magnitude and precision of the primary RSV LRTD estimate, the hospitalization result, and the demonstration of lower utilization among breakthrough infections.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: real-world evidence for nirsevimab effectiveness and healthcare-utilization impact in healthy term infants. Do not use as comparative evidence versus maternal RSV vaccination or clesrovimab, or as direct evidence for preterm/high-risk infants.

Relevance 9/10

Rationale: Directly informs infant RSV prevention and real-world implementation.

Evidence basis: Large healthy-term cohort, clinically meaningful RSV outcomes, utilization outcomes.

Limiting factor: Population is narrower than the full current prevention landscape.

Would change with: Would rise with comparative maternal-vaccine/antibody analyses or high-risk subgroup data.

Novelty 8/10

Rationale: Extends randomized/hospitalization evidence into outpatient disease and utilization in a broad treated cohort.

Evidence basis: Primary RSV LRTD plus any-RSV and encounter analyses.

Limiting factor: Other 2023–2024 observational effectiveness studies already existed.

Would change with: Would rise with comparative effectiveness or multi-season persistence data.

Method strength 8/10

Rationale: Strong observational design choices address calendar-time confounding and use a negative control.

Evidence basis: Time-varying exposure, calendar-time Cox model, birth-month stratification, adjusted models, sensitivity analyses.

Limiting factor: Nonrandomized exposure, possible testing bias, residual confounding.

Would change with: Would rise with target-trial emulation, richer confounder capture, or randomized pragmatic evidence.

Evidence strength 8/10

Rationale: Large effects are precise and robust across sensitivity analyses.

Evidence basis: 87.2% primary effectiveness with narrow CI; 98% hospitalized estimate; consistent lags; null influenza control.

Limiting factor: Main-text/S7 reporting mismatch and post hoc severity/utilization analyses.

Would change with: Would rise with replicated multi-system data and correction/clarification of S7 wording.

External validity 7/10

Rationale: Integrated-system population is large and diverse but intentionally excludes several clinically important groups.

Evidence basis: 31,900 infants across 21 hospitals/259 clinics; high uptake.

Limiting factor: Single Northern California system; healthy term only; maternal-vaccinated infants excluded.

Would change with: Would rise with multi-region systems and inclusion of high-risk/preterm/maternal-vaccine pathways.

Implementation value 8/10

Rationale: Shows both prevention and downstream utilization effects in routine care.

Evidence basis: Administration setting data, uptake curve, outpatient/ED/hospital encounter breakdowns.

Limiting factor: No cost-effectiveness analysis; no head-to-head prevention-strategy comparison.

Would change with: Would rise with operational cost, access, comparative product, and maternal-vaccine pathway analyses.

Strengths

  • Large cohort with substantial treatment uptake and clinically interpretable event counts.
  • Calendar-time and birth-month handling is well aligned to rapidly changing RSV incidence and product uptake.
  • Negative-control influenza analysis is a useful residual-confounding check.
  • Supplement provides reproducible diagnostic/exclusion code sets and utilization detail.

Principal limitations

  • Observational design: residual confounding remains possible.
  • Testing behavior was incompletely characterized.
  • Post hoc conditional-on-breakthrough severity/utilization analyses are more fragile than the primary cohort estimate.
  • Healthy-term, non-maternally-vaccinated cohort limits generalizability.
  • Internal reporting mismatch between main-text post hoc encounter magnitude and Supplemental Table S7.
  • Industry funding and several Sanofi-employed authors require attention, although methods and quantitative reporting are relatively transparent.

Implementation / practice translation

What this supports

Use the study as strong observational support that nirsevimab can markedly reduce RSV LRTD in healthy term infants and can reduce both outpatient and acute-care utilization during breakthrough disease.

What it does not answer

It does not establish comparative effectiveness among nirsevimab, maternal RSV vaccination, and clesrovimab; it also does not directly estimate benefit in preterm/high-risk infants excluded from this cohort.

Monitoring implications

For local implementation, track seasonal uptake, timing of administration, PCR testing behavior, RSV LRTD/hospitalization rates, and access by demographic/clinical subgroup.

External current-practice verification (separate from source findings): CDC guidance updated in 2025 describes prevention of severe infant RSV disease through either maternal RSV vaccination or a long-acting infant RSV antibody; infant antibody options include nirsevimab and clesrovimab for eligible first-season infants, while nirsevimab remains an option for certain high-risk children entering a second season. This 2025 Pediatrics study predates that expanded product landscape and excluded infants whose mothers received RSV vaccine, so it should not be treated as comparative-strategy evidence.

Bottom line: High-value real-world effectiveness paper. The primary RSV LRTD estimate is compelling for the studied population; post hoc severity/utilization findings are supportive but should be interpreted more cautiously, especially given sparse treated hospitalizations and the S7 text/table discrepancy.
