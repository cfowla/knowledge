Scope warning
This is indirect comparative evidence. It does not establish active-agent equivalence or superiority.

One-page synthesis

Verdict: read soon for formulary context, but do not use as the stand-alone source for choosing one SGLT2 inhibitor over another.

The review linked DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced, SOLOIST-WHF, EMPEROR-Preserved, and DELIVER through placebo and compared dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and sotagliflozin indirectly. Across cardiovascular death or heart-failure hospitalization, all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, and heart-failure hospitalization, every active-agent comparison had a 95% confidence interval crossing 1. The strongest source-supported statement is therefore that this network did not detect a difference among the active agents.

The ranking plots are more tempting than the estimates. Sotagliflozin ranked best for the composite and heart-failure hospitalization, while dapagliflozin ranked best for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. The paper itself says ranking was used when comparisons were nonsignificant. Those rankings should not be converted into superiority claims. For a dapagliflozin-only formulary decision, this paper helps support the absence of a demonstrated efficacy gap, but it cannot prove therapeutic equivalence with empagliflozin.

Source integrity issue. The four endpoint sections repeatedly state n=21,927. The randomized arm denominators in Supplementary Table S3 sum to 21,947. The 20-patient discrepancy is preserved rather than corrected.

Methods and development process

The authors searched PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane CENTRAL from inception through 20 August 2022, updated the search on 4 September 2022, and also searched Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov, and reference lists. Eligible studies were English-language RCTs in established heart failure with SGLT2 inhibitor and placebo arms. Two reviewers screened studies independently. A different two-reviewer pair extracted aggregate data. The review excluded nonrandomized studies, observational or registry studies, and post hoc heart-failure strata from trials not designed around heart failure.

The Bayesian network meta-analysis used odds ratios with 95% intervals, a random-effects model, four Markov chains, 30,000 burn-in iterations, and 100,000 additional iterations in GeMTC 1.0-1 with R 4.1.3. The authors assessed heterogeneity with Q and I-squared. They could not assess network consistency with an inconsistency factor because there were no direct active-agent comparisons. Sensitivity analyses separated HFrEF from HFmrEF/HFpEF.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 9/10

Directly addresses comparative SGLT2 inhibitor efficacy in established heart failure.

Evidence: Five dedicated heart-failure RCTs and clinically important outcomes.
Limit: Comparative estimates are indirect.
Would change: Direct head-to-head evidence would raise the score.
Novelty: 6/10

The paper applies Bayesian network comparison to a small dedicated-HF evidence set.

Evidence: It compares dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and sotagliflozin within one network.
Limit: The same five major trials were already individually available.
Would change: More direct comparative evidence or patient-level modeling would raise the score.
Method strength: 5/10

The search, dual review, random-effects Bayesian model, and sensitivity analysis are clear.

Evidence: PRISMA-based workflow, Cochrane bias assessment, four-chain MCMC, subgroup sensitivity analyses.
Limit: Only five trials, clinical heterogeneity, aggregate data, and no active-agent direct comparisons.
Would change: Direct comparisons and stronger consistency/transitivity diagnostics would raise the score.
Evidence strength: 4/10

The analysis supports no detected active-agent difference, but does not establish equivalence or superiority.

Evidence: All active-active 95% CIs include the null across the four major outcomes.
Limit: Wide intervals, heterogeneity for the composite and CV death, and rank probabilities after nonsignificant comparisons.
Would change: Narrower direct estimates or prespecified noninferiority evidence would raise the score.
External validity: 5/10

The included RCTs cover HFrEF, HFmrEF/HFpEF, and recent worsening HF.

Evidence: Large dedicated-HF trials span multiple phenotypes and regions.
Limit: Trials differ in phenotype, follow-up, diabetes requirements, and other baseline features.
Would change: Patient-level harmonization across phenotypes would raise the score.
Implementation value: 4/10

Useful as supporting class-effect context, not as a stand-alone formulary choice between dapagliflozin and empagliflozin.

Evidence: No significant active-agent differences were detected.
Limit: The study was not designed to prove therapeutic equivalence and ranks are unstable.
Would change: Direct comparative effectiveness or head-to-head RCT evidence would raise the score.

Main strengths

  • Only dedicated heart-failure RCTs entered the network.
  • The search, screening, extraction, risk-of-bias method, Bayesian model, and sensitivity analysis are described.
  • The included trials cover reduced, mildly reduced, preserved, and recently worsening heart failure.

Main limitations

  • No active-agent head-to-head comparison exists in the network.
  • Only five trials were included, and clinical heterogeneity is substantial.
  • The analysis uses aggregate trial-level data.
  • Composite and cardiovascular-death heterogeneity were I-squared 71% and 63%.
  • The source has a 20-patient denominator discrepancy between endpoint prose and Supplementary Table S3.
  • The Methods say publication bias was not assessed, while the Results and supplement still present funnel plots and comment on mild asymmetry.

Implementation and practice takeaways

What this paper can support. In this indirect network, no statistically significant efficacy difference was detected among dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and sotagliflozin for the four major outcomes studied.

What it cannot support. It cannot prove that dapagliflozin and empagliflozin are equivalent, interchangeable for every patient, or ranked correctly by the posterior rank plots. A formulary argument should use this paper as one supporting layer beside direct trial evidence, guideline class recommendations, safety, renal indications, operational constraints, and any stronger comparative-effectiveness data.
