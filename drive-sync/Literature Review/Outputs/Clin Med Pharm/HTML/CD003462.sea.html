Design

Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.

Scope warning

This is historical placebo-controlled evidence. The review itself notes limited applicability to later NSTEACS care using P2Y12 inhibitors and cannot define a preferred contemporary heparin regimen.

One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first for historical efficacy foundation; do not use alone for current-practice regimen selection.

Core thesis. Across eight older randomized trials (3,118 participants) in unstable angina/NSTEMI, parenteral UFH or LMWH added to aspirin reduced myocardial infarction compared with placebo or untreated control, but did not demonstrate a mortality benefit. Minor bleeding increased substantially; major bleeding showed an imprecise trend toward harm. The authors rated the clinically important evidence as low quality.

What is most reusable. The strongest quantitative signal is prevention of MI: RR 0.40 (95% CI 0.25-0.63), with 24/1238 events on heparin versus 57/1188 controls and an NNTB of 33. The death/MI composite also favored heparin (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.47-0.80). Mortality remained uncertain (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.36-1.98). Minor bleeding increased (RR 6.80, 95% CI 1.23-37.49; NNTH 17), while major bleeding was too imprecise to establish a clear difference (RR 2.05, 95% CI 0.91-4.60).

Interpretive constraint. The trials predate routine modern dual-antiplatelet therapy; endpoint timing, heparin regimens, and some outcome definitions varied. Only one included study was assessed as low risk of bias overall, and safety outcomes were incompletely reported.

Section condensation

Abstract / plain-language summary

The review asked whether parenteral UFH or LMWH improves outcomes compared with placebo or untreated control in unstable angina/NSTEMI. Eight trials with 3,118 participants were included and no new trial was added in the 2014 update.

The abstract reports no clear mortality difference, fewer myocardial infarctions with heparin, a trend toward more major bleeding, sparse thrombocytopenia data, and low overall certainty. The authors emphasize caution because the evidence quality was low.

Background and rationale

The review frames NSTEACS as plaque disruption with coagulation activation and platelet aggregation, giving anticoagulation a mechanistic rationale. UFH and LMWH differ in pharmacologic predictability, and prior reviews suggested benefit but had incomplete searches and weak risk-of-bias assessment.

The clinical question was therefore the drug-class effect of heparins versus no heparin, not a head-to-head modern anticoagulant strategy.

Objectives

To update the prior review of heparin versus placebo for NSTEACS and estimate effects of UFH/LMWH on clinically relevant outcomes.

Methods

Eligible studies were randomized controlled trials in adults with unstable angina or NSTEMI requiring treatment within 72 hours. All participants received aspirin; intervention arms received parenteral UFH or LMWH and controls received placebo or no heparin. Primary outcomes were death, MI, and major hemorrhage; secondary outcomes included recurrent angina, revascularization, minor bleeding, and thrombocytopenia.

The update searched CENTRAL, MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, and LILACS through 15 January 2014 without language restrictions. Review processes used independent screening/extraction and Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment. Dichotomous outcomes were pooled as risk ratios with 95% CIs; I² quantified heterogeneity; sensitivity/subgroup analyses and GRADE were used.

Results: included evidence

Eight RCTs involving 3,118 participants were included. Average age was 62 years and about 75% were male. Roughly half the participants were eligible for LMWH and half for UFH. Regimens, timing, and follow-up varied; aspirin was standard concomitant therapy.

Risk-of-bias reporting was incomplete. Only two trials clearly described adequate sequence generation, only two clearly described allocation concealment, several trials were not blinded, and two were stopped early. GRADE rated clinically important outcomes as low quality.

Results: efficacy and safety

Heparin reduced MI (RR 0.40, 95% CI 0.25-0.63) and the death/MI composite (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.47-0.80). Mortality, recurrent angina, and revascularization were not significantly different overall. Recurrent angina was heterogeneous (I² 65%), and a fixed-effect sensitivity model crossed into statistical significance.

Minor bleeding increased markedly (RR 6.80, 95% CI 1.23-37.49), while major bleeding showed a non-significant trend toward harm (RR 2.05, 95% CI 0.91-4.60). Thrombocytopenia evidence was extremely sparse. LMWH subgroup estimates favored heparin for recurrent angina and revascularization, but the authors warn against indirect LMWH-versus-UFH conclusions.

Discussion

The authors interpret the MI reduction as the clearest benefit, with mortality underpowered because death was uncommon. They stress that absolute benefits were small for several endpoints, heterogeneity affected recurrent angina/minor bleeding/revascularization, and side effects were poorly reported.

External validity is a major problem: the trials largely predate routine P2Y12 use. Possible publication bias and selective patient enrollment further limit confidence. The authors conclude that the evidence base does not support strong conclusions about all benefits and harms.

Authors' conclusions

The authors support early heparin treatment as an adjunct to aspirin because MI was reduced, but not mortality. They could not recommend a specific dosing regimen because of the small and heterogeneous evidence base.

Research gaps include optimal initiation timing, treatment duration, patient-centered outcomes, and cost-effectiveness. Indirect comparisons implying LMWH superiority over UFH were explicitly described as unreliable and potentially misleading.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance

9/10

Rationale: Directly addresses the foundational efficacy and safety evidence for heparin in NSTEACS.

Evidence basis: Eight placebo-controlled RCTs and pooled clinically relevant outcomes.

Principal limit: Historical rather than current-practice context.

Would change score: Current comparative evidence in modern antiplatelet/PCI practice would raise implementation relevance.

Novelty

4/10

Rationale: Historically important but the underlying trials and the principal review conclusions are old.

Evidence basis: Review published 2014 and declared stable in 2021.

Principal limit: No new included trials in the 2014 update.

Would change score: New randomized evidence against a clinically relevant contemporary comparator would raise novelty.

Method strength

7/10

Rationale: Cochrane review methods were explicit and included independent review, risk-of-bias assessment, sensitivity analysis, and GRADE.

Evidence basis: Multiple databases, independent extraction, Cochrane RoB tool, pooled effect models, I², GRADE.

Principal limit: Only eight trials; several domains were unclear/high risk and outcome definitions/timing varied.

Would change score: More high-quality trials with consistently reported methods and outcomes would raise this score.

Evidence strength

5/10

Rationale: The MI and composite signals are statistically clear, but the review grades clinically important evidence as low quality and several outcomes are imprecise or sparsely reported.

Evidence basis: MI RR 0.40; death/MI RR 0.61; mortality CI 0.36-1.98; major bleeding CI 0.91-4.60; thrombocytopenia based on two studies.

Principal limit: Risk of bias, imprecision, safety under-reporting, possible publication bias.

Would change score: Larger well-conducted trials with complete safety reporting and precise estimates would raise the score.

External validity

3/10

Rationale: Applicability to contemporary NSTEACS management is limited.

Evidence basis: The review states that evidence came from older trials before P2Y12 blockers were widely used.

Principal limit: Selected trial populations, older co-therapy, varying heparin regimens and endpoint timing.

Would change score: Evidence embedded in modern DAPT, early invasive care, and current anticoagulant strategies would raise the score.

Implementation value

5/10

Rationale: Useful for understanding why heparin became standard early therapy, but not sufficient to select a modern regimen or current treatment strategy.

Evidence basis: Authors support early heparin but cannot recommend a particular dosing regimen.

Principal limit: Placebo comparison is historical and the review was declared stable because such comparisons are no longer expected.

Would change score: Contemporary regimen-specific comparative evidence would raise the score.

Independent bottom line

Do not use this review as a stand-alone current NSTEACS treatment protocol. It is best used as historical evidence establishing that anticoagulation reduced early ischemic events versus no heparin, while also increasing bleeding. It does not resolve contemporary choice of UFH versus LMWH, duration, dose, or anticoagulation strategy in the setting of modern antiplatelet therapy and invasive management.

Confidence in appraisal: High for what the retrieved review reports and for the internal methodological critique; lower for current-practice translation because no external/current verification was requested or performed.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use for

Historical efficacy rationale for heparin in NSTEACS; quantitative benefit-harm framing; provenance for older placebo-controlled evidence.

Do not use for

Choosing a contemporary anticoagulant regimen, asserting LMWH superiority over UFH, or estimating current bleeding risk under modern DAPT/PCI practice.

Most useful numbers

MI RR 0.40; death/MI RR 0.61; mortality RR 0.84; major bleeding RR 2.05; minor bleeding RR 6.80; MI NNTB 33; minor-bleed NNTH 17.

Research gaps named by source

Optimal initiation timing, treatment duration, quality of life, and cost-effectiveness; robust contemporary comparative evidence is outside this review.
