One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In TIMI II patients treated with rt-PA, heparin, and aspirin, bleeding reflected an interaction between invasive procedures, anticoagulation intensity, patient characteristics, thrombocytopenia, and the plasmin-mediated hemostatic defect. The cleanest causal signal was the randomized invasive-versus-conservative management comparison.

Among patients receiving the 100-mg rt-PA regimen, major or minor non-surgery-related hemorrhage occurred in 18.5% with invasive management versus 12.8% with conservative management (P<0.001). The excess clustered on days 2-3, when protocol-prescribed angiography/intervention occurred: day-2 relative risk 1.90 and day-3 relative risk 2.74. Major hemorrhage alone was 7.0% versus 4.2%. In conservatively assigned patients, angiography performed for clinical indications was also associated with more severe bleeding (major hemorrhage 7.7% with angiography vs 3.0% without).

Bleeding risk was also associated with APTT >90 seconds (major hemorrhage RR 1.71; major/minor RR 1.36, both with 99% CIs excluding 1), low body weight, female sex, cardiac decompensation, hypertension for major events, thrombocytopenia, low fibrinogen, and high rt-PA antigen levels. The 150-mg rt-PA era showed more intracranial hemorrhage (2.1% vs 0.5%) and a much larger fibrinogenolytic effect, but that dose comparison was not randomized and coincided with eligibility and aspirin-timing changes.

Best use: historical safety evidence supporting disciplined anticoagulation monitoring and recognition of procedure- and patient-related bleeding risk during thrombolytic therapy. Do not use for: setting modern alteplase or UFH protocols without current guideline/protocol verification.

Section condensation

Abstract / clinical question

The authors asked how invasive procedures, hemostatic and clinical variables, beta-blocker timing, and rt-PA dose related to hemorrhagic events during TIMI II. All patients received rt-PA, heparin, and aspirin; management strategy was randomized, and eligible patients in a substudy were also randomized to immediate or deferred beta-blockade.

The abstract highlights three central results: invasive management increased major/minor hemorrhage with 100-mg rt-PA (18.5% vs 12.8%); multiple hemostatic and clinical variables correlated with bleeding; and intracranial hemorrhage was more common in the 150-mg than 100-mg rt-PA era (2.1% vs 0.5%).

Introduction

The introduction frames hemorrhage as a consequence of thrombolytic agents acting on physiologic hemostatic plugs, systemic plasmin-mediated degradation of hemostatic proteins, and adjunctive anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy. Prior trials had shown that bleeding frequency varied strongly with use of invasive procedures and that the magnitude of fibrinogenolysis was not precise enough for individual prediction.

This analysis was positioned as an extension of smaller rt-PA studies, with special attention to the 100-mg conservative-management cohort because the TIMI II investigators viewed that approach as the preferred clinical strategy after the main trial results.

Methods

TIMI II enrolled 3339 patients. The first 520 received alteplase 150 mg over 6 hours; the remaining 2819 received 100 mg over 6 hours. UFH consisted of a 5000-unit IV bolus within 1 hour of rt-PA, then 1000 units/hour titrated to an APTT 1.5-2 times laboratory control; from day 5, UFH was given 10,000 units subcutaneously every 12 hours until discharge. Aspirin timing changed during the trial after unexpected intracranial hemorrhage: it was initially started at 80 mg/day on the thrombolysis day, later delayed until the next day, then increased to 325 mg/day on day 6.

Hemorrhage was independently reviewed by a blinded committee. Major bleeding included intracranial hemorrhage, tamponade, hemorrhagic death, or >50 g/L hemoglobin fall; minor bleeding required a >30 to ≤50 g/L fall with an identified site or specified gross bleeding. Laboratory sampling at baseline, 50 minutes, 5 hours, and 8 hours measured fibrinogen, fibrin(ogen) degradation products, rt-PA antigen, and plasminogen. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests, unpaired t tests, correlations, stepwise logistic regression, and a time-dependent Cox model for APTT >90 seconds during days 1-5.

Results

The randomized management comparison showed more bleeding with invasive care. In the 100-mg cohort, major hemorrhage was 7.0% versus 4.2%, minor hemorrhage 11.5% versus 8.7%, and the combined major/minor rate 18.5% versus 12.8% for invasive versus conservative strategy. The time course strengthens the procedure link: no meaningful day-1 difference, but relative risks rose to 1.90 on day 2 and 2.74 on day 3, then diminished. Within the conservative arm, clinically indicated angiography also identified a higher-risk/procedure-exposed subgroup (major hemorrhage 7.7% vs 3.0%).

In the 100-mg conservative group, major/minor hemorrhage was associated with low fibrinogen, high rt-PA antigen, female sex, weight ≤70 kg, cardiac decompensation, and thrombocytopenia; hypertension was especially associated with major hemorrhage. APTT >90 seconds carried RR 1.71 for major and 1.36 for major/minor hemorrhage. The 150-mg era had more intracranial hemorrhage and markedly greater fibrinogenolysis: at 5 hours, fibrinogen averaged 90 vs 195 mg/dL, and 59.5% vs 11.3% had fibrinogen <100 mg/dL for 150 vs 100 mg.

Discussion / authors’ interpretation

The authors attributed the excess bleeding with invasive management to catheterization/procedural exposure and noted that the randomized assignment made confounding an unlikely explanation for that comparison. They also emphasized that moderate hemoglobin reductions without an identified site can occur and may partly reflect phlebotomy, fluid shifts, or measurement effects rather than clinically important hemorrhage.

Hemostatic markers were associated with bleeding at the population level but were not sufficiently predictive for individual monitoring. Platelet counts fell for several days after rt-PA and during heparin exposure; the authors regarded the timing as more consistent with a possible heparin contribution than a direct rt-PA effect. For rt-PA dose, they explicitly cautioned that the 150- versus 100-mg comparison overlapped with changes in eligibility criteria and aspirin timing.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a heparin/thrombolysis historical deep dive; do not use alone for current practice. The article is particularly useful because it separates a randomized procedural bleeding signal from observational hemostatic/patient risk associations and directly analyzes excessive APTT during UFH therapy.

Main strengths

  • Large multicenter dataset with randomized management strategy.
  • Blinded, committee-based hemorrhage classification with explicit definitions.
  • Detailed anticoagulation protocol and serial hemostatic measurements.
  • Temporal bleeding analysis links excess events to invasive-procedure days.
  • Time-dependent APTT analysis is directly relevant to UFH safety.

Main limitations

  • rt-PA dose comparison is sequential rather than randomized and overlaps with other protocol changes.
  • Many risk-factor analyses are secondary/exploratory and stepwise regression can overfit or select unstable predictors.
  • The study’s own multiplicity rule treats P values between 0.01 and 0.001 cautiously; several nominal associations are therefore weaker than a conventional P<0.05 reading would suggest.
  • Hemoglobin-based event definitions may overestimate clinically meaningful bleeding when no site is identified.
  • Historical thrombolysis-era practice limits direct generalization to contemporary reperfusion and anticoagulation workflows.
Key interpretation boundary: The randomized invasive-vs-conservative strategy comparison supports a causal procedural contribution to bleeding. The 150-vs-100 mg rt-PA comparison and patient/laboratory predictor analyses should be treated as observational associations within the trial dataset.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Heparin monitoring

APTT >90 seconds was associated with higher same-day hemorrhage risk. This is historical evidence against allowing markedly supratherapeutic anticoagulation during thrombolytic therapy.

Body size

Lower body weight was repeatedly associated with bleeding, and the authors explicitly raised reduced or weight-adjusted dosing as a consideration.

Platelets

Severe thrombocytopenia was uncommon but strongly associated with bleeding; the authors recommended daily platelet checks after thrombolytic therapy and considered heparin a possible contributor.

Procedures

Catheterization drove much of the randomized bleeding excess, emphasizing procedure timing and access-site exposure as major determinants of hemorrhage.

Fibrinogenolysis

Low fibrinogen/high rt-PA levels identify population-level bleeding associations but were not sufficiently discriminative for individual prediction.

Current-use boundary

Use the paper to understand historical mechanisms and protocol evolution; cross-check any dosing/monitoring decisions against current evidence and local policy.
