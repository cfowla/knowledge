Executive synthesis

Verdict: highly useful as a cardiorenal management synthesis and research agenda, but it is not a graded clinical practice guideline.

The report's main clinical message is that kidney-function changes in heart failure must be interpreted in context. A small creatinine rise or estimated glomerular filtration rate decline after decongestion or guideline-directed therapy can be hemodynamic rather than structural kidney injury. Stopping effective heart-failure therapy solely because creatinine moved in the wrong direction can therefore be a treatment error. The report repeatedly redirects attention to congestion, blood pressure, symptoms, potassium, hemodynamics, and the trajectory of kidney function.

The second major message is diagnostic. Estimated glomerular filtration rate alone is not enough. Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio adds prognostic information and remains underused. Natriuretic peptides remain useful in chronic kidney disease, but the report does not endorse a CKD-specific diagnostic cutoff because none is established. Imaging and invasive hemodynamics become more important when kidney failure, fluid retention, obesity, or other factors make symptoms and biomarkers hard to interpret.

The treatment section is practical but needs a source-type guardrail. Tables and figures integrate heart-failure guidance, kidney guidance, trial evidence, and conference opinion. They are conceptual management frameworks, not newly graded KDIGO recommendations. The strongest implementation value is in preserving disease-modifying therapy when kidney changes are expected and manageable, identifying the small group that needs investigation for true acute kidney injury, and using objective response measures during decongestion.

Pathophysiology

The report treats HF and CKD as bidirectional disorders that share much of their upstream biology. Obesity, diabetes, and hypertension form the "common soil." Figure 2 then groups downstream mechanisms into hemodynamic, neurohormonal, and inflammatory processes. The hemodynamic branch includes salt and water retention, venous congestion, hypoperfusion, vasoconstriction, and higher abdominal pressure. The neurohormonal branch includes sympathetic and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation plus changes in vasopressin and natriuretic peptides. The inflammatory branch includes oxidative stress, immune activation, endothelial dysfunction, gut dysbiosis, and uremic toxins.

This framework is clinically useful because it resists a simple low-cardiac-output explanation for kidney dysfunction. Blood pressure change, venous pressure, cardiac output, tubular adaptation, and neurohormonal effects may each contribute, and their relative importance varies. The source is explicit that mechanistic certainty is limited. Clinical studies disagree on whether venous congestion is causal or mainly a marker of worse HF, and available tubular injury markers do not reliably separate reversible functional decline from intrinsic injury.

Appraisal. Figure 2 is a mechanistic synthesis, not a causal model validated by this report. Its value is organizational. It tells the reader which mechanisms need to be considered and which remain unresolved.

Diagnostic dilemmas

Kidney assessment in heart failure

The report supports repeated eGFR assessment and routine UACR measurement. UACR is not presented as a nephrology-only test. It carries cardiovascular and HF prognostic information. The report cites a large US laboratory analysis in which kidney assessment was markedly incomplete, with UACR testing far less common than eGFR testing. That implementation gap is one reason Table 1 calls for wider UACR awareness and integration into HF diagnostic recommendations.

Creatinine and acute kidney injury

Creatinine is a weak stand-alone adjudicator of kidney injury during acute HF. It changes with muscle mass, performs best near steady state, and can lag behind rapid physiology. More importantly, a rise during effective decongestion can coexist with better outcomes. The report therefore argues that conventional AKI definitions may need a heart-failure-specific refinement that separates expected hemodynamic change from intrinsic injury.

Natriuretic peptides

The report cites general diagnostic thresholds of BNP at least 35 pg/mL and NT-proBNP at least 125 pg/mL for ambulatory evaluation, and BNP at least 100 pg/mL and NT-proBNP at least 300 pg/mL in acute hospital or emergency settings. CKD shifts natriuretic peptide levels upward, while obesity and HFpEF can shift them downward. No CKD-specific diagnostic cutoff is established. The report therefore recommends context, serial trajectory, and when useful an individual asymptomatic baseline rather than a single universal CKD threshold.

Imaging and hemodynamics

Echocardiography can support HF diagnosis, define phenotype, estimate filling pressure, and identify structural causes. Advanced CKD complicates interpretation because fluid retention from kidney failure can itself raise filling pressures. In hemodialysis patients, the report says imaging should ideally occur on nondialysis days. Right-heart catheterization becomes useful when shock, congestion, hypoperfusion, or AKI cannot be sorted out by examination and noninvasive testing.

HF staging

Figure 3 proposes that CKD be explicitly incorporated into HF stage A. The figure uses eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio above 30 mg/g as the CKD risk criterion. This is a conference proposal layered onto the universal HF staging framework, not a new universal definition formally issued by the original HF staging societies.

Treatment

HFrEF and CKD

The report carries forward the four major HFrEF medication classes: angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibition, SGLT2 inhibition, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism, and beta-blockade. CKD makes implementation harder because creatinine, potassium, blood pressure, and volume status can change during treatment. The report's response is not therapeutic minimalism. It favors individualized but persistent optimization, with mitigation of hyperkalemia and slower titration when needed.

Advanced CKD is the main evidence boundary. Patients with G4 to G5 CKD and dialysis-treated kidney failure are often excluded from randomized HF trials. The report is careful not to claim that standard HFrEF evidence is equally strong in G5D.

HFpEF and CKD

SGLT2 inhibitors are the most established disease-modifying treatment discussed for HFpEF. The report also incorporates finerenone data from FINEARTS-HF and GLP-1 receptor agonist evidence in obesity-related HFpEF. These newer data broaden the cardiorenal treatment discussion, but advanced CKD and dialysis remain underrepresented.

How to interpret kidney-function change

Most actionable point. A small, stable eGFR decline after starting effective therapy is not the same as progressive kidney injury. The report says a single mild 10% to 15% decline that stabilizes is less concerning than a continuing decline. Table 2 states that hemodynamic eGFR fluctuations up to 30% can occur with several cardiorenal drug classes and should not automatically lead to discontinuation. A decline above 30% should prompt evaluation for other causes of AKI.

The source also makes a useful hierarchy explicit: treat symptoms, volume status, and hemodynamics rather than serum creatinine alone. Urinalysis, potassium, blood pressure, decongestion response, and serial kidney trajectory help decide whether the laboratory change is acceptable or dangerous.

Iron, sodium, and exercise

The report describes intravenous iron as potentially useful for symptoms in iron-deficient HF but notes that large randomized trials have not consistently improved hard cardiovascular outcomes. It also challenges strict sodium restriction as a universal outcome-improving strategy. Trials using roughly 1500 to 2500 mg daily have not consistently reduced death or HF hospitalization. Sodium restriction may still have a role for selected patients or symptom and quality-of-life goals. Exercise remains recommended in HF and CKD, but the optimal program for patients with both diseases is not established.

Volume overload and decongestion

Loop and thiazide diuretics remain symptom-control tools, not survival therapies. The report therefore separates successful decongestion from disease modification. Diuretics should relieve volume overload while ARNI, SGLT2 inhibitors, and other disease-modifying therapies are initiated or restored when clinically possible.

Figure 5 proposed diuretic framework

  1. Recognize that low eGFR or high albuminuria increases the likelihood of diuretic resistance.
  2. Start intravenous loop diuretic at least 2.5 times the outpatient dose. Lower eGFR may require still higher doses.
  3. Continue guideline-directed medical therapy if there is no adverse-effect concern.
  4. At 1 to 2 hours, target spot urine sodium above 50 to 70 mEq/L.
  5. At 6 hours, target urine output above 100 to 150 mL/h.
  6. At 24 hours, target weight reduction of 1 to 3 kg.
  7. If response is inadequate, double the loop-diuretic dose and consider sequential nephron blockade with a thiazide, potassium-sparing diuretic, or acetazolamide.
  8. Once euvolemia and steady-state kidney function are achieved, resume or initiate guideline-directed therapy.

The framework is operationally useful because it converts "diurese more" into measurable response targets. It remains a proposed framework modified from ESC guidance, not an outcome-validated KDIGO protocol.

Evidence examples summarized by the report

CLOROTIC is cited for greater 72-hour weight loss with hydrochlorothiazide added to loop diuretics, 2.3 kg versus 1.5 kg, with P = 0.002. Acetazolamide 500 mg once daily added to standardized intravenous loop diuretics improved successful decongestion but did not reduce three-month death or HF rehospitalization. CARESS-HF is used as a caution against routine early ultrafiltration because ultrafiltration did not improve outcomes and caused a larger creatinine rise than maximized diuretics in the cited population.

Clinical trial design

The report argues that HF and CKD trials should stop behaving as if the other organ is a side issue. Both eGFR and UACR should be measured. Trial populations should be characterized by HF phenotype, albuminuria, kidney function, natriuretic peptides, hospitalization status, ischemic status, and metabolic disease. Figure 6 then links those phenotypes to early biomarker and functional outcomes and to later mortality, hospitalization, kidney failure, dialysis, and device-support outcomes.

Baseline kidney function

For hospitalized participants, the preferred baseline is two outpatient creatinine measurements more than 90 days apart and at least seven days before hospitalization. If that history is unavailable, the report discusses alternatives such as the first available or nadir inpatient creatinine and recommends prespecified sensitivity or subgroup analyses.

Endpoints

Cardiorenal endpoint design has a timing problem. HF events accumulate faster than kidney failure events, while kidney-protective therapies can cause an early hemodynamic eGFR dip that makes short-term slope interpretation harder. Traditional kidney composites, lower eGFR-decline thresholds, eGFR slope, hierarchical composites, recurrent-event methods, win-ratio approaches, days alive out of hospital, and quality-of-life adjusted endpoints each solve part of the problem and introduce another tradeoff.

The report states that no chronic HF trial had yet used a primary composite endpoint integrating cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. It also notes that typical HF trial follow-up of about 1.5 to 2.5 years is often too short to capture slow CKD progression, especially in populations not enriched for albuminuria.

Patient-reported outcomes and core data

Validated patient-reported outcome measures should be used in most, if not all, randomized trials. The report emphasizes physical and social function that routine laboratory and clinical endpoints miss. It also calls for core cardiovascular-kidney data sets so results can be compared and interpreted consistently across disciplines.

Appraisal

10/10

Relevance

Directly addresses the highest-friction decisions in patients who have both HF and CKD, including biomarkers, decongestion, GDMT, hyperkalemia, and trial endpoints.

8/10

Novelty

Integrates recent SGLT2 inhibitor, finerenone, GLP-1 receptor agonist, decongestion, and endpoint work and makes several concrete proposals, including CKD in HF stage A and refinement of AKI definitions.

6/10

Method strength

The multidisciplinary conference process is credible and transparent at a high level, but the report does not describe a systematic review, formal evidence grading, or a reproducible consensus-voting method for each clinical statement.

7/10

Evidence strength

Many positions are supported by large trials, meta-analyses, and major guidelines. The weakest area is advanced CKD and kidney failure, which the report repeatedly identifies as underrepresented.

7/10

External validity

The report is international and cross-disciplinary, but much of the therapeutic evidence comes from trial populations that exclude or thinly represent G4, G5, dialysis, and stage D HF.

9/10

Implementation value

It offers concrete thresholds, monitoring principles, and two practical management frameworks. Implementation still requires alignment with current formal guidelines and local protocols because this is a conference report.

Main strengths

  • Cardiology and nephrology questions are handled in one document rather than as separate disease silos.
  • The report makes the creatinine problem clinically explicit and distinguishes expected hemodynamic change from likely injury.
  • UACR is elevated from a background CKD test to a core cardiorenal risk measure.
  • Figures 4 and 5 translate broad principles into eGFR-based therapy and diuretic-response workflows.
  • The trial-design section explains why kidney-protective therapies can look less kidney-protective in HF trials without assuming the therapies have different biology.

Main limitations

  • This is not a systematic review or graded guideline. The report combines evidence review, expert interpretation, and proposed frameworks.
  • Advanced CKD, dialysis, transplantation, and stage D HF remain evidence-poor.
  • CKD-specific natriuretic peptide thresholds and validated markers that separate hemodynamic from intrinsic AKI are missing.
  • Several treatment thresholds come from different guideline sources that do not fully align.
  • Figure 1 compares event burden across different trial programs and populations. It should not be used as a cross-trial efficacy comparison.

Clinical use boundary

This report is best used to interpret cardiorenal physiology, avoid reflex discontinuation of useful therapy, plan monitoring, and identify evidence gaps. It should not replace a current KDIGO CKD guideline, a current HF guideline, a local diuretic protocol, or patient-specific clinical judgment.
