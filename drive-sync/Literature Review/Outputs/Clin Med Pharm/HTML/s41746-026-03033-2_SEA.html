One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Digital-health RCT publications expanded much faster than the broader ClinicalTrials.gov RCT-result set from 2010–2023, while reporting of digital intervention characteristics was consistently detectable but reporting of participant social/demographic characteristics and technology-access considerations was uneven. The authors argue that more standardized common data elements are needed to support equitable and accessible digital-health research.

The analysis begins with 16,924 database records, reduces to 7,527 screened records after duplicate/keyword removal, and uses LLM-assisted screening to identify 5,452 eligible reports. Region-specific extraction then operates on 5,298 abstracts, 4,835 eligibility-criteria sections, and 4,304 baseline demographic tables. The pipeline combines programmatic document parsing, OCR for demographic tables, standardized GPT-4o prompts via Azure OpenAI, and normalization against digital-health, WHO, AAMC, CONSORT, and social-determinant taxonomies.

The headline descriptive findings are large in scale and internally coherent: digital-health-enabled RCT publications rose from 100 in 2010 to 790 in 2023 (7.9-fold); the manuscript describes about 17–18% annual growth versus 9% for all ClinicalTrials.gov RCTs with results. Psychiatry was the largest specialty (14%, n=788). In abstracts, technology type/name, purpose, and intended user were extracted for 100% of 5,298 reports, but trial registry identifiers were found in only 36%. Technology-related eligibility criteria were present in more than half of studies when inclusion/exclusion categories are combined, with internet access (27%) and device access (20%) the most common access requirements. Baseline reporting was nearly universal for age (98%) and high for sex/gender (87%), but substantially lower for education (47%), race/ethnicity (29%), income (15%), internet access (6%), insurance (4%), disability (2%), and environmental safety (<1%).

Interpretive boundary. Low reporting of race/ethnicity, disability, insurance, or other variables is evidence of incomplete reporting—not direct evidence that the underlying trial samples lacked diversity. Likewise, requiring internet/device access plausibly creates an access barrier, but this study does not quantify how many otherwise-eligible people were excluded or whether provision of devices would change enrollment composition.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame digital health as a rapidly expanding evidence domain in which both access to trials and reporting quality determine generalizability and reproducibility. Using LLM-assisted review methods, they assess technology and study-population reporting across 5,452 digital-health-technology-enabled RCT outcomes published from 2010 through January 2024.

The abstract emphasizes >17% compound annual growth, technology-related eligibility criteria in roughly half of trials, and sharply uneven demographic reporting: age 98%, sex/gender 87%, race/ethnicity 29%. The stated implication is a need for international consensus around common data elements for digital-health trials.

Introduction

The introduction links rapid digital-health adoption with regulatory pressure for rigorous trial evaluation and notes that traditional trial reporting frameworks may not fully capture technology-specific details. It situates the work against EQUATOR resources and reporting standards including iCHECK-DH, CONSORT, CONSORT-AI, and CONSORT-EHEALTH, arguing that adoption and real-world reporting behavior remain incompletely characterized.

The study question is therefore not whether digital interventions work, but who can participate in their trials and how technology/population characteristics are documented. The authors position LLM-assisted mining as a way to characterize this at scale.

Results — corpus growth and specialty distribution

From 16,924 published final outcomes identified by the literature search, 5,452 eligible reports underwent full-text information extraction. The authors report an 18% average annual growth rate for digital-health-enabled RCT publications, increasing from 100 in 2010 to 790 in 2023. For a 2010–2023 comparison framed as compound annual growth, the manuscript reports 17% for digital-health-enabled RCTs versus 9% for all ClinicalTrials.gov RCTs with results (3.2-fold increase from 1,036 to 3,305).

The leading specialties were Psychiatry 14% (n=788), Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation 6% (329), Cardiology 6% (320), Pediatric and Adolescent Medicine 5% (246), Endocrinology 4% (237), Obstetrics/Gynecology 4% (222), Oncology 4% (202), Neurology 4% (200), Orthopedic Surgery 3% (148), and Infectious Disease 2% (131). Frequent title keywords included internet, web-based, online, mobile, virtual reality, app, smartphone, digital, computer, and electronic.

Results — abstract reporting

Across 5,298 parsed abstracts, the study reports 100% extraction/reporting of technology class/product name, purpose, and intended user. Intended users were categorized non-mutually exclusively as persons (50%, n=2,635), persons and providers (29%, n=1,533), and providers (21%, n=1,101). Technology purpose was most often assistance (32%, n=1,677), education (16%, n=863), and treatment (14%, n=726).

A trial registry number was identified in only 36% of abstracts (n=1,905). The authors explicitly caution that this is an abstract reporting measure and likely understates actual registration.

Results — technology access and eligibility

Among 4,835 parsed Methods sections, technology-related eligibility appeared in inclusion criteria for 26% (n=1,268), exclusion criteria for 7% (n=319), and both for 19% (n=906), supporting the authors’ statement that more than half had technology-related participation criteria. Keyword analysis found internet-access requirements in 27% (n=1,323) and device-access requirements in 20% (n=982), commonly involving smartphones, computers, or tablets.

Language requirements were detected in 9% (n=457) and technology-related disability criteria in 8% (n=382). The manuscript interprets these as possible participation barriers but also recognizes practical constraints in providing infrastructure or devices.

Results — baseline participant reporting

Among 4,304 extracted baseline demographic tables, age was reported in 98% and sex/gender in 87%. Reporting then dropped to education 47%, race/ethnicity 29%, employment 25%, income 15%, internet access 6%, insurance 4%, housing 3%, disability 2%, and environmental safety <1%.

These percentages describe whether variables were present in baseline reporting. They do not reveal the actual distributions of those characteristics within trial participants, nor do they establish whether the enrolled samples were representative of target populations.

Discussion and limitations

The authors argue that technology-specific eligibility details matter because digital trials can introduce new access barriers, and they suggest temporary device provision, transportation support to public internet access points, and decentralized approaches as possible inclusion strategies. They also call for more consistent reporting of social determinants and identity characteristics while acknowledging privacy and safety concerns for vulnerable groups.

Reported limitations include lower machine-assisted performance than traditional manual methods for some tasks, publisher/layout heterogeneity causing parsing failures and reduced denominators, ambiguity in sex/gender and race/ethnicity labels, and omission of sparsely reported characteristics such as immigration status. Additional appraisal concerns are that extraction error is not propagated into uncertainty around the landscape estimates, the literature search is centered on Ovid MEDLINE, and the ClinicalTrials.gov growth comparator is not a matched corpus.

Methods and validation

A professional librarian developed the search in Ovid MEDLINE and related Ovid record states through 8 Feb 2024; eligible publication years ran from 2010 through Jan 2024. Articles were parsed into content regions using Python 3.13.3, and OCR with Python-tesseract 5.0.0 was used for baseline demographic tables. Standardized prompts sent region-specific text to GPT-4o through Azure OpenAI Services. Outputs were normalized using ODHT, WHO digital intervention user categories, AAMC specialties, CONSORT 2010 items, and proposed social-determinant domains. Sex/gender and race/ethnicity were intentionally collapsed because source reporting was inconsistent.

For screening, a randomly sampled gold standard of 587 articles was independently annotated by two reviewers and adjudicated to 100% consensus; the paper reports mean inter-annotator F1 0.939 and LLM F1 0.954 across two subgroup analyses. For full-text information extraction on 90 eligible articles (50 PDFs, 40 XML), LLM F1 was 0.980 for abstracts, 0.945 for eligibility criteria, and 0.912 for baseline demographic tables, compared with inter-annotator F1 of 0.894, 0.963, and 0.962, respectively. Descriptive statistics used Python 3.13.3 and R 4.5.1. The authors state that code and datasets are publicly available through the OHNLP/ACCEL GitHub repository.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first for work on LLM-assisted evidence synthesis, scientific information extraction, or digital-health reporting quality. For readers interested only in whether digital-health interventions are clinically effective, this is a secondary-methods paper rather than an efficacy source.
Relevance
9/10

Directly useful for large-scale literature mining, reporting-quality measurement, and digital-health evidence infrastructure. Evidence basis: 5,452-report corpus plus explicit extraction validation. Limit: it studies reporting metadata rather than clinical outcomes. Would raise: direct integration into guideline/evidence-review workflows with downstream impact evaluation.

Novelty
8/10

Combines a large real-world digital-health RCT landscape with an LLM-assisted extraction pipeline and equity/access reporting questions. Evidence basis: multi-region extraction at scale and specialty/access analyses. Limit: screening/extraction with LLMs is not itself methodologically novel, and the approach extends the group’s prior ACCEL work. Would raise: new validated methods for uncertainty-aware or cross-publisher extraction.

Method strength
7/10

Strong curation and task-level F1 evaluation support the pipeline. Evidence basis: random 587-article screening gold standard, adjudication, 90 full-text documents across PDF/XML, explicit F1 by task. Limit: MEDLINE-centered retrieval, reduced extractable denominators, selected validation sets, and no uncertainty propagation. Would raise: multi-database replication, preregistered validation, error-stratified estimates, and sensitivity analysis.

Evidence strength
7/10

The descriptive reporting frequencies are supported by a very large corpus and transparent denominators. Evidence basis: thousands of extracted abstracts/methods/tables and validated extraction performance. Limit: extraction error and missing-region bias can shift prevalence estimates, and confidence intervals are not reported. Would raise: manual audit of a random sample of final prevalence labels and quantitative error-adjusted intervals.

External validity
6/10

Coverage spans many specialties and technology classes, supporting broad relevance within indexed digital-health RCT literature. Evidence basis: 10+ specialties and diverse technology keywords. Limit: one bibliographic search platform/database family, publisher-dependent extractability, and no direct assessment of non-indexed/engineering venues. Would raise: replication across Embase, Scopus/Web of Science, IEEE/ACM, non-English corpora, and independent teams.

Implementation value
8/10

The paper provides a concrete architecture for scalable, region-specific literature extraction and states that code/data are public. Evidence basis: preprocessing, OCR, prompts, ontologies, validation design, and GitHub availability are described. Limit: the supplied manuscript lacks the supplement and the repository was not independently verified here. Would raise: fully versioned prompts, fixtures, error taxonomy, reproducible environment, and end-to-end rerun documentation within the paper/supplement.

Main strengths

  • Large corpus with explicit denominators for each extraction region.
  • Separates screening validation from three different extraction tasks rather than reporting one aggregate metric.
  • Uses human double-annotation/adjudication for the screening gold standard.
  • Links extraction targets to established reporting/taxonomy frameworks rather than inventing unconstrained categories.
  • Makes code and data availability claims that, if reproducible, materially improve auditability.

Principal limitations

  • Reporting ≠ representation: the strongest equity claims remain indirect because participant composition is not analyzed.
  • Access criteria ≠ realized exclusion: internet/device requirements identify potential barriers but not their actual enrollment effect.
  • Missingness is structured: baseline-table extraction falls from 5,452 eligible reports to 4,304, plausibly non-random by publisher/layout; this can bias reporting prevalence.
  • Extraction uncertainty is not propagated: F1 below 1.0—especially 0.912 for demographic tables—means some landscape counts are necessarily imperfect.
  • Comparator asymmetry: published digital-health RCT outcomes and ClinicalTrials.gov studies with results are different datasets; the growth comparison is illustrative, not a matched denominator analysis.
  • Version inconsistency in growth wording: the Results section reports an 18% average annual growth rate, while the abstract/discussion and comparator analysis describe >17%/17% compound annual growth. This does not change the direction of the finding but should be clarified in final publication.
  • Supplementary material unavailable: the exact search strategy and expert-survey details cannot be independently assessed from the supplied PDF.

Bottom line

This is a credible, useful large-scale reporting landscape and a practical demonstration of LLM-assisted evidence extraction. Its most defensible conclusion is that digital-health RCT reporting is heterogeneous and technology-access criteria are common. The evidence is weaker for stronger claims that the trial populations themselves are inequitable, because actual participant distributions and exclusion impacts were not measured.

Implementation / practice takeaways

For literature-extraction systemsUse region-specific extraction tasks rather than one monolithic prompt. Maintain different validation sets for abstracts, eligibility text, and tables because error profiles differ materially.
For schema designRepresent “reported/not reported” separately from the extracted value. Preserve source region, denominator, and whether a category was normalized or collapsed.
For quality controlDo not treat a single F1 score as sufficient. Table/OCR extraction deserves separate monitoring, and final prevalence estimates should ideally include manual spot checks or uncertainty/sensitivity analyses.
For digital-health trialsTechnology access, device ownership, language, and disability accommodations should be explicit eligibility/reporting fields. Where barriers are unavoidable, document mitigation such as device provision or decentralized support.
For equity interpretationCollect and report participant characteristics when justified, but distinguish documentation completeness from actual representativeness and account for privacy risks in vulnerable populations.
Do not overclaimThis paper does not establish that digital health improves equity, worsens equity, or that any specific reporting standard changes trial outcomes. It characterizes the reporting landscape and identifies gaps.
