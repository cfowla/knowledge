One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Standardizing heparin dose adjustment after thrombolytic therapy was associated with better maintenance of the study's aPTT target and substantially fewer heparin interruptions, without an observed increase in major spontaneous hemorrhage; however, nomogram use was not randomized, so the center comparison is confounded.

The study used a fixed starting UFH regimen - 5000 U IV bolus then 1000 U/h for up to 4 days - with an aPTT target of 1.5-2 times control. aPTT was measured at 8, 12, and 24 hours and daily thereafter, including 6-8 hours after a dose change. Table 1 adjusted infusion rates in 25% or 50% increments depending on aPTT. Across the cohort, median aPTT declined from 120 seconds at 8 hours to 51 seconds at 24 hours and 40-47 seconds from 48-96 hours. Subtherapeutic aPTT (<1.5 times control) increased from 4% at 8 hours to 29% at 24 hours and 46% at 48 hours, then remained common at 72 and 96 hours (37% and 34%).

At 24 hours, subtherapeutic patients were younger (55.2±10.6 vs 59.6±10.6 years; P=.02) and heavier (86.4±13.5 vs 78.9±15.7 kg; P=.007). Nine centers used the nomogram and six used local practice. Nomogram centers had significantly fewer subtherapeutic aPTT values at 48 and 96 hours. Heparin was interrupted within 96 hours in 45/118 (38%) vs 68/99 (69%) patients (P<.001). Major spontaneous hemorrhage was 5/118 (4%) vs 4/99 (4%), not significant. Reinfarction and reocclusion were 4% and 5% overall, but event counts were too small for a valid group comparison.

Main interpretive constraint: The randomized element of TIMI 4 concerned thrombolytic regimens, not whether a center used the heparin nomogram. The authors explicitly state that nomogram use was not randomly assigned and that other center-level factors could explain some of the apparent benefit.

Section condensation

Structured abstract

The abstract frames the problem as difficulty maintaining therapeutic anticoagulation after thrombolysis despite a standard heparin bolus and infusion. A center poll divided sites into nomogram users and nonusers. The primary operational signal was the frequency of subtherapeutic aPTT values over 96 hours and interruption of heparin therapy.

The abstract reports worsening subtherapeutic aPTT frequency after the early thrombolytic effect dissipated, identifies younger age and greater weight among patients subtherapeutic at 24 hours, and reports fewer subtherapeutic values at 48/96 hours plus less therapy interruption at nomogram centers. It concludes that a standardized approach improved anticoagulation and proposes weight- and age-sensitive refinements.

Introduction / background

The rationale is that IV heparin helps maintain infarct-related vessel patency after thrombolysis and that inadequate anticoagulation may be associated with lower patency. The authors highlight uncertainty about the upper therapeutic range and bleeding risk, while motivating standardized dose adjustment as a way to reduce periods of inadequate anticoagulation.

The article's specific objective is narrower than the parent TIMI 4 thrombolytic comparison: determine how well the TIMI heparin nomogram achieves/maintains the aPTT target and whether its use appears safe in thrombolysis-treated acute MI.

Methods

TIMI 4 randomized acute-MI patients among front-loaded alteplase, anistreplase, or a combination regimen. All participants received adjunctive aspirin; other therapies were allowed. The heparin regimen was 5000 U IV bolus followed by 1000 U/h for 4 days unless contraindicated. aPTT was measured at 8, 12, and 24 hours and daily thereafter, 6-8 hours after dose changes, and when reinfarction or bleeding occurred. The target was 1.5-2 times control.

Reocclusion required loss of angiographic patency between the 90-minute and 18-36-hour studies. Reinfarction was based on ischemic pain with ECG/enzyme criteria. Proportions were compared by chi-square and means by t tests with two-tailed P<.05. The study did not randomize nomogram use; 9 centers adopted the recommended Table 1 algorithm and 6 used local practice.

Results

The first 217 patients included 158 men and 59 women; mean age was 58.0±10.9 years. Median aPTT values were 120, 73, 51, 40, 44, and 47 seconds at 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours. Subtherapeutic aPTT frequencies were 4%, 14%, 29%, 46%, 37%, and 34% at those same time points. Excluding patients who underwent angioplasty or bypass surgery reportedly produced similar values.

At 24 hours, subtherapeutic patients were younger and heavier. Nomogram centers had fewer subtherapeutic values at 48 and 96 hours. Heparin interruption was 38% vs 69% (P<.001). Forty-five patients stopped heparin because of a major hemorrhagic event; the reported sites included instrumented (29), retroperitoneal (7), gastrointestinal (4), intracranial (1), and 4 patients with >50 g/L hemoglobin decrease without an identified source. Major spontaneous hemorrhage was 4% in each center group. Reinfarction and reocclusion were uncommon but underpowered for group comparison.

Comment / discussion

The authors interpret the center comparison as support for standardized dose adjustment and frequent monitoring, but explicitly acknowledge that nonrandom assignment of nomogram use allows confounding. They also acknowledge residual failure: many patients remained subtherapeutic even where the nomogram was used.

Because subtherapeutic patients at 24 hours were heavier and younger, the authors hypothesize that weight-adjusted and possibly age-specific nomograms could improve performance. They also argue that a larger initial bolus would be unlikely to solve the later subtherapeutic problem and propose more frequent aPTT monitoring, potentially every 6 hours, during the first 24 hours. These are author interpretations/hypotheses rather than tested randomized comparisons in this analysis.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first for a historical heparin-protocol deep dive. Do not use for current practice as a standalone dosing source. Its greatest value is showing how explicit monitoring thresholds and standardized rate changes affected anticoagulation process measures before weight-based nomograms became the dominant conceptual direction.
9/10

Relevance

Directly addresses standardized IV unfractionated-heparin adjustment and aPTT monitoring after thrombolysis.

Evidence basis: Explicit nomogram, monitoring schedule, time-course anticoagulation data, and center comparison.

Limiting factor: Clinical setting is a 1994 thrombolysis-era MI cohort rather than a broad anticoagulation population.

Would change with: Higher only if the task were specifically historical STEMI fibrinolysis/UFH protocol development.

Generalizability to current practice is limited by the historical thrombolysis-era population, fixed initial UFH regimen, and center-specific implementation.

Evidence basis: Acute MI within 6 h, age <80 years, 15 participating centers, 1994 publication.

Limiting factor: Major differences from contemporary ACS systems and anticoagulation practice.

7/10

Implementation value

The paper is unusually explicit about the nomogram logic, monitoring cadence, and interruption workflow, making it useful for understanding protocol design.

Evidence basis: Table 1 provides four adjustment branches; Methods gives aPTT schedule and sheath-management rules.

Limiting factor: The exact dosing algorithm is historical and should not be adopted as a current protocol without contemporary verification.

Would change with: A modern protocol with current dosing/monitoring targets and prospectively validated outcomes.

Validity

  • Strengths: explicit protocol; repeated serial aPTT measurements; clear center exposure definition; operational outcomes; safety accounting; authors acknowledge the central confounding limitation.
  • Bias/confounding: nomogram use was a center practice choice, not randomized. Center culture, case mix, staffing, catheterization practices, and adherence could influence both aPTT management and interruption rates.
  • Statistical limitations: no confidence intervals reported for the key center comparison; no adjusted model reported; clinical event comparisons were underpowered.
  • Outcome hierarchy: improved aPTT target attainment is a surrogate/process outcome. Reinfarction/reocclusion data were too sparse to show clinical benefit, and major spontaneous bleeding was not statistically different.

Pharmacotherapy appraisal

  • Dose: fixed 5000-U IV bolus plus 1000 U/h; not weight-based at initiation.
  • Monitoring: aPTT at 8, 12, 24 h, then daily; 6-8 h after dose change; additional checks for reinfarction/bleeding.
  • Target: aPTT 1.5-2× control.
  • Adjustment logic: percent-based rate changes with four threshold bands and a 2500 U/h maximum on the upward branch.
  • Safety: major spontaneous hemorrhage was similar between center groups, but the study was not powered to exclude clinically important safety differences and included many instrumented/retroperitoneal bleeds in the broader major-hemorrhage count.

Implementation / practice takeaways

High-value concept

Protocolization matters: a shared adjustment algorithm and scheduled reassessment can reduce variability and periods of inadequate anticoagulation.

Design lesson

A fixed starting dose produced predictable later under-anticoagulation in heavier patients, which is a strong historical signal motivating patient-size-sensitive dosing.

Monitoring lesson

The study highlights that dose-adjustment rules are only as effective as the reassessment cadence; delayed aPTT feedback can leave patients subtherapeutic for prolonged periods.

What not to overclaim

Do not infer that the nomogram reduced reinfarction/reocclusion or that bleeding safety was equivalent. The clinical-outcome comparisons were underpowered and the center exposure was confounded.

Best reuse

Historical design input for a heparin nomogram evolution timeline, rule-engine provenance, and rationale for weight-sensitive dosing and standardized monitoring.

Current-practice boundary

Use the article to understand protocol architecture and historical evidence; use contemporary guidelines/local policy for actual dosing, monitoring targets, and reperfusion-era practice.
