Multisource surveillance study plus interim test-negative vaccine effectiveness analysis, serologic immunogenicity assessment, viral genetic/antigenic characterization, antiviral susceptibility surveillance, FluSurv-NET burden estimation, and VISION ED/UC and hospitalization VE estimates.

Clinical domain

Influenza epidemiology, acute care antiviral stewardship, vaccination counseling, inpatient risk stratification, and seasonal respiratory virus operations.

Scope warning

This is not a randomized trial and does not create new drug dosing guidance. It supports continuation of current vaccination and antiviral practices during drifted A(H3N2) circulation, but practice changes requiring dosing, formulary, or vaccine-policy decisions still require current CDC/ACIP guidance and local policy review.

Clinical bottom line

Verdict: Read first for acute care / ID stewardship context; do not use as a stand-alone guideline.
During the 2025–2026 US season, antigenically drifted influenza A(H3N2) subclade K predominated, vaccine effectiveness was modest but statistically protective, and tested A(H3N2) viruses remained susceptible to recommended antivirals. The practical action is not to abandon vaccination or antivirals; it is to tighten prompt treatment workflows for hospitalized, severe, progressive, or high-risk patients and to maintain renal-dose and interaction safeguards.
Most actionable result

Adjusted interim VE was 35% against influenza-associated ED/UC encounters and 27% against influenza-associated hospitalizations for any influenza. This is modest but clinically meaningful at population scale.

Most pharmacy-relevant result

All 1,729 tested A(H3N2) viruses, including subclade K viruses, were susceptible to oseltamivir, peramivir, zanamivir, and baloxavir. Sporadic resistance signals were mainly in A(H1N1)pdm09 and a small number of B/Victoria isolates.

Most important caution

VISION did not systematically subtype influenza A, so the article does not provide a direct subclade-K-specific VE estimate. The serology substudy used 60 healthy vaccine responders, limiting generalizability.

Implementation focus

Order sets should preserve empiric oseltamivir prompts for hospitalized/severe/high-risk suspected influenza, dose-adjust oseltamivir/peramivir for renal impairment, and flag zanamivir in asthma/COPD and baloxavir with polyvalent cations.

Population, exclusions, and applicability

Included populations and data streams

  • Clinical laboratory surveillance: approximately 300 clinical laboratories reported diagnostic respiratory specimen testing across US jurisdictions.
  • Public health laboratory surveillance: approximately 100 public health laboratories submitted surveillance specimens for typing, subtyping, clade characterization, and selected antigenic and susceptibility testing.
  • FluSurv-NET: population-based laboratory-confirmed influenza hospitalization surveillance across counties in 14 states, covering approximately 10% of the US population.
  • VISION: EHR-based test-negative case-control VE estimates from ED/UC and hospitalized acute respiratory illness encounters.
  • Serology substudy: 20 children aged 3–8 years, 20 adults aged 18–49 years, and 20 adults aged 65 years or older who were healthy vaccine responders.

Exclusions and caveats that matter clinically

  • VISION excluded encounters with positive SARS-CoV-2 tests to reduce bias from correlated vaccination behaviors.
  • Vaccinated status required at least 1 dose of 2025–2026 influenza vaccine at least 14 days before the encounter index date.
  • VISION did not systematically subtype influenza A, so VE cannot be assigned specifically to A(H3N2) subclade K.
  • The antigenic characterization subset was purposively selected, not necessarily proportional to circulating viruses.
  • Serology participants were selected vaccine responders and may not represent frail older adults, immunocompromised patients, transplant recipients, or people with poor vaccine response.
  • Burden estimates depend on FluSurv-NET catchment data, testing practices, diagnostic sensitivity assumptions, and prior-season healthcare-seeking multipliers.
Evidence-insufficiency warning: The study is sufficient to support current vaccination and antiviral treatment policy, but insufficient to create new patient-level vaccine-effectiveness estimates, new antiviral dosing regimens, new resistance-treatment algorithms, or a new formulary preference.

Section-by-section synthesis

Abstract

The authors describe a 2025–2026 US influenza season dominated by antigenically drifted influenza A(H3N2) J.2.4.1 subclade K viruses. Using national virologic surveillance, serology, FluSurv-NET hospitalization data, burden models, and VISION VE estimates, they conclude that the season caused substantial morbidity and mortality but that vaccines and antivirals remained useful.

Key numbers: 90.9% of public-health-laboratory influenza-positive specimens were influenza A; 87.8% of subtyped influenza A viruses were A(H3N2); 92.7% of characterized A(H3N2) viruses were subclade K. The estimated cumulative influenza-associated hospitalization rate was 80.0 per 100,000, corresponding to 28–49 million illnesses, 360,000–740,000 hospitalizations, and 22,000–74,000 deaths. Adjusted interim VE was 35% for ED/UC encounters and 27% for hospitalizations.

Introduction

The introduction frames subclade K as a drifted A(H3N2) threat because it accumulated multiple hemagglutinin amino-acid changes near receptor and antibody-binding sites after vaccine-strain selection. These changes raised concern for vaccine immune escape and reduced effectiveness.

The authors position the US findings in a broader global context: subclade K rapidly emerged, displaced other viruses, and contributed to major influenza activity in Australia, Japan, and the Northern Hemisphere. The clinical question is whether vaccination and antivirals still mitigate disease under drift conditions.

Methods

The surveillance design combined virologic, immunologic, hospitalization, burden-estimation, and VE methods. Public health labs submitted influenza-positive specimens for sequencing and selected antigenic testing. Antiviral susceptibility used sequence analysis for resistance-associated substitutions and phenotypic assays in purposive subsets.

VE used a test-negative case-control design in VISION. Cases tested positive for influenza by molecular assay; controls tested negative. Models adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, calendar time, and site. VE was reported as 1 minus adjusted odds ratio, multiplied by 100. Encounters positive for SARS-CoV-2 were excluded.

Results: surveillance and viral characterization

From September 28, 2025 through March 14, 2026, clinical laboratories reported 1,939,806 specimens, 281,329 of which were influenza-positive. Public health labs tested 80,834 samples, with 55,318 influenza-positive; 50,291 were influenza A, and among subtyped A viruses, 35,801 were A(H3N2).

Genetic characterization showed A(H3N2) subclade K predominance: 1,626 of 1,754 characterized A(H3N2) viruses were subclade K. Antigenic characterization found only 4 of 196 tested A(H3N2) viruses were antigenically similar to the current-season A(H3N2) vaccine reference virus, supporting material antigenic drift.

Results: antiviral susceptibility and immunogenicity

The CDC assessed antiviral susceptibility of 2,790 viruses. Most viruses, including all A(H3N2) subclade K viruses, were susceptible to oseltamivir, peramivir, zanamivir, and baloxavir. Resistance or reduced inhibition was uncommon and described mainly among A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses with H275Y, I223V, or S247N substitutions and two B/Victoria viruses with reduced peramivir inhibition.

Postvaccination neutralizing antibody titers against a representative subclade K virus were reduced 1.62-fold compared with the vaccine virus across the three age groups. Still, all 20 children, 18 of 20 adults aged 18–49 years, and 18 of 20 adults aged 65 years or older had postvaccination neutralizing titers of at least 40 against subclade K.

Results: burden and vaccine effectiveness

FluSurv-NET identified 27,881 laboratory-confirmed influenza-associated hospitalizations. Adults aged 65 years or older accounted for 54.0% of hospitalizations and had the highest cumulative hospitalization rate, 261.5 per 100,000. Children younger than 5 years had the next highest rate, 85.4 per 100,000, with infants younger than 1 year at 131.7 per 100,000.

VISION included 135,029 ED/UC encounters and 33,629 hospitalizations with acute respiratory illness. VE against any influenza was 35% for ED/UC encounters and 27% for hospitalizations. For influenza A, VE against ED/UC encounters was 37% in children and 33% in adults; against hospitalization it was 47% in children and 26% in adults. There were insufficient cases to estimate VE against influenza B hospitalization.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusions

The authors interpret the season as moderate severity overall, high severity for children, and dominated by drifted A(H3N2) subclade K. They argue that lower VE is biologically plausible because of antigenic drift, but the vaccine still reduced serious influenza outcomes. They also emphasize that vaccination can still protect against severe disease and other circulating vaccine-represented viruses.

Limitations are clinically important: surveillance specimen selection may not be proportional to circulating viruses; the serology study may not represent all vaccine recipients; FluSurv-NET covers about 10% of the US population; burden models use assumptions about testing and healthcare seeking; VISION lacked systematic subtyping; and data were preliminary through March 2026. The conclusion supports continuing vaccination and antiviral recommendations, especially prompt treatment for hospitalized, severe/complicated, or high-risk patients.

Monitoring checklist for inpatient pharmacy

  • Document symptom onset, admission date, severity, pregnancy/postpartum status, age, immunocompromise, chronic conditions, and residence in long-term care.
  • Start oseltamivir promptly for hospitalized/severe/high-risk suspected influenza; do not delay for PCR confirmation.
  • Calculate renal function before selecting oseltamivir or peramivir dose; reassess with acute kidney injury or renal replacement therapy changes.
  • Assess enteral access and absorption; if no enteral route, discuss alternative strategy with ID/primary team rather than defaulting automatically to peramivir or baloxavir.
  • Monitor for secondary bacterial pneumonia, clinical deterioration, and persistent viral replication in immunocompromised hosts.
  • Check baloxavir-cation interactions and recent/near-future live attenuated influenza vaccine timing when relevant.

Safety signals and bedside risk interpretation

High pediatric seasonal severity

The season was moderate overall but high severity in children. Hospitalization rates were highest in older adults, then children younger than 5 years, with infants younger than 1 year at highest pediatric risk.

Severe inpatient course markers

Among FluSurv-NET hospitalizations with available detailed data, 14.7% were admitted to the ICU and 5.8% required mechanical ventilation.

Antiviral underuse signal

Preliminary hospitalized-patient data showed antiviral treatment was lower in children aged 0–17 years than adults. This is an actionable workflow gap.

Resistance signal

All tested A(H3N2) viruses were susceptible. Resistance/reduced inhibition was uncommon and mainly involved A(H1N1)pdm09 substitutions and two B/Victoria peramivir-reduced-inhibition isolates.

Do not misread vaccine drift as vaccine failure. Antigenic drift reduced antibody titers and likely contributed to lower VE, but VE remained statistically protective and postvaccination titers against subclade K were commonly still at or above the study's seropositivity threshold.

What should and should not change in practice

Should change or be reinforced
  • Reinforce annual influenza vaccination and counsel that drift reduces but does not eliminate benefit.
  • Prioritize prompt oseltamivir for hospitalized, severe/progressive, or high-risk suspected influenza.
  • Audit pediatric inpatient antiviral use and missed high-risk outpatient treatment opportunities.
  • Keep renal-dose decision support active for oseltamivir and peramivir.
  • Ensure baloxavir-cation and zanamivir-asthma/COPD alerts are visible if these agents are on formulary.
  • Maintain surveillance awareness for late-season influenza B as A activity declines.
Should not change based on this paper alone
  • Do not discontinue or de-emphasize influenza vaccination because VE is modest.
  • Do not switch default inpatient therapy away from oseltamivir solely because A(H3N2) subclade K predominated.
  • Do not routinely add baloxavir to neuraminidase inhibitor therapy in hospitalized patients.
  • Do not use peramivir as a routine inpatient substitute without a specific rationale.
  • Do not infer that a vaccinated patient cannot have influenza.
  • Do not create new renal/hepatic dosing rules from this surveillance study.
