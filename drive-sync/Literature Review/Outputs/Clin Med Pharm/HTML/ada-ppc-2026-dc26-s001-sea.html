One-page synthesis

Core thesis: Improving diabetes outcomes requires a coordinated population-health system that combines evidence-based treatment, person-centered team care, measurement and QI, affordable access, social-context assessment, and community support.

The chapter broadens diabetes quality beyond A1C and medication choice. It argues that care systems should use registries, decision support, proactive planning, equity-stratified quality data, interprofessional teams, and community linkages while explicitly incorporating treatment burden, cost, social determinants, and patient preferences. The strongest operational contribution is not a single effect estimate but a coherent implementation architecture spanning clinic, system, payer, and community levels.

The evidence summary supports team-based care, Chronic Care Model interventions, telehealth/mHealth, and community-oriented support with generally modest improvements in glycemia and cardiometabolic risk factors. At the same time, the chapter repeatedly shows that access and digital infrastructure shape whether interventions work. U.S.-specific insurance, affordability, and workforce data limit direct transfer of some policy claims to other settings.

Section condensation

Systems to Support Diabetes Population Health

Recommendations 1.1–1.5 frame diabetes care as a health-system responsibility: timely evidence-based decisions, person-centered shared decision-making, team-based models, registries and decision support, reliable quality metrics, and continuous quality improvement. The recommendations explicitly include treatment burden and cost, not only biomedical targets.

The chapter positions the Chronic Care Model as a core organizing framework. Its six elements include delivery-system design, self-management support, decision support, clinical information systems, community resources/policies, and health systems. Randomized CCM interventions are summarized as producing a modest but statistically supported A1C improvement, with better results in higher-baseline-A1C populations and interventions using at least four CCM elements.

State of Diabetes and Diabetes Care

The chapter documents a large U.S. burden of diabetes, prediabetes, and obesity and emphasizes persistent quality gaps. It reports 38.4 million people with diabetes in 2021, 97.6 million adults with prediabetes, and limited simultaneous attainment of A1C, blood-pressure, and lipid goals in 2015–2018.

The economic burden is also substantial: the 2022 annual cost of diagnosed diabetes is reported at at least $413 billion. The chapter uses these epidemiologic and cost estimates to justify population-health approaches rather than relying on encounter-level treatment alone.

Evidence-Based Care Delivery and Payment Models

The chapter describes evidence-based care delivery as person-centered, team-based, proactive, registry-supported, and linked to community resources. It notes that payment design, specialist access, and coordinated multidisciplinary programs can influence quality and outcomes.

The evidence is heterogeneous across programs and settings. The chapter specifically cautions that inconsistent findings may arise from variation in interventions, evaluation strategies, structural barriers, and community needs; this is important when translating pooled effects into a local care model.

Flexible Modalities of Care Delivery: Telehealth and Behavioral Support

Telehealth is presented as a complement—not a replacement—for in-person care. The chapter summarizes multiple recent syntheses showing modest A1C improvement across telehealth, type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes with hypertension, and Black/Hispanic populations; it also reports blood-pressure improvement in the combined diabetes/hypertension evidence.

Implementation context is load-bearing. Infrastructure, device/data access, digital literacy, CHW support, accessibility, affordability, connectivity, trust, and contextual tailoring can determine whether telehealth reduces or worsens disparities. The same section reports modest A1C benefits from mHealth-delivered DSME/DSMES and self-management support.

Strategies for System-Level Improvement

The chapter supports coordinated interprofessional care, benchmarking, population registries, and QI. A meta-analysis of 35 team-based interventions is summarized as improving A1C, blood pressure, LDL, and total cholesterol; CINEMA is offered as an example of a multidisciplinary program improving weight, glycemia, lipids, and estimated ASCVD risk.

Table 1.1 operationalizes team composition across the life span and specific complexity states. Of particular pharmacy relevance, it explicitly names clinical pharmacists for people with polypharmacy or complex medication plans and again within older-adult care teams.

Access to Care and Cost Considerations

The chapter separates insurance coverage from true affordability. It reports low uninsured rates in some age groups but substantial exposure to high-deductible plans and describes delayed or reduced care when out-of-pocket burden rises.

Insulin affordability is treated as a safety and adherence issue. A cited 2021 national survey found insulin rationing in 18.6% of adults with type 1 diabetes and 15.8% of insulin-treated adults with type 2 diabetes. The chapter also summarizes Medicare insulin cost caps and broader affordability initiatives.

Tailoring Treatment for Social Context

Recommendations 1.6–1.9 call for equity stratification of quality data, routine SDOH assessment, referral to community resources, lay/community self-management support, and CHW involvement. Subsequent sections address food insecurity, housing insecurity, migrant and seasonal agricultural work, language barriers, health literacy/numeracy, and social/community support.

The operational message is that treatment plans should be feasible within a person’s material and social context. Screening is not presented as an endpoint: the chapter repeatedly links identification of barriers to referral, navigation, tailored communication, and community-based support.

Summary

The source closes by arguing that diabetes population health depends on collaboration across people with diabetes, clinical teams, health systems, community partners, and policy/social resources.

The chapter is best used as a systems-and-equity implementation reference. It should not be treated as a primary evidence source for the underlying effect estimates; those estimates require direct review of the cited studies or syntheses when used for evidence claims.
