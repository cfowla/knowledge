One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In non-diabetic, non-obese adults with HIV aged ≥50 years receiving contemporary ART, metformin produced a modest divergence in weight trajectory over 96 weeks compared with placebo; the separation diminished after metformin was stopped. The treatment-associated rise in GDF-15 correlated inversely with weight change, supporting but not proving a mechanistic link.

No metformin-treated participant reached the prespecified ≥3% or ≥5% weight-gain thresholds by week 96, compared with 38.9% and 11.1%, respectively, with placebo. Its clinical strength is limited by the small sample, failure to reach the planned pragmatic enrollment target (40 randomized vs 60 planned), per-protocol efficacy analysis, secondary/exploratory outcome status, single-center population, demographic narrowness, and imbalance in ART regimens that also changed metformin dose exposure.

Section condensation

Abstract

The manuscript reports a prespecified metabolic secondary analysis of METFORAGING. Forty participants were randomized; 35 remained on assigned treatment through week 96. Metformin produced a statistically significant between-group difference in weight change at week 96 (−2.8 kg) and weight moved back toward baseline after treatment discontinuation. GDF-15 change correlated inversely with weight change in the metformin group.

The authors conclude that metformin attenuated progressive weight gain in this selected HIV population but that the effect was not sustained after discontinuation. They frame the results as justification for larger trials rather than definitive practice-changing evidence.

Introduction

The rationale is contemporary ART-associated weight gain, particularly with TAF and INSTI-based regimens, and the limited efficacy of ART switching for reversing weight gain. The authors position metformin as inexpensive, widely available, and plausibly weight-modifying through appetite/energy-intake pathways including GDF-15.

The claimed gap is specific: prior metformin data in HIV focused on metabolic disease, lipodystrophy, prediabetes, or shorter treatment; METFORAGING allows examination of prolonged metformin exposure in metabolically normal, non-obese older PWH receiving contemporary ART.

Methods

METFORAGING was a single-center, double-blind, randomized, parallel-group placebo-controlled pilot trial. The parent trial targeted biological ageing biomarkers; this manuscript analyzes prespecified secondary and exploratory metabolic outcomes. Enrollment ran March-October 2022. Participants were followed to week 144. Randomization used central computer allocation; participants, investigators, and outcome assessors were masked.

Weight and BMI were tracked longitudinally; fasting metabolic laboratory measures were obtained at baseline, week 96, and week 144; GDF-15 was measured at baseline and weeks 48, 96, and 144. Efficacy analyses used the per-protocol population. Linear regression estimated week-96 changes, mixed-effects models evaluated trajectories, ANCOVA addressed other metabolic measures, and Pearson correlation tested GDF-15/weight associations. No formal sample-size calculation was performed; a pragmatic target of 60 was planned. No imputation was used.

Results

Fifty-five people were screened and 40 randomized (19 metformin, 21 placebo); recruitment stopped below the planned 60 because of slow enrollment. Five discontinued before week 96, leaving 35 in the per-protocol analysis. Baseline medians were similar for age (~56 years), BMI (~24.4 kg/m²), metabolic status, and immune status, but INSTI composition differed: dolutegravir 57.9% vs 76.2% and bictegravir 31.6% vs 9.5% in metformin versus placebo, respectively.

At week 96, mean weight change was −1.5 kg with metformin and +1.3 kg with placebo; the between-group difference was −2.8 kg (95% CI −5.2 to −0.4; p=.025). BMI difference was −0.9 kg/m² (95% CI −1.7 to −0.1; p=.022). After discontinuation, prior metformin recipients gained more weight from week 96 to 144 (+1.9 vs −0.3 kg; difference 2.2 kg, 95% CI 0.05 to 4.5; p=.045). GDF-15 increased with metformin and its week-96 change inversely correlated with weight change (r=−0.67; p=.003). Other glycaemic, lipid, and anthropometric secondary measures were reported as not significantly different between groups.

Discussion and limitations

The authors interpret the findings as an exposure-dependent suppression of weight gain, consistent with prior shorter metformin studies and with a potential GDF-15 pathway. They emphasize the absence of glycaemic change as support for a weight-regulating effect that is not simply mediated by improved glycaemic control.

The manuscript explicitly acknowledges the small sample, inability to explore heterogeneity, predominantly White/non-obese population, single-center design, secondary outcome status, lack of body-composition imaging, and ART-regimen imbalance. A particularly important complication is that dolutegravir recipients received a lower metformin dose, so the between-arm ART imbalance also implies heterogeneous metformin exposure.

Validity, safety, and practice translation

Internal validity - strengths

  • Randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind design with masked investigators and outcome assessors.
  • Long intervention duration (96 weeks) plus 48-week off-treatment observation.
  • Multiple convergent weight analyses: continuous change, mixed-effects trajectories, BMI, categorical thresholds.
  • Prospectively defined secondary/exploratory metabolic assessments and biologically coherent GDF-15 time course.
  • High retention through week 96 among randomized participants (35/40, 87.5%).

Threats / uncertainty

  • Pilot sample was not formally powered for metabolic efficacy and missed the pragmatic target (40 vs 60 randomized).
  • Efficacy analysis was per-protocol rather than intention-to-treat, which can weaken preservation of randomization.
  • Weight was not the parent trial primary endpoint; multiple secondary/exploratory tests increase false-positive risk when interpreted at nominal p<.05.
  • ART imbalance (DTG vs BIC) is clinically relevant because metformin dose was reduced with DTG.
  • No body-composition imaging; weight difference cannot be attributed specifically to fat mass.

Safety

Adverse-event frequency was reported as similar between arms. Diarrhoea occurred in 4 metformin participants (21%); one required dose adjustment. No participant stopped treatment because of a metformin-related adverse event, and no serious adverse event was attributed to metformin. Laboratory safety parameters and virologic suppression remained stable according to the manuscript.

Practice translation: This trial is not sufficient to support routine off-label metformin solely to prevent ART-associated weight gain in metabolically normal, non-obese PWH. The absolute effect was modest, the sample small and selected, and benefit was not durable after discontinuation. The study is better used to justify confirmatory trials and to inform hypothesis generation around inexpensive long-term weight-management strategies.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read soon. The paper has a clean randomized signal, long exposure window, and an unusually informative discontinuation phase, but its metabolic conclusions remain pilot-level. It is worth preserving for HIV pharmacotherapy/weight-management literature, not as a practice standard.
Relevance
8/10

Directly addresses a common contemporary ART complication with a familiar, inexpensive drug.

Evidence basis: Randomized comparison of a clinically recognizable intervention and outcome.

Principal limit: Population is restricted to older, non-obese, metabolically normal PWH.

Would increase: Replication in broader contemporary HIV cohorts with patient-centered metabolic outcomes.

Novelty
8/10

Long-duration metformin exposure and post-discontinuation follow-up in metabolically normal PWH are distinctive.

Evidence basis: 96-week intervention, 48-week withdrawal observation, GDF-15 kinetics.

Principal limit: Builds on prior shorter metformin and non-HIV GDF-15 work rather than introducing a wholly new mechanism.

Would increase: Independent long-term trials designed primarily around ART-associated weight management.

Method strength
6/10

Randomization, blinding, placebo control, and repeated measures are strong, but the trial is small and the efficacy analysis is per-protocol.

Evidence basis: Central allocation, masking, longitudinal measurements, mixed-effects models.

Principal limit: Under-recruitment, no formal power calculation, per-protocol efficacy population, secondary outcomes.

Would increase: Adequately powered multicenter ITT trial with prespecified multiplicity handling and body-composition measures.

Evidence strength
5/10

The direction is coherent across outcomes, but precision and confirmatory status are limited.

Evidence basis: Week-96 weight/BMI differences with CIs; longitudinal interaction; categorical thresholds; biomarker correlation.

Principal limit: Small n, nominal p-values near .05 for several key endpoints, exploratory biomarker analysis, unavailable supplements.

Would increase: Larger confirmatory trial reproducing effect size and durability with prespecified primary weight outcome.

External validity
4/10

The selected single-center cohort is substantially narrower than the broader population experiencing ART-associated weight gain.

Evidence basis: Median age ~56; 87.5% White overall; BMI <30; no prediabetes/diabetes/insulin resistance.

Principal limit: Single Madrid center; metabolically normal/non-obese; limited ethnic diversity; older age restriction.

Would increase: Multicenter enrollment including overweight/obesity, diverse demographics, different ART backbones, and younger adults.

Implementation value
5/10

The regimen is simple and inexpensive, but evidence is insufficient for routine clinical adoption.

Evidence basis: Explicit dosing, DTG interaction adjustment, tolerability, withdrawal effect.

Principal limit: No demonstrated long-term patient-centered benefit; effect reverses after discontinuation; no guideline-level validation.

Would increase: Effectiveness/safety data in target clinical populations and evidence defining who benefits and for how long.

Bottom-line confidence

Moderate. The manuscript is sufficiently complete to appraise design, major quantitative findings, and main figures/table. Confidence is reduced because supplementary Figure 1 and Tables S1-S2 were not supplied, and several detailed methods are referenced to the previously published primary METFORAGING analysis rather than fully reproduced here.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Use this paper forEvidence that metformin may modestly suppress weight gain during active exposure in selected PWH; designing larger trials; understanding the potential GDF-15 signal.
Do not use it forRoutine prescribing recommendations, estimating benefit in obesity or prediabetes, or assuming durable weight control after stopping metformin.
Clinical detail to retainDTG recipients stayed at metformin 850 mg once daily, while other participants could escalate to 850 mg twice daily; ART imbalance therefore overlaps with metformin-exposure differences.
Read nextThe parent METFORAGING primary analysis for full trial procedures and the unavailable supplementary material if exact secondary metabolic estimates are needed.
Most reusable finding: the treatment-on / treatment-off pattern. Weight separation developed during metformin exposure and then moved back toward baseline after withdrawal, while GDF-15 showed a parallel exposure-linked rise and decline. This strengthens temporal coherence while still falling short of proving a causal GDF-15 mediation mechanism.
