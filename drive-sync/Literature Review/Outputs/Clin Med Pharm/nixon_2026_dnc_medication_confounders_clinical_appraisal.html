Bottom line for practice

Verdict: Read first for pharmacy-driven DNC protocol improvement; do not use as a standalone practice-changing clinical standard. Nixon et al. show that institutional DNC protocols commonly require medication confounder review but vary substantially in the operational details that matter to pharmacists: who reviews medications, how altered pharmacokinetics are handled, whether ancillary testing can be used when drugs remain uncertain, whether reversal agents are addressed, and whether drug appendices exist.

Practice implication: This article supports local protocol work: mandate or strongly trigger pharmacist involvement for medication confounder review, build a standardized DNC medication-confounder checklist, include patient-specific PK modifiers, specify when serial concentrations or toxicology consultation are needed, and explicitly state that ancillary testing should not be used to bypass unresolved drug effects. Evidence is insufficient to change bedside DNC criteria, define universal drug waiting times, endorse routine reversal-agent use, or replace local legal/policy requirements.

One-page synthesis

Core thesis

Most institutions acknowledge medications as DNC confounders, but protocols often stop at broad requirements such as excluding intoxication or waiting a specified number of half-lives. They frequently do not specify a responsible medication reviewer, a structured pharmacist workflow, how to adjust for organ dysfunction/hypothermia/obesity/prolonged infusion, or what to do when clearance remains uncertain.

Most useful finding for pharmacists

The clearest actionable gap is role definition: 78% of reviewed protocols did not designate a specific medication reviewer, while pharmacist involvement was mentioned in only 39% and usually as consultative rather than mandatory. That is a high-yield policy target for critical care and neurocritical care pharmacy services.

Clinical-practice risk

Residual CNS depressant or neuromuscular blocker effects can mimic components of DNC. A protocol that allows a five-half-life shortcut without context, relies on nonspecific toxicology screening, or leaves ancillary testing language ambiguous can create risk of unsafe or delayed evaluation.

What this should trigger locally

Review the institution's DNC policy for explicit medication review responsibility, pharmacist consult triggers, required data elements, handling of renal/hepatic dysfunction and hypothermia, serum concentration strategy, NMBA assessment, overdose/toxicology escalation, documentation standards, and ancillary-test restrictions.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the problem: DNC requires a reliable clinical exam, and medication confounders must be absent or eliminated, but guidelines do not fully specify how institutions should assess or manage these confounders. The authors requested exact protocol language from prior survey respondents and analyzed protocol excerpts related to medication confounders, ancillary testing, reversal agents, practitioner responsibilities, and drug appendices.

The main result is broad protocol variability across 18 U.S. institutions. Protocols commonly relied on half-life calculations, drug levels, and toxicology screening; ancillary testing was usually reserved for cases where clinical examination could not be completed; reversal agents were rarely addressed; pharmacist roles were inconsistently specified; and drug appendices were often absent. The conclusion calls for clearer evidence-based protocols.

Background

The background explains that DNC is a clinical determination when no confounding condition compromises exam reliability. Medication confounders include sedatives, neuromuscular blockers, and drugs taken in overdose, and the authors emphasize that some medications can mimic brain death criteria.

The clinical gap is operational rather than conceptual. Existing consensus guidance requires excluding intoxication and pharmacologic paralysis but gives limited detail beyond selected examples such as pentobarbital concentration, leaving institutions to implement medication review with variable specificity. This creates a natural role for critical care pharmacists.

Methods

The study followed a prior Neurocritical Care Society survey. Of 69 prior respondents, 23 agreed to be contacted, and 18 institutions provided protocol language. Respondents were asked for exact wording on CNS-depressant/medication confounders, ancillary testing, reversal agents, required practitioner type, additional medication-confounder practices, and drug appendices.

Institutional identifiers were removed. Responses were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis assisted by an LLM, with a standardized prompt asking for the top three themes by occurrence for each question. Investigators then reviewed and adjudicated AI-generated themes for accuracy and fidelity to the original protocol language. This supports structured theme extraction but remains limited by sample size, respondent selection, and reliance on written protocols rather than actual practice.

Results: demographics and overview

The sample consisted of 18 U.S. institutions. Most were urban academic medical centers; all had neurology or neurocritical care consult services and neurocritical care units. This is a high-resource sample, not a representative cross-section of all hospitals performing DNC determinations.

The main themes were reliance on pharmacokinetic clearance strategies, conditional use of ancillary testing, minimal reversal-agent integration, unclear responsibility for medication review, recognition of patient-specific PK complexity, and inconsistent drug appendices.

Results: medication confounder assessment

Fourteen of 18 institutions explicitly referenced time- or level-based clearance requirements, commonly using five half-lives or drug levels below a therapeutic range. Serum concentrations were used as confirmatory tools when available, especially for barbiturates and alcohol; cited thresholds included pentobarbital <=5-10 mcg/mL and blood alcohol <80 mg/dL.

All 18 institutions referenced toxicology screening and alcohol thresholds, but the article highlights that simplified pharmacokinetic thresholds are often used despite recognized complexity in critically ill patients. Protocols frequently mention prolonged clearance from organ dysfunction or hypothermia without operational instructions for what to do.

Results: ancillary testing

Sixteen institutions restricted ancillary testing to incomplete or uninterpretable clinical exams or apnea testing. Eleven emphasized that ancillary testing should not bypass unresolved medication clearance concerns. Where modality preference was specified, cerebral perfusion-based approaches were favored; EEG, CTA, or MRA were discouraged or omitted in many protocols.

The implementation concern is that 7 of 18 institutions did not explicitly restrict ancillary testing in the setting of potential medication confounders. That ambiguity matters because ancillary testing should not be treated as a shortcut around unresolved drug effects.

Results: reversal agents

Reversal agents were absent from most protocols: 16 of 18 did not reference routine reversal-agent use. When reversal was mentioned, it was framed as optional and case-based after risk-benefit assessment rather than part of a standardized DNC algorithm.

This supports a conservative approach: physiologic clearance and reliable exclusion of drug effect dominate current protocol language. It does not establish that reversal agents should or should not be used in a specific patient; rather, it shows that institutions have not standardized this part of the workflow.

Results: practitioner responsibility and pharmacist role

Fourteen protocols did not designate a specific medication reviewer. Many emphasized credentialed physicians for DNC testing but did not assign responsibility for medication assessment. Seven responses acknowledged pharmacist involvement, typically as consultative and triggered by uncertainty rather than as a required step.

For pharmacy practice, this is the central operational gap. Medication confounder review requires pharmacokinetic interpretation, infusion history, drug-level interpretation, organ dysfunction assessment, toxicology limitations, and documentation. The study supports formalizing pharmacist involvement but does not prove outcome improvement from doing so.

Results: patient-specific considerations and appendices

Fifteen institutions emphasized individualized PK assessment, including organ dysfunction, hypothermia, obesity, and drug interactions. However, few protocols gave specific instructions for uncertain clearance, such as serial concentrations, escalation pathways, or standardized consult workflows.

Fourteen institutions lacked a dedicated drug appendix. Where appendices existed, they were typically based on existing half-life tables and clearance modifiers. This is an opportunity for pharmacy-led protocol resources, but appendices must avoid false precision and should state when half-life estimates are unreliable.

Discussion and conclusion

The authors interpret the findings as evidence that institutions broadly adhere to the principle of excluding medication confounders but vary in implementation. They specifically highlight gaps in medication review responsibility, patient-specific PK interpretation, ancillary testing language, reversal-agent guidance, and supporting drug resources.

The stated conclusion is that standardized, evidence-based protocols are needed to ensure reliable DNC determinations in the presence of medication confounders. The strongest practice translation is local protocol quality improvement; the evidence does not support universal bedside rules beyond guideline-aligned principles.

Clinical practice translation

Insufficient evidence for clinical action: Do not change bedside DNC determination criteria based on this article alone. This study does not validate a universal waiting period, a universal drug appendix, a specific serum threshold for most sedatives, or routine use of reversal agents.

What should change

  • Audit local DNC policy for explicit medication-confounder language.
  • Add required or default pharmacist involvement when medication confounders are present or uncertain.
  • Require documentation of medication exposure history, last dose/infusion stop time, renal/hepatic status, temperature history, obesity, interacting drugs, and available levels.
  • Define escalation to toxicology/poison center for overdose, unknown ingestion, unreliable history, prolonged coma, or discordant levels.
  • Specify that ancillary testing is not a workaround for unresolved drug effect unless local policy and current guideline criteria support the exact scenario.

What should not change

  • Do not declare DNC while intoxication, sedative effect, or pharmacologic paralysis remains unresolved.
  • Do not rely on five half-lives as sufficient in overdose, hypothermia, organ failure, prolonged infusion, morbid obesity, saturable kinetics, or interacting drugs.
  • Do not treat a negative routine urine drug screen as proof that intoxication is absent.
  • Do not adopt routine naloxone or flumazenil use as part of DNC testing based on this study.
  • Do not substitute this protocol review for local legal, ethics, neurologic, and critical care policy review.

Pharmacy workflow opportunity

  • Create an EHR note template for DNC medication-confounder review.
  • Build an on-call pharmacist checklist for overnight DNC evaluations.
  • Link drug appendices to institutional assays, send-out turnaround times, and local toxicology contacts.
  • Require second review for complex sedation, targeted temperature management, overdose, hepatic/renal failure, ECMO, CRRT, or high-dose/prolonged infusions.

Safety signals, implementation constraints, and failure modes

Diagnostic safety

Residual CNS depressant or NMBA effect can mimic DNC findings. The catastrophic failure mode is a false-positive DNC determination when drug effect has not been excluded.

Operational safety

Overly conservative or unclear medication review can delay DNC evaluation, family counseling, ICU throughput, and organ donation processes. Standardization should reduce uncertainty without creating unsafe shortcuts.

Reversal-agent safety

Reversal may destabilize selected patients or obscure interpretation if used without a clear question and documentation plan. The source supports case-based caution, not routine reversal.

Implementation constraints

  • Assay availability: Many sedatives and intoxicants lack rapid quantitative assays; send-out levels may not be clinically useful in time-sensitive decisions.
  • Documentation burden: Medication review requires MAR reconstruction, infusion stop times, transfer records, prehospital/ED exposure, home medications, and possible co-ingestions.
  • Coverage: Overnight and community settings may lack neurocritical care pharmacists or toxicologists. Protocols should specify escalation through poison center or regional resources.
  • Local law and policy: DNC determination is legally and institutionally governed. Pharmacy workflow changes must be reviewed with neurology, critical care, ethics, legal/risk, nursing, respiratory therapy, and organ donation partners.
  • False precision: A half-life table can improve consistency but may be dangerous if it implies certainty in overdose, hypothermia, shock, hepatic/renal failure, or prolonged infusion.
