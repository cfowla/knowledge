One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Aspergillosis management should combine host-risk assessment, early high-resolution imaging and targeted microbiologic diagnosis, prompt mold-active therapy when invasive pulmonary aspergillosis is strongly suspected, attention to antifungal pharmacokinetics and interactions, and syndrome-specific use of surgery, prophylaxis, or long-term suppression. The guideline organizes this approach into 98 numbered recommendations using GRADE.
Most durable clinical contributionThe guideline integrates diagnosis, pharmacology, treatment, prophylaxis, chronic pulmonary disease, and allergic syndromes in one framework rather than treating aspergillosis as only an invasive mold infection.
Highest-value treatment messageVoriconazole is the 2016 preferred primary treatment for IPA; isavuconazole and liposomal amphotericin B are alternatives. Echinocandin monotherapy is not routine primary therapy.
Highest-value diagnostic messageDiagnosis is multimodal: CT, bronchoscopy/BAL when feasible, culture/histopathology, galactomannan in the right populations, and contextual interpretation of PCR and β-D-glucan.
Evidence-to-action strength. Several pivotal areas have direct comparative or randomized evidence (voriconazole primary therapy, isavuconazole noninferiority, selected prophylaxis trials, portions of TDM), but many organ-specific, adjunctive, chronic, and salvage recommendations rely on lower-quality evidence and expert synthesis.
Main limitation for 2026 use. The evidence search primarily covered January 2008 through December 2014. IDSA has since issued a 2026 update specifically addressing prevention of invasive aspergillosis in adult solid-organ transplant recipients, so the 2016 transplant prophylaxis section is no longer a sufficient current source on its own.

Methods / guideline construction

Clinical questions and panel process

The panel organized the guideline around seven broad clinical questions spanning exposure prevention, diagnosis, antifungal pharmacology, invasive disease treatment, prophylaxis, empiric/preemptive treatment, and chronic/allergic disease. Seventeen experts reviewed assigned topics, discussed recommendations during one face-to-face meeting and a series of conference calls over roughly 10 months, resolved discrepancies by discussion, and reviewed the final sections as a group.

The recommendations use GRADE, separating strength (strong/weak) from quality of evidence (very low/low/moderate/high). This distinction matters because the guideline contains strong recommendations even when direct evidence is limited, reflecting judgments about benefit-harm balance, feasibility, and clinical stakes rather than evidence quality alone.

Evidence search

Librarians developed PICO-based PubMed searches from panel-generated keywords. Searches were restricted to English-language publications from January 2008 through December 2014; conference abstracts from the prior two years were also considered. Relevant systematic reviews were identified through PubMed and the Cochrane Library. Each topic author reviewed the literature for the assigned section and drafted recommendations and evidence summaries for full-panel review.

Review, funding, and conflicts

Two external peer reviewers provided feedback; PIDS endorsed the guideline; the IDSA Standards and Practice Guideline Committee and IDSA Board approved it. IDSA funded guideline development. Section-by-section condensation

I. Epidemiology and risk reduction — Recommendations 1–5

Highest-risk groups include prolonged neutropenia, allogeneic HSCT, SOT, corticosteroid exposure, CGD, and other major immune defects. The guideline recommends protected environments for hospitalized allogeneic HSCT recipients and extends reasonable mold-exposure precautions to similarly high-risk patients. Outpatients at high risk are advised to avoid intense environmental mold exposures such as gardening, mulch/compost, and construction.

Centers caring for leukemia and transplant populations should track invasive mold infections and investigate unexpected increases or cases outside expected risk groups. Routine environmental sampling without an outbreak is not supported; targeted environmental evaluation becomes more relevant when nosocomial transmission is suspected.

II. Diagnosis of invasive aspergillosis — Recommendations 6–16

The diagnostic strategy is deliberately multimodal. Tissue/fluid specimens should support both histopathology/cytology and culture. PCR was considered promising but insufficiently standardized for routine standalone use in 2016; if used, assay-specific performance and clinical context matter. Serum/BAL galactomannan is most useful in hematologic malignancy and HSCT populations, while serum screening performs poorly in several other groups and during mold-active prophylaxis. β-D-glucan can support diagnosis in selected high-risk patients but is not Aspergillus-specific.

Chest CT is recommended whenever IPA is suspected, even with a normal chest radiograph. Routine contrast is unnecessary unless vascular proximity changes risk assessment. Repeat CT is generally deferred until at least two weeks unless the patient deteriorates. Bronchoscopy with BAL is recommended when feasible, with culture, cytology, fungal stains and nonculture tests; peripheral nodules may require percutaneous or endobronchial biopsy because BAL yield is lower.

III. Antifungal agents, TDM, combination therapy, susceptibility — Recommendations 17–24

Lipid amphotericin formulations are positioned as important alternatives when azoles cannot be used; amphotericin B deoxycholate is discouraged when safer alternatives are available. Echinocandins are primarily salvage agents rather than routine primary monotherapy for IA. Triazoles are the preferred therapeutic/preventive class for most patients, with extensive attention to drug interactions, formulation differences, nonlinear or variable pharmacokinetics, organ dysfunction, and toxicity.

TDM is recommended for triazole-based IA therapy and situations with prolonged exposure or important interactions, particularly for itraconazole, voriconazole and the posaconazole formulations supported by 2016 data. Routine susceptibility testing of every initial isolate is not recommended; testing is emphasized when azole resistance is suspected, treatment response is inadequate, or epidemiologic investigation requires it.

IV. Invasive syndromes — Recommendations 25–65

Voriconazole is the preferred 2016 primary treatment for IPA. Isavuconazole and liposomal amphotericin B are alternatives; echinocandin monotherapy is not routine primary therapy. Treatment should begin early when IPA is strongly suspected, usually continue at least 6–12 weeks, and be individualized to immune recovery, disease site and trajectory. Combination voriconazole plus an echinocandin can be considered in selected documented/severe disease but is not a default for all patients.

The section extends beyond pulmonary disease: reduction of immunosuppression when feasible, selective surgery, salvage strategies, biomarker monitoring, pediatric dosing differences, tracheobronchial disease, CNS infection, endophthalmitis, sinus disease, endocarditis, osteoarticular disease, skin/soft tissue disease, peritonitis, gastrointestinal/hepatic/renal disease, otologic disease, keratitis and Aspergillus bronchitis. Evidence strength varies sharply across these syndromes; many organ-specific recommendations are supported by observational data, case series, pathophysiologic rationale or expert consensus rather than large trials.

V. Prophylaxis and breakthrough infection — Recommendations 66–73

The 2016 document recommends mold-active prophylaxis for selected high-risk neutropenic and GVHD populations, with posaconazole receiving the strongest evidence support in several hematologic settings. It also provides lung-transplant and other SOT strategies based on transplant type, colonization and institutional epidemiology. Breakthrough disease should trigger aggressive diagnostic reassessment, azole-level review when applicable, susceptibility testing when an isolate is available, and typically a change to another antifungal class.

Currency boundary: adult SOT prophylaxis has been specifically revisited by IDSA in 2026; see the currency section below before applying Recommendations 69–72 to present practice.

VI. Empiric and preemptive strategies — Recommendations 74–80

Empiric antifungal treatment is recommended for persistently febrile high-risk patients with prolonged neutropenia despite broad-spectrum antibiotics; it is not recommended solely on the basis of fever when neutropenia is expected to be short (<10 days) and no other findings suggest IFI. Biomarker-driven/preemptive approaches can reduce unnecessary antifungal exposure without compromising survival in studied high-risk populations.

Suspected IPA still warrants early active treatment while the diagnostic evaluation proceeds. The guideline also distinguishes airway colonization from disease in lung transplant recipients, using time from transplant, recent rejection therapy, clinical findings and radiographic/bronchoscopic evidence to decide when treatment may or may not be warranted.

VII. Chronic, saprophytic and allergic disease — Recommendations 81–98

Chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA) requires a chronic course (≥3 months), characteristic progressive cavitary/radiographic abnormalities, Aspergillus IgG or other microbiologic evidence, and little or no overt immunocompromise, usually on a background of structural lung disease. Stable minimally symptomatic patients may be observed; symptomatic or progressive disease generally warrants at least six months of antifungal therapy, commonly itraconazole or voriconazole, with longer or lifelong suppression for some progressive cases. Hemoptysis management can include tranexamic acid, bronchial artery embolization, antifungal therapy and selected surgery.

A stable asymptomatic single aspergilloma can be observed, whereas significant symptoms—especially hemoptysis—support resection when feasible. ABPA diagnosis relies on Aspergillus-specific and total IgE in the appropriate clinical context; azoles are used as steroid-sparing therapy in selected symptomatic asthma/CF patients with TDM. Allergic fungal rhinosinusitis is managed principally with surgery and topical steroids, reserving systemic mold-active azoles for refractory or rapidly relapsing disease.

Future directions

The panel identifies persistent gaps in earlier diagnosis, optimal use of existing diagnostics, treatment of breakthrough/refractory disease, combination therapy, response assessment and selection of patients who benefit most from prophylaxis. The source also emphasizes that mortality remains substantial despite better antifungal options.

Figure 1 — GRADE framework PDF p.2 / e2

Purpose: show how evidence quality and recommendation strength are determined.
Flow:
1) Establish initial confidence from study design (randomized trials start higher; observational studies lower).
2) Lower or raise confidence for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias, large effect, dose response, and plausible confounding.
3) Arrive at final evidence-quality rating: high / moderate / low / very low.
Recommendation strength is then determined separately from evidence quality using certainty, benefit-harm balance, patient values/preferences, resources/cost, and implementation implications.
Key interpretation: a strong recommendation does not necessarily mean the evidence itself is high quality.

Pharmacotherapy-focused reading

Voriconazole2016 first-line IPA therapy. Nonlinear adult PK, CYP2C19/CYP3A interactions, substantial interpatient variability, and exposure-related neurotoxicity make TDM central. The guideline also highlights visual effects, hepatotoxicity, photosensitivity, QT effects, periostitis/fluoride toxicity, and skin cancer risk with prolonged exposure.
IsavuconazoleAccepted as an alternative primary therapy based on noninferiority to voriconazole and fewer drug-related adverse effects in the cited trial. The 2016 guideline notes less mature TDM evidence and distinctive QT-shortening behavior.
PosaconazoleThe source predates the mature evidence base for newer formulations. It distinguishes the absorption limitations of the suspension from improved delayed-release tablet/IV exposure and explicitly notes uncertainty about whether routine TDM is necessary for the newer formulations.
Amphotericin BLipid formulations are preferred over deoxycholate when possible because of nephrotoxicity and tolerability. The source details electrolyte wasting, renal toxicity, infusion reactions and formulation-specific differences.
EchinocandinsUseful in salvage therapy and as alternatives when azoles/polyenes cannot be used, but not routine primary monotherapy for IPA. Ocular, urinary and CSF concentrations are limited.
Stewardship angleBiomarker-driven preemptive strategies, selective susceptibility testing, TDM, avoidance of unnecessary empiric therapy in short neutropenia, and reassessment of breakthrough infection all represent stewardship principles embedded in the guideline.

Currency verification — external information

Verified 8 August 2026: the official IDSA site continues to host the 2016 guideline, but IDSA published a new 2026 Clinical Practice Guideline Update on the Prevention of Invasive Aspergillosis in Adult Solid Organ Transplant Recipients on 26 June 2026 (last updated 6 July 2026). This is a substantive partial update, not merely a commentary.

The 2026 SOT update changes how the 2016 transplant-prophylaxis material should be used. For example, the 2016 guideline recommended 3–4 months of antifungal prophylaxis after lung transplant, whereas the 2026 update makes no recommendation for or against universal prophylaxis in lung transplant recipients because of uncertainty, bias, heterogeneity and limited generalizability of available evidence. The newer update emphasizes individualized strategy selection, local epidemiology, adverse effects, drug interactions, cost and stewardship.

Main strengths

  • Comprehensive syndrome coverage and strong clinical organization.
  • Explicit separation of recommendation strength and evidence quality.
  • Substantial pharmacotherapy depth, especially azole PK, TDM and interactions.
  • Quantitative evidence embedded in narrative summaries rather than recommendation statements alone.
  • Transparent funding and conflict disclosures.

Main limitations

  • Literature search is now more than a decade old and largely ended in 2014.
  • English-language restriction and a relatively narrow set of named search resources reduce comprehensiveness.
  • Many strong recommendations rest on low-quality evidence, making the underlying rationale more important than the label alone.
  • Clinical populations and diagnostics are heterogeneous; performance of biomarkers depends heavily on host, prophylaxis and disease phenotype.
  • At least one major domain—adult SOT prevention—has a 2026 IDSA update that materially changes the interpretation of 2016 recommendations.
  • Many panelists disclosed industry relationships relevant to antifungal therapeutics; these were disclosed and reviewed but remain an appraisal consideration.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the characterization of the 2016 source and its internal evidence structure; moderate for present-day applicability outside the verified 2026 SOT update because a complete review of all post-2016 external guidelines/trials was outside this artifact's scope.

Practice / implementation takeaways

Use the guideline as a diagnostic architecture. Host risk → CT → invasive sampling when feasible → culture/histopathology + context-appropriate biomarkers → early therapy when suspicion is high. Avoid interpreting any biomarker outside its host population and antifungal-exposure context.
Build antifungal stewardship around exposure, not only drug choice. TDM, formulation-specific absorption, interaction review, organ function, adherence, susceptibility and breakthrough disease are core components of successful azole therapy.
Separate “strong recommendation” from “high-quality evidence.” Many clinically sensible actions are strong recommendations despite low-quality evidence. For teaching or CDS, preserve both fields rather than collapsing them into a single confidence score.
Represent chronic pulmonary aspergillosis separately from IPA. CCPA is a chronic structural-lung-disease syndrome with different diagnostic criteria, treatment duration and goals; treating it as merely “slow IPA” loses important distinctions.
Do not hard-code the transplant prophylaxis section as timeless. The 2026 adult SOT update shows why guideline content needs versioned, domain-specific provenance and replacement rules.
For literature atomization. The 98 numbered recommendations are natural reported-claim atoms, while diagnostic performance estimates, trial outcomes, TDM targets and limitations should remain separate quantitative or methodological atoms rather than being folded into the recommendation text.
