# 3. Search reproducibility and comprehensiveness

## 3.1 Source-reported electronic search

The 2021 update was part of a broader search for anticoagulation studies in people with cancer. The review reports electronic searches on **14 August 2021** in:

- CENTRAL;
- MEDLINE via Ovid, beginning in 1946;
- Embase via Ovid, beginning in 1980.

Previous electronic searches had been performed in:

- January 2007;
- February 2010;
- February 2013;
- January 2018.

The search combined:

1. anticoagulant terms;
2. cancer/neoplasm terms;
3. an RCT search filter.

No language restrictions were used.

**Source anchors:** Methods, article p. 8 / physical PDF p. 10, “Electronic searches”; Appendix 1, article pp. 54–62 / physical PDF pp. 56–64.

---

## 3.2 Appendix 1 reproducibility

Appendix 1 supplies full strategies rather than merely naming databases. The search vocabulary is unusually broad. Anticoagulant terms include:

- generic anticoagulant headings and text words;
- heparin and numerous LMWH names/brands;
- fondaparinux;
- coumarins and vitamin K antagonists;
- thrombin inhibitors;
- factor Xa inhibitors;
- direct oral anticoagulants and historical development names.

The cancer block includes controlled vocabulary plus free-text terms for malignancy, neoplasm, cancer, carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, tumor/tumour, glioma, myeloma, lymphoma, leukemia/leukaemia, epithelioma, and adenoma.

For MEDLINE, the RCT filter includes publication types and terms such as randomized/randomised, placebo, randomly, trial, with an animal-only exclusion. Embase similarly uses indexed randomized-trial/blinding/crossover terms plus text-word randomization, placebo, allocation, and related concepts.

Appendix 1 also presents **systematic-review search strategies** for MEDLINE and Embase. These appear to reflect the broader anticoagulation-in-cancer search program rather than only the RCT question in this review.

CENTRAL is searched with combined anticoagulant and malignancy terms. An Appendix row labeled “ISI (International Scientific Information) the Web of Science” is also present before the CENTRAL block, although the main Methods section names CENTRAL, MEDLINE, and Embase as the electronic databases searched for this update. Because the review separately states that Web of Science citation tracking was discontinued in February 2018, the exact role of this Appendix block in the 2021 execution is **unclear from the source**.

### Methodological appraisal

**Strengths**

- The core electronic strategies are inspectable and sufficiently explicit to permit substantial replication.
- Search vocabulary is broad across anticoagulant classes, drug synonyms, and cancer terminology.
- Controlled vocabulary is combined with free-text searching.
- No language restriction reduces language-based selection.
- Multiple historical update dates are documented.

**Important concern: retrieval architecture versus the evidence actually needed**

The target evidence is unusual: all included cancer evidence ultimately comes from **cancer subgroups inside broader VTE trials**. Yet the electronic strategy requires an anticoagulant concept **and a cancer/neoplasm concept**. A broadly framed VTE trial may therefore be relevant even when cancer is not prominent in its title, abstract, or indexing.

The review itself later acknowledges this as a potential bias: the electronic search was limited to people with cancer even though the required data came from studies not necessarily restricted to cancer populations.

This is the most consequential search-validity concern identified in Pass 1. It is not a minor technical issue; it reflects a mismatch between:

- **retrieval target:** trials discoverable through cancer terminology; and
- **evidence target:** cancer subgroup data embedded in general VTE trials.

The extensive supplementary searching partly mitigates this risk but does not remove it.

---

## 3.3 Searching outside bibliographic databases

The review reports:

- ASCO conference proceedings handsearched from **1982 through February 2021**;
- ASH proceedings searched from **2003 through 14 August 2021**;
- ClinicalTrials.gov;
- WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform;
- reference lists of included papers;
- relevant systematic reviews;
- expert contact for unpublished and ongoing trials.

### Methodological appraisal

This is a substantial strength for recall and unpublished/ongoing-study detection. In particular, searching prior systematic reviews is logically useful for a question dependent on subgroup data.

However, reproducibility is weaker outside the main bibliographic databases:

- exact search strings for ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP are not supplied in the main text;
- the procedural details of conference handsearching are not fully specified;
- expert-contact methods are not described in enough detail to reproduce precisely.

Thus, **electronic database reproducibility is high; total-search reproducibility is moderate rather than complete**.

# 4. Study selection

## 4.1 Source-reported process

Four pairs of authors independently screened titles and abstracts.

A deliberately sensitive retrieval rule was used: full text was obtained when **at least one reviewer** judged the citation potentially eligible.

Full texts were then independently assessed with:

- a standardized form;
- explicit inclusion criteria;
- explicit exclusion criteria.

Disagreements were resolved by:

1. discussion; or
2. consultation with a third review author.

In Results, reviewer agreement for eligibility is reported as **κ = 0.94**.

**Source anchors:** Methods, article p. 8 / physical PDF p. 10; Results, article p. 10 / physical PDF p. 12.

---

## 4.2 Figure 1 cross-check

Figure 1 reports the review flow as:

- 11,484 records identified through database searching;
- 1,136 additional records identified through other sources;
- 3,073 records after duplicates were removed;
- 3,073 records screened;
- 2,926 records excluded;
- 147 full-text articles assessed;
- 131 full-text articles excluded;
- 16 reports of 15 RCTs included in qualitative synthesis;
- 8 RCTs included in quantitative synthesis.

The exclusion categories shown in the figure sum to 131:

- not population of interest: 86;
- not intervention of interest: 17;
- not design of interest: 27;
- no relevant data for cancer subgroup: 1.

### Methodological appraisal

The selection process is a clear strength:

- dual independent screening reduces single-reviewer error;
- the “at least one reviewer” full-text rule prioritizes sensitivity;
- standardized eligibility forms improve consistency;
- third-party adjudication is available;
- κ = 0.94 indicates very high reported agreement.

The later study-base pass should continue to inspect a separate source inconsistency already identified in the ATOM extraction: narrative exclusion reporting does not map perfectly onto the study-flow wording/count descriptions. Pass 1 does not silently reconcile that issue.

# 5. Data extraction and management

## 5.1 Source-reported extraction

The review authors independently extracted data and resolved disagreements through discussion.

The planned extraction set was broad:

### Participant characteristics
- age;
- sex;
- histologic cancer type;
- cancer site;
- cancer stage;
- time since diagnosis;
- life expectancy;
- current cancer therapy;
- performance status;
- DVT/PE status;
- participants per arm;
- randomized participants;
- followed participants;
- intervention discontinuations.

### Intervention characteristics
- anticoagulant type;
- dose;
- administration schedule;
- duration;
- co-interventions, including radiation/systemic therapy.

### Outcomes
The reviewers attempted to obtain:

- time-to-event survival data;
- categorical data for all outcomes.

No included study reported cancer-subgroup time-to-event data.

For dichotomous outcomes, the review extracted data for complete-case analysis. When VTE was not reported as one combined outcome, DVT and PE event counts were added.

The authors attempted to contact investigators for incomplete reporting.

Abstracts were eligible for the **main analysis only if authors supplied full methods and results**; otherwise they were relegated to sensitivity analysis.

The review also extracted:

- funding;
- ethical approval;
- conflicts of interest.

**Source anchors:** Methods, article p. 8 / physical PDF p. 10.

---

## 5.2 Methodological appraisal

This is another strong component.

The extraction framework explicitly sought variables important for:

- clinical heterogeneity;
- treatment fidelity;
- follow-up;
- attrition;
- outcome interpretation;
- financial/conflict context.

The main limitation is not that the reviewers failed to plan to capture relevant information; it is that the underlying cancer-subgroup reports often **did not provide it**. Later passes must distinguish:

- **methodological collection failure**, from
- **primary-study reporting failure / subgroup-data unavailability**.

The source supports the latter much more strongly.

The policy for abstracts is conservative: incomplete abstracts do not enter the main analysis unless the investigators provide enough information to reconstruct methods and results.

# 6. Risk-of-bias methodology

## 6.1 Source-reported framework

The review assessed included studies at the study level with the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool.

Domains:

- random sequence generation;
- allocation concealment;
- participant/personnel blinding;
- outcome-assessor blinding;
- incomplete outcome data;
- selective reporting;
- other bias, including early stopping for benefit.

Review authors independently made judgments and resolved disagreements through discussion.

**Source anchor:** Methods, article p. 9 / physical PDF p. 11.

### Methodological appraisal

For the review era, this is a standard and appropriate structured framework.

Pass 1 does **not** judge whether every individual risk-of-bias rating was correct. That belongs primarily in Pass 2, where the study-level support statements and Figures 2–3 can be reconciled.

One methodological boundary should be preserved: risk of bias was assessed **at study level**, while the review's clinically relevant evidence is often a **cancer subgroup**. Study-level trial quality does not automatically resolve biases introduced by subgroup availability, selective subgroup reporting, or secondary recovery of subgroup data.

That subgroup-specific limitation is not fully represented by a conventional trial-level risk-of-bias grid.

# 7. Missing participant data

## 7.1 Source-reported classification

The review explicitly recognized that labels such as “withdrew consent” or “experienced adverse events” do not necessarily tell a reviewer whether the participant's outcome is truly missing.

Participants were therefore classified into:

### Definitely not missing
Examples include:
- explicitly followed participants;
- participants who died during the trial;
- participants from centers that were excluded.

### Definitely missing
Examples include:
- explicitly not followed participants;
- some participants with unclear follow-up status who were excluded from analysis denominators;
- some participants whose outcomes were imputed, when observed event counts could be separated from imputed events.

### Potentially missing
Participants with unclear follow-up status whose analysis inclusion did not make imputation status clear.

**Source anchor:** Methods, article p. 9 / physical PDF p. 11.

---

## 7.2 Primary complete-case analysis

For categorical outcomes:

**Denominator**
> randomized participants minus participants classified as definitely missing.

**Numerator**
> observed participants with at least one event during available follow-up.

For continuous outcomes, the authors planned to use the reported mean and SD among participants actually followed.

**Source anchor:** Methods, article p. 9 / physical PDF p. 11.

---

## 7.3 Planned missing-data sensitivity analyses

When a primary meta-analysis produced a **statistically significant effect**, the review planned sensitivity meta-analyses using progressively more stringent assumptions about outcomes among participants with missing data.

For an apparent beneficial effect (RR < 1), the planned relative incidence of events among missing versus followed participants was:

- control = 1; intervention = 1.5;
- control = 1; intervention = 2;
- control = 1; intervention = 3;
- control = 1; intervention = 5.

For apparent harmful effects (RR > 1), the assumptions were reversed between groups.

For continuous outcomes, the review planned four imputation strategies based on observed means, with the median SD from control arms used for SD imputation.

---

## 7.4 Methodological appraisal

### Strengths

The missing-data plan is substantially more explicit than a simple “available-case” statement. It:

- distinguishes missingness categories;
- avoids automatically equating administrative labels with absent outcomes;
- specifies numerator and denominator construction;
- prespecifies increasingly adverse assumptions;
- separates missingness from treatment non-compliance.

### Principal limitation

The missing-data sensitivity analyses were triggered only when the primary meta-analysis was **statistically significant**.

This makes robustness testing depend on a significance threshold. A non-significant estimate can still be clinically important, highly imprecise, or materially altered by missing outcomes. Therefore, the method is optimized to test whether a “positive” result survives increasingly adverse assumptions rather than to characterize missing-data sensitivity across the entire evidence base.

This is a **model appraisal**, not a statement made by the authors.

In this review, the trigger mattered operationally: because no primary effect estimate was statistically significant, the planned missing-data sensitivity analyses were not performed.

---

# 8. Non-compliance and intention-to-treat

The authors state that when participants were reported as non-compliant, they attempted to adhere to ITT principles and explicitly treated non-compliance as a separate problem from missing outcome data.

### Methodological appraisal

Conceptually, this distinction is sound. A participant can deviate from assigned therapy and still have a known outcome; conflating non-adherence with missingness can distort denominators and event assignment.

The phrase “attempted to adhere” is less explicit than a fully specified ITT implementation rule. Later study-level review may be needed where trial reporting makes treatment adherence or follow-up ambiguous.

---

# 9. Treatment-effect measure and synthesis model

## 9.1 Source-reported synthesis

- Risk ratios were used for dichotomous outcomes.
- The individual participant was the unit of analysis.
- RRs were calculated for each study.
- Primary meta-analysis used complete cases.
- Results were pooled with a **random-effects model**.
- Review Manager is cited in the synthesis method.
- No outcome of interest was meta-analyzed as a continuous variable.

**Source anchors:** Methods, article pp. 9–10 / physical PDF pp. 11–12.

---

## 9.2 Methodological appraisal

Using RR is appropriate for binary clinical outcomes such as mortality, recurrent VTE, and bleeding.

A random-effects model is defensible in a review spanning:

- different LMWH products;
- different dosing schedules;
- different VTE presentations;
- multiple decades of trial conduct;
- differing transition-to-VKA regimens;
- heterogeneous cancer subgroups.

However, the outcome-specific meta-analyses are often very small. The review does not describe, in the inspected Methods text, a dedicated small-meta-analysis correction or alternative inference strategy. Therefore, random-effects pooling should not be interpreted as solving the uncertainty created by sparse trials and sparse events.

This limitation belongs primarily to the precision/evidence-strength appraisal rather than being a procedural error.

---

# 10. Heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

## 10.1 Source-reported plan

Heterogeneity was assessed using:

1. forest-plot inspection;
2. I²;
3. a formal statistical test.

If substantial heterogeneity was identified, the authors planned to investigate and report possible reasons.

Subgroup analyses based on participant characteristics were planned, but **not conducted because the necessary data were unavailable**.

**Source anchor:** Methods, article p. 10 / physical PDF p. 12.

---

## 10.2 Methodological appraisal

The use of visual, quantitative, and formal heterogeneity assessment is methodologically reasonable.

The major limitation is executional rather than conceptual: the review could not perform the planned subgroup analyses. This is particularly consequential because cancer-associated VTE is plausibly heterogeneous across:

- malignancy types;
- stages;
- VTE presentations;
- treatment contexts;
- bleeding risk;
- concomitant cancer therapies.

The source itself reports that cancer type was not specified across the included cancer subgroups. Therefore, the review's ability to explain heterogeneity or assess effect modification is weak even though the methods anticipated such analysis.

No explicit numerical threshold defining “substantial heterogeneity” is stated in the inspected Methods text.

---

# 11. Sensitivity analyses

Two sensitivity-analysis structures were prespecified.

### A. Abstract-only evidence
If an abstract lacked full methods/results supplied by investigators, it was excluded from the main analysis and retained for sensitivity analysis.

### B. Missing participant data
Missing-data sensitivity analysis was planned when a primary outcome estimate was statistically significant.

### Methodological appraisal

The abstract policy is conservative and directly addresses incomplete reporting.

The missing-data policy is more debatable because sensitivity analysis is conditional on significance. Nevertheless, the assumptions themselves are explicit and progressively stringent.

Passes 3–5 should identify exactly which reported comparison results include sensitivity analyses and why. In particular, the LMWH-versus-UFH evidence includes analyses incorporating studies whose cancer-subgroup data were not directly reported in the primary trial publications; that provenance question belongs to the evidence-base and comparison passes rather than this generic methods pass.

---

# 12. Reporting bias and publication bias

## 12.1 Source-reported plan

The authors explored:

- whether trials were included in trial registries;
- whether protocols were available.

Funnel plots were planned for outcomes with **10 or more trials**.

No outcome had enough trials to permit a funnel plot.

### Methodological appraisal

This is a sensible prespecified boundary; funnel-plot interpretation in very small meta-analyses would be weak.

The consequence is that the review had **little empirical ability to assess small-study/publication bias at the outcome level**. Absence of a funnel plot should therefore be read as “not assessable with the planned method,” not evidence that publication bias was absent.

The broader search for conference abstracts, registries, and unpublished/ongoing studies partially mitigates publication-bias risk.

---

# 13. GRADE methodology

The authors assessed certainty **at the outcome level** using GRADE and cite:

- the GRADE handbook;
- GRADE guidance for communicating systematic-review findings.

The structured Summary of Findings tables provide:

- effect estimates;
- absolute effects;
- participant/study counts;
- certainty category;
- explicit downgrade footnotes.

### Methodological appraisal

Outcome-level GRADE is a major strength because it prevents one undifferentiated “quality” rating from being applied to an entire comparison.

For this review, imprecision is the dominant downgrade mechanism in the Summary of Findings tables.

A source-internal inconsistency remains unresolved: one Discussion paragraph describes some evidence as “moderate” despite the Abstract, Plain Language Summary, and structured Summary of Findings material classifying those outcomes as **low certainty**. This is not a flaw in the GRADE framework itself; it is a reporting/reconciliation issue to preserve for final QA.

# 15. Differences between protocol and review

The source explicitly reports four changes:

1. PubMed “related citation” searching and Web of Science citation tracking were discontinued as of February 2018 after two years in which they retrieved no additional references beyond the automated database search.
2. The search strategy was updated.
3. The author list was revised to include only authors who contributed to the updated review.
4. Risk-of-bias and certainty-of-evidence judgments were revisited.

**Source anchor:** “Differences between protocol and review,” article p. 64 / physical PDF p. 66.

---

## 15.1 Appraisal of protocol deviations

### Discontinuing related-citation / citation-tracking methods

The change is transparent and supported by a stated empirical rationale: no incremental references were found over two years.

This is methodologically defensible as a pragmatic review-maintenance decision, especially because database searches, conference searching, registries, reference-list review, prior systematic reviews, and expert contact remained.

However, citation chasing provides redundancy against imperfect indexing. Given this review's unusual dependence on cancer subgroups inside general VTE trials, removing one supplementary retrieval route modestly increases reliance on the remaining search architecture.

### Updating the search strategy

Updating terminology is appropriate for a long-running review, especially as anticoagulant vocabulary changed substantially between 2007 and 2021.

Appendix 1 confirms inclusion of newer anticoagulant terminology, including direct oral anticoagulants, even though this review's eligible initial-treatment comparisons remained parenteral.

### Revisiting risk-of-bias and certainty judgments

This is appropriate in an updated review, but it creates a need for careful version control: final SEA synthesis should not assume that judgments in earlier CD006649 versions are identical to the 2021 review.

The internal GRADE wording discrepancy makes this particularly relevant.

The review is transparent about support, roles, and methodological divergence. Final appraisal should preserve that transparency without inferring causation that the source does not establish.

# 17. Methodological-validity synthesis

## Major strengths

### 1. Strong reviewer-process protections
Independent screening, sensitive full-text retrieval, standardized eligibility assessment, independent extraction, discussion-based reconciliation, and third-reviewer adjudication provide a strong procedural foundation.

### 2. Broad multimodal search
The review used three major bibliographic databases plus conference proceedings, registries, references, prior reviews, and expert contact, with no language restriction.

### 3. Reproducible main electronic strategies
Appendix 1 gives detailed search logic rather than an abbreviated conceptual description.

### 4. Explicit missing-data framework
The review classifies missingness and prespecifies complete-case calculations and adverse sensitivity assumptions.

### 5. Separation of non-compliance from missingness
This reduces conceptual conflation between protocol deviation and unavailable outcomes.

### 6. Prespecified handling of incomplete abstracts
Abstracts without recoverable full methods/results were excluded from the main analysis.

### 7. Structured bias and certainty assessment
Cochrane risk-of-bias methods and outcome-level GRADE provide explicit validity and certainty frameworks.

### 8. Transparent review maintenance
Update dates, protocol differences, funding, interests, and author-list changes are disclosed.

---

## Major methodological concerns

### 1. Cancer-restricted retrieval may miss the evidence structure of interest
This is the most important Pass 1 concern. The review needs cancer subgroup data from broad VTE RCTs, but the electronic strategy requires cancer terminology. The source itself acknowledges this risk.

### 2. Missing-data sensitivity analysis depends on statistical significance
Robustness testing was planned primarily to challenge significant findings rather than characterize missing-data uncertainty for all outcomes.

### 3. Planned effect-modifier analysis could not be executed
The methods anticipated subgroup analysis, but the underlying reports did not provide enough cancer-specific characteristics.

### 4. Publication-bias assessment was intrinsically underpowered
No outcome reached the ≥10-trial threshold for funnel plots.

### 5. Random-effects modeling cannot compensate for sparse evidence
Several outcome syntheses involve very few studies or a single study. Pooling framework does not remove the underlying uncertainty.

### 6. Overall search reproducibility exceeds supplementary-search reproducibility
Database Boolean strategies are detailed; registry, conference, and expert-contact procedures are less fully reproducible.

### 7. Study-level risk-of-bias assessment does not fully capture cancer-subgroup provenance
Subgroup availability and secondary recovery of subgroup data introduce concerns distinct from conventional trial-level risk-of-bias domains.

---

# 18. Methodological validity: provisional non-numeric judgment

**Procedural quality:** **Strong**

The review uses mature systematic-review procedures: duplicate/independent review, broad search methods, explicit missing-data handling, random-effects synthesis, risk-of-bias assessment, GRADE, protocol-change reporting, and update traceability.

**Search validity for this particular question:** **Moderate, with a specific structural concern**

The search is broad and reproducible, but requiring cancer terminology is imperfectly aligned with a review in which eligible cancer evidence may be embedded in general VTE trials.

**Analytic robustness:** **Moderate**

The statistical plan is explicit, but the actual evidence base is sparse, subgroup-derived, and incompletely reported. Sensitivity-analysis triggering based on statistical significance further narrows robustness assessment.

**Transparency:** **Strong**

Search strategies, methodological changes, funding, interests, and guideline-interface differences are explicitly documented.

> **Pass 1 bottom line:** The review's procedural methods are generally stronger than the evidence base they ultimately have to analyze. The dominant methodological threat is not careless review execution; it is the difficulty of reliably identifying, recovering, and analyzing cancer-subgroup evidence from trials that were not designed or necessarily indexed around cancer.

This judgment is provisional and non-numeric. It must be integrated with Pass 2 study-level provenance/risk of bias and Passes 3–5 comparison-specific evidence before final SEA scoring.

1. How many studies and events actually support each outcome.
2. Whether random-effects pooling materially changes interpretation compared with individual study estimates.

4. Which sensitivity analyses were actually executed.
5. Whether the authors' direction-of-effect language is calibrated to confidence intervals and GRADE certainty.
6. Whether absence of harms data undermines comparative claims.
