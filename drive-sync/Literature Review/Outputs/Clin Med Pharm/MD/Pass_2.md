# 1. Section function

Section 2 establishes a mechanism-informed treatment framework for
Enterobacterales capable of clinically significant inducible chromosomal ampC
expression. The section does not treat "AmpC organisms" as a homogeneous
taxonomic group. Instead, it separates organisms by their demonstrated
likelihood of clinically important AmpC induction and then combines:

1. species-level risk;
2. antibiotic induction potential;
3. antibiotic stability against AmpC hydrolysis;
4. infection severity and source control;
5. susceptibility results;
6. probability of co-produced resistance mechanisms, especially ESBLs;
7. toxicity and stewardship considerations.

The practical thesis is that the clinical importance of AmpC is not simply
whether an organism possesses an ampC gene. Treatment selection depends on
whether AmpC expression can rise sufficiently during therapy to compromise the
chosen agent and whether that agent remains stable to the resulting enzyme
activity.

# 2. Mechanistic framework

## 2.1 Three mechanisms of increased AmpC production

The guidance identifies three major mechanisms:

1. inducible chromosomal expression of ampC;
2. constitutive chromosomal ampC hyperexpression caused by regulatory mutations;
3. plasmid-mediated ampC genes, which may sometimes integrate into the chromosome.

This section primarily addresses the first mechanism: Enterobacterales with a
moderate likelihood of clinically significant inducible ampC expression.

Induction can occur after exposure to selected β-lactams. Increased periplasmic
AmpC accumulation can raise MICs to agents such as:

- aztreonam;
- cefotaxime;
- ceftazidime;
- ceftriaxone;
- piperacillin-tazobactam.

The clinically important phenomenon is therefore dynamic resistance: an isolate
that initially tests susceptible, particularly to ceftriaxone, may become
resistant after only a few doses of therapy.

## 2.2 Constitutive and plasmid-mediated AmpC

Constitutive chromosomal hyperexpression and plasmid-mediated AmpC generally
produce persistently elevated enzyme expression, often manifesting as baseline
non-susceptibility to cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and ceftriaxone.

Plasmid-mediated ampC appears relatively uncommon in the cited United States
cohorts, occurring in <10% of ceftriaxone-non-susceptible isolates and largely
among E. coli and K. pneumoniae. CMY-type enzymes, particularly CMY-2,
predominate in E. coli; both CMY- and DHA-type enzymes occur in K. pneumoniae.

# 3. Q2.1 — Species at moderate risk for clinically significant inducible AmpC

## Clinical question

Which commonly identified Enterobacterales species should be considered at
moderate risk for clinically significant inducible ampC?

## Suggested approach

The panel identifies the following as the principal moderate-risk organisms:

- Enterobacter cloacae complex
- Klebsiella aerogenes
- Citrobacter freundii

Hafnia alvei is also placed in this risk category on the basis of in-vitro
evidence, although clinical experience is more limited.

## Core rationale

The ideal evidence would prospectively document:

1. baseline β-lactam susceptibility;
2. resistance emerging after β-lactam exposure;
3. molecular confirmation that the subsequent isolate is the same strain;
4. demonstration that increased AmpC production caused the resistance.

The guidance explicitly states that such comprehensive data are unavailable.

Older mnemonics such as SPACE, SPICE, and ESCPM are criticized because they
collapse organisms with meaningfully different AmpC biology into a single
category. Examples include:

- C. freundii possesses chromosomal ampC, whereas C. koseri does not.
- P. vulgaris generally lacks a chromosomal ampC despite historical inclusion
  of "indole-positive Proteus" in AmpC mnemonics.
- Morganella morganii and Providencia rettgeri were historically represented
  under outdated taxonomic groupings.

The section therefore favors explicit species identification over mnemonic-based
decision-making.

## Load-bearing quantitative finding

For E. cloacae complex, K. aerogenes, and C. freundii, clinical reports suggest
resistance emergence after exposure to agents such as ceftriaxone in
approximately:

- **~20% of infections**

The guidance therefore generally advises avoiding cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and
ceftriaxone for these organisms despite initial susceptibility.

## Lower-risk organisms

The guidance distinguishes:

- Morganella morganii
- Providencia spp.
- Serratia marcescens

as organisms in which clinically significant AmpC overexpression appears to
occur in:

- **<5% of infections**

For these organisms, therapy may generally follow AST results.

Evidence is not entirely uniform: one observational study found worse outcomes
with ceftriaxone than cefepime/carbapenem among mostly M. morganii and
S. marcescens infections, whereas a subsequent study did not reproduce this
association.

## Less common organisms

Examples such as Citrobacter youngae and Yersinia enterocolitica possess
inducible chromosomal ampC but are inadequately studied.

For these organisms:

- AST-directed therapy is considered reasonable;
- ceftriaxone may be used when susceptible;
- cefepime becomes a reasonable alternative when bacterial burden is high or
  source control is limited, such as:
  - endocarditis;
  - central nervous system infection.

## Conditional exception

For E. cloacae, K. aerogenes, C. freundii, or H. alvei:

If a third-generation cephalosporin was already started for a **non-severe
infection**, source control is adequate, and the patient is improving, completing
that treatment course may be reasonable.

## Intrinsic-resistance reminder

Basal AmpC expression in the moderate-risk organisms confers intrinsic
resistance to:

- ampicillin;
- amoxicillin-clavulanate;
- ampicillin-sulbactam;
- first-generation cephalosporins;
- cephamycins.

## Evidence architecture

- observational clinical reports
- in-vitro induction studies
- mechanistic microbiology
- expert synthesis
- no comprehensive prospective genotype-confirmed induction study

## Major uncertainty

Species-specific estimates are incomplete, and the true probability of
clinically relevant induction depends on both organism and clinical context.

# 4. Q2.2 — How induction potential and hydrolytic stability should guide β-lactam selection

## Suggested approach

β-lactam selection should consider **two distinct properties**:

1. the drug's propensity to induce ampC expression;
2. the drug's stability against hydrolysis by the resulting AmpC enzyme.

Induction potential alone is insufficient to predict clinical usefulness.

## Antibiotic-mechanism matrix

### Potent inducers that are readily hydrolyzed

- aminopenicillins
- first-generation cephalosporins
- cephamycins

These agents are generally ineffective even at basal AmpC expression.

### Potent inducer but stable to AmpC

**Imipenem**

- potent inducer;
- generally stable to AmpC hydrolysis through formation of stable acyl-enzyme
  complexes.

### Carbapenems with incompletely characterized induction

- ertapenem
- meropenem

Formal induction potential has not been adequately studied, but both are
generally stable to AmpC hydrolysis.

### Weak inducers but vulnerable to hydrolysis

- aztreonam
- cefotaxime
- ceftazidime
- ceftriaxone
- piperacillin-tazobactam

Their weak induction potential does **not** make them reliable for
moderate-risk AmpC organisms because increased AmpC can hydrolyze them.

### Weak inducer and relatively stable to AmpC

**Cefepime**

This combination—low induction plus relative hydrolytic stability—is the major
mechanistic basis for cefepime's preferred role.

### Non-β-lactams

The following neither induce ampC nor serve as AmpC substrates:

- TMP-SMX
- fluoroquinolones
- aminoglycosides
- tetracyclines
- other non-β-lactam classes

## Core clinical inference made by the panel

The AmpC treatment hierarchy cannot be reduced to "strong inducer = bad."
Imipenem is an important counterexample: it induces ampC strongly but remains
effective because the resulting enzyme does not efficiently hydrolyze it.

Likewise, ceftriaxone and piperacillin-tazobactam may be relatively weak
inducers but remain clinically vulnerable because increased AmpC expression can
hydrolyze them.

# 5. Q2.3 — Cefepime

## Suggested approach

Cefepime is suggested for infections caused by:

- E. cloacae complex
- K. aerogenes
- C. freundii
- H. alvei

when the cefepime MIC is:

- **≤8 µg/mL**

This includes both susceptible and susceptible-dose-dependent isolates.

## Mechanistic rationale

Cefepime:

- has low propensity for ampC induction;
- demonstrates relative stability to AmpC hydrolysis;
- can achieve favorable PK/PD target attainment when appropriately dosed.

## Clinical evidence

No randomized clinical trial has directly compared cefepime with carbapenems
for AmpC-E infections.

A **2025 meta-analysis** included:

- 7 studies
- 1,099 patients
- bacteremia caused by E. cloacae, K. aerogenes, C. freundii,
  M. morganii, Providencia spp., or S. marcescens

and found no detected difference between cefepime and carbapenem treatment in:

- mortality;
- clinical cure;
- infection relapse.

## Important limitations of that evidence

The meta-analysis was limited by:

- inter-study heterogeneity;
- confounding by indication, because sicker patients may preferentially receive
  carbapenems;
- inclusion of species with differing baseline probabilities of clinically
  significant AmpC induction.

Thus, the section's support for cefepime reflects a combination of mechanistic
plausibility, PK/PD, and absence of a convincing clinical inferiority signal
rather than direct randomized equivalence evidence.

## Dosing dependency

Table 1 is directly relevant.

For infections other than uUTI, the global dosing table specifies:

- cefepime 2 g IV loading dose over 30 minutes;
- then 2 g IV every 8 hours infused over 3 hours;

assuming normal renal and hepatic function.

The section's favorable PK/PD interpretation therefore depends on use of an
appropriate high-exposure regimen rather than arbitrary cefepime dosing.

## ESBL co-production caveat

Cefepime is considered less reliable for ESBL-E. The guidance therefore treats
co-production of ESBLs as an important exception.

Reported United States estimates vary:

- E. cloacae, 66-hospital surveillance, 2019–2020:
  - blaCTX-M in **3% (6/211)**
- Pennsylvania E. cloacae bloodstream isolates, 2003–2005:
  - SHV-type ESBLs in **33% (15/45)**
- Maryland bloodstream isolates, 2018–2021:
  - K. aerogenes: **22% (4/18)**
  - E. cloacae: **14% (7/51)**
  - C. freundii: **0% (0/8)**

The latter studies did not demonstrate a consistent relationship between ESBL
presence and cefepime MIC.

The guidance concludes that current data do not establish cefepime
susceptible-dose-dependent MICs of 4–8 µg/mL as a reliable surrogate for ESBL
co-production.

## Practical boundary

cefepime_preferred_if:
  - organism is moderate-risk AmpC species
  - cefepime MIC <=8 µg/mL
  - ESBL gene has not been identified
  - appropriate dosing/PK-PD exposure can be achieved

cefepime_enmetazobactam:
  expected_activity: yes
  preferred_use: >
    Reserved preferentially for ESBL-E or organisms co-producing AmpC and ESBL
    rather than routine AmpC-E treatment.

## Evidence maturity

moderate:
  strengths:
    - strong mechanistic basis
    - PK/PD plausibility
    - large pooled observational evidence base
  limitations:
    - no direct randomized cefepime-versus-carbapenem trial
    - heterogeneous species composition
    - confounding by illness severity
    - uncertain contemporary ESBL co-production prevalence

# 6. Q2.4 — Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and ceftazidime

## Suggested approach

For invasive infection caused by moderate-risk AmpC organisms:

- ceftriaxone is **not suggested**;
- cefotaxime is **not suggested**;
- ceftazidime is **not suggested**.

Organisms specifically named:

- E. cloacae complex
- K. aerogenes
- C. freundii
- H. alvei

### Conditional continuation

If ceftriaxone was initiated empirically for a **non-severe infection** and the
patient demonstrates:

- clear clinical improvement;
- adequate source control;

continuation to complete therapy may be reasonable.

## Central quantitative finding

Across available studies, emergence of ceftriaxone resistance after exposure
occurs in approximately:

- **20%** of infections caused by E. cloacae, K. aerogenes, or C. freundii.

Comparable clinical data for H. alvei are lacking.

## Methodological problems in estimating this risk

The guidance explicitly identifies multiple limitations:

1. No CLSI-endorsed method or FDA-cleared device exists for routine detection of
   ampC genes or AmpC enzymes in clinical isolates.
2. Ceftriaxone resistance may arise from mechanisms other than AmpC, including
   ESBL production.
3. Many studies pool low-risk species such as S. marcescens with higher-risk
   species such as E. cloacae.
4. Molecular confirmation that baseline and resistant follow-up isolates are the
   same strain is frequently absent.
5. Some studies used pre-2010 ceftriaxone breakpoints:
   - historical susceptible MIC ≤8 µg/mL
   - current susceptible MIC ≤1 µg/mL
6. Studies vary substantially in:
   - source of infection;
   - illness severity;
   - comorbidities;
   - concomitant therapy;
   - ceftriaxone dose;
   - duration of exposure.

## Comparative outcomes

No clinical trial directly compares ceftriaxone with alternatives such as
cefepime for presumed AmpC-E infection.

Among five cited observational cohorts comparing ceftriaxone with cefepime or
carbapenems for E. cloacae, K. aerogenes, and C. freundii:

- **2 of 5 cohorts** reported poorer outcomes with ceftriaxone.

## Patient-level modifiers

Risk of resistance emergence may be higher when there is:

- persistent infection source;
- inadequate source control;
- prolonged antibiotic exposure.

## Practical interpretation

The panel prioritizes prevention of on-therapy resistance for invasive disease
over ceftriaxone's operational convenience.

Once-daily ceftriaxone may still have a role after clear improvement in a
relatively uncomplicated infection without an ongoing source such as:

- abscess;
- retained infected catheter.

This is explicitly framed as a risk-benefit decision rather than evidence that
ceftriaxone becomes biologically reliable after improvement.

## Evidence maturity

low-to-moderate:
  supporting_signal:
    - repeatedly observed resistance emergence near 20%
    - some observational outcome signals
    - strong biologic plausibility
  limitations:
    - no randomized comparative treatment trial
    - major diagnostic misclassification potential
    - older breakpoint use
    - heterogeneous species and syndromes
    - incomplete strain/genotype confirmation

# 7. Q2.5 — Piperacillin-tazobactam

## Suggested approach

Piperacillin-tazobactam is **not suggested for invasive infections** caused by
Enterobacterales at moderate risk of clinically significant inducible AmpC.

## Mechanistic rationale

Tazobactam provides substantially weaker protection against AmpC-mediated
β-lactam hydrolysis than newer inhibitors such as:

- avibactam;
- relebactam.

One cited in-vitro study of E. cloacae demonstrated inhibition by tazobactam
paired with ceftolozane in:

- wild-type isolates: **100% (32/32)**
- ESBL-producing isolates: **67% (6/9)**
- AmpC-producing isolates: **19% (10/53)**

Another study found progressively increasing piperacillin-tazobactam MICs with
greater ampC expression.

## Observational evidence

A 2019 meta-analysis of:

- 12 observational studies

did not identify a mortality difference between piperacillin-tazobactam and
carbapenems for bacteremia caused by mixed Enterobacterales species including:

- Enterobacter spp.
- Citrobacter spp.
- Serratia spp.
- Providencia spp.
- Morganella spp.

However, the guidance highlights:

- moderate heterogeneity;
- probable confounding by indication.

At least three later observational studies including:

- **759 patients**

suggested poorer outcomes with piperacillin-tazobactam than cefepime and/or
carbapenems.

## Randomized trial signal

One clinical trial randomized:

- **72 patients**

with bloodstream infections caused by a mixed group of potential AmpC organisms
to:

- piperacillin-tazobactam: n=38
- meropenem: n=34

The composite primary outcome did not differ significantly.

Individual component outcomes were discordant:

| Outcome | Piperacillin-tazobactam | Meropenem |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mortality | 0% (0/38) | 6% (2/34) |
| Clinical failure | 21% (8/38) | 12% (4/34) |
| Microbiological failure | 13% (5/38) | 0% (0/34) |
| Microbiological relapse | 0% (0/38) | 9% (3/34) |

## Interpretation

The trial does not provide a clean efficacy signal:

- mortality numerically favored piperacillin-tazobactam;
- clinical and microbiological failure numerically favored meropenem;
- microbiological relapse numerically favored piperacillin-tazobactam;
- sample size was small;
- species were heterogeneous.

The panel explicitly characterizes the findings as difficult to interpret and
states that a larger trial is needed.

## Why the recommendation remains cautious

The "not suggested" position for invasive infection is driven by triangulation:

1. weak inhibitor activity of tazobactam against AmpC;
2. rising MICs with increased AmpC expression;
3. observational signals suggesting worse outcomes;
4. inconclusive small randomized evidence.

The recommendation therefore appears more conservative than the randomized data
alone would justify, but is consistent with the section's general strategy of
avoiding agents vulnerable to AmpC hydrolysis in serious infection.

## Evidence maturity

mixed:
  mechanistic_evidence: concerning
  observational_evidence: mixed but increasingly unfavorable
  randomized_evidence: small and inconclusive
  certainty_of_inferiority: not established
  guideline_position: avoid for invasive disease / use caution in serious infection

# 8. Q2.6 — Newer β-lactams active against carbapenem-resistant organisms

## Suggested approach

Expected to retain activity against AmpC-E:

- aztreonam-avibactam
- ceftazidime-avibactam
- imipenem-relebactam
- meropenem-vaborbactam
- cefiderocol

However, the panel recommends generally **reserving these agents for
carbapenem-resistant organisms**.

Ceftolozane-tazobactam is **not suggested** for AmpC-E infection.

## Mechanistic rationale

The newer β-lactamase inhibitors:

- avibactam;
- vaborbactam;
- relebactam

inhibit AmpC enzymes.

Cefiderocol generally retains activity against AmpC-E.

Carbapenems are stable to AmpC-mediated hydrolysis, making the carbapenem
components of:

- imipenem-relebactam;
- meropenem-vaborbactam

intrinsically active against AmpC-E even apart from the inhibitor contribution.

## Stewardship principle

The principal reason not to use these agents routinely is **not lack of expected
AmpC activity**. It is preservation of high-value drugs for organisms with
carbapenem resistance.

This is therefore primarily a stewardship recommendation.

## Ceftolozane-tazobactam exception

Ceftolozane was engineered for stability against pseudomonal AmpC
cephalosporinases, but evidence for Enterobacterales AmpC is substantially less
favorable.

Limited in-vitro data show inconsistent activity. One cited investigation found
activity against only:

- **19% (10/53)**

of E. cloacae isolates with moderate AmpC production.

The weak protective effect of tazobactam against AmpC is a major biologic
limitation.

## Conditional polymicrobial use

If both:

- DTR P. aeruginosa;
- AmpC-E

are present in a polymicrobial infection, ceftolozane-tazobactam may be
considered to reduce the need for multiple antibiotics and associated toxicity.

If used this way:

- close clinical monitoring is advised.

## Evidence maturity

For newer inhibitor combinations and cefiderocol:
  activity_support:
    - enzymology
    - microbiology
    - established class pharmacology
  routine_AmpC_clinical_outcome_data: limited
  recommendation_driver: antimicrobial stewardship

For ceftolozane-tazobactam:
  AmpC-E_support: weak
  principal_problem:
    - poor tazobactam protection against AmpC
    - limited/inconsistent in-vitro activity

# 9. Q2.7 — Non-β-lactam therapy

## Suggested approach

Non-β-lactam drugs remain treatment options for AmpC-E when:

1. the isolate is susceptible;
2. the agent is appropriate for the infection site;
3. illness severity permits its use;
4. agent-specific toxicity is acceptable.

Mechanistically, non-β-lactams are not AmpC substrates and do not induce ampC.

## uUTI

Preferred:

- nitrofurantoin
- TMP-SMX

Alternatives:

- single-dose aminoglycoside
- ciprofloxacin
- levofloxacin
- pivmecillinam
- sulopenem

Mecillinam and sulopenem demonstrate in-vitro activity against:

- **>90% of AmpC-E isolates**

but clinical outcomes data remain limited.

### Gepotidacin

Susceptibility data against AmpC-E are comparatively sparse, and available data
suggest elevated MICs among common AmpC-E isolates.

Therefore:

- gepotidacin is **not suggested empirically for AmpC-E uUTI in the absence of
  confirmed susceptibility**.

## cUTI

Preferred:

- ciprofloxacin
- levofloxacin
- TMP-SMX

Alternatives:

- aminoglycosides
- sulopenem

## Invasive infection and oral transition

Because TMP-SMX and fluoroquinolones are not AmpC substrates, oral transition
with:

- ciprofloxacin;
- levofloxacin;
- TMP-SMX

may be considered for invasive AmpC-E infections once:

- susceptibility is confirmed;
- clinical stability has been achieved.

This recommendation should also be interpreted within the global oral-transition
criteria from Pass 0:

- hemodynamic stability;
- adequate oral absorption;
- adequate drug exposure at the infection site.

## Evidence maturity

Mechanistic basis: strong.

Clinical evidence is syndrome- and drug-dependent.

The guidance frequently extrapolates from broader UTI or Enterobacterales data
rather than from dedicated AmpC-E randomized trials, particularly for newer oral
agents such as pivmecillinam and sulopenem.

# 11. Clinical modifiers that repeatedly alter recommendations

## Infection severity

More severe or invasive infection increases the importance of choosing an agent
stable to AmpC hydrolysis.

## Source control

Adequate source control permits greater flexibility, particularly when a patient
is already improving on ceftriaxone.

Poor source control increases concern for:

* resistance emergence;
* bacterial burden;
* prolonged drug exposure.

## Bacterial burden

High-burden infections such as:

* endocarditis;
* CNS infection;
* persistent abscess;

may justify cefepime even for organisms otherwise considered at lower AmpC risk.

## Duration of exposure

Longer β-lactam exposure may increase the opportunity for clinically significant
AmpC derepression and resistance emergence.

## ESBL co-production

This is particularly important when considering cefepime.

The guidance does not support using cefepime MIC alone as a reliable indicator
that ESBL co-production is absent.

## Susceptibility testing

All treatment suggestions assume susceptibility to the selected agent.

For AmpC specifically, the absence of standardized routine clinical AmpC
detection limits diagnostic certainty.

# 13. Breakpoint dependencies

The most important AmpC-specific breakpoint dependency is cefepime.

From the global Table 2 framework:

* Enterobacterales cefepime susceptible breakpoint: ≤2 µg/mL
* cefepime MIC 4–8 µg/mL: susceptible dose-dependent

Section 2 explicitly extends its cefepime recommendation through:

* MIC ≤8 µg/mL

provided:

* appropriate dosing is used;
* an ESBL gene has not been identified.

This is a central example of why the final SEA should retain Table 2 rather than
summarizing susceptibility simply as "sensitive" or "resistant."

# 14. Safety and toxicity considerations

This section is less toxicity-centered than the CRAB or CRE sections, but
several safety/implementation factors remain relevant:

* aminoglycosides require consideration of nephrotoxicity and PK-guided dosing;
* fluoroquinolone use remains conditional on agent-specific toxicities;
* TMP-SMX use requires agent-specific toxicity assessment;
* unnecessary use of newer broad-spectrum β-lactams is discouraged partly to
  preserve future antimicrobial effectiveness;
* ceftolozane-tazobactam may occasionally simplify polymicrobial therapy and
  reduce toxicity from multiple-drug regimens.

The AmpC section's dominant safety strategy is therefore **avoiding
microbiologic failure and resistance emergence**, not simply minimizing direct
drug adverse effects.

---

# 15. Stewardship principles

Major stewardship messages:

1. Do not treat all historical "SPICE/SPACE" organisms as equivalent.
2. Avoid unnecessarily broad treatment for lower-risk organisms when AST-directed
   therapy is adequate.
3. Prefer cefepime over automatic carbapenem escalation when appropriate.
4. Reserve newer agents active against carbapenem-resistant organisms for
   infections where those agents are more necessary.
5. Avoid piperacillin-tazobactam and third-generation cephalosporins for serious
   moderate-risk AmpC infections when more reliable agents are available.
6. Use oral non-β-lactam transition when susceptibility and clinical stability
   permit.
7. Maintain vigilance for resistance emergence when response is inadequate.

# 18. Major uncertainties and limitations

## 18.1 No standardized routine AmpC diagnostic test

No CLSI-endorsed method or FDA-cleared device is available for routine clinical
confirmation of ampC genes or AmpC enzyme production.

Consequences:

* AmpC attribution can be uncertain;
* alternative mechanisms may be misclassified as AmpC;
* clinical cohorts may contain biologically heterogeneous isolates.

## 18.2 Species heterogeneity

Studies often combine:

* moderate-risk organisms;
* low-risk organisms;

making pooled treatment estimates difficult to apply to a specific species.

## 18.3 Resistance-emergence studies have important historical limitations

Some used:

* outdated ceftriaxone breakpoints;
* no strain-relatedness testing;
* incomplete molecular resistance characterization.

## 18.4 Cefepime evidence is not randomized

The recommendation is supported by:

* mechanistic strength;
* PK/PD;
* observational comparative data;

but not a definitive cefepime-versus-carbapenem RCT.

## 18.5 Piperacillin-tazobactam evidence is discordant

The mechanistic data and some observational studies are unfavorable, but the
available randomized trial is too small and internally inconsistent to establish
a clear treatment effect.

## 18.6 ESBL co-production prevalence is uncertain

Published US estimates vary markedly by:

* geography;
* period;
* species;
* ESBL gene studied.

Contemporary multicenter molecular surveillance remains needed.

## 18.7 Newer oral agents

Pivmecillinam and sulopenem show favorable in-vitro AmpC-E activity, but
AmpC-specific clinical outcomes remain limited.

# 20. Appraisal observations to carry into final SEA

These are provisional appraisal findings, NOT final scores.

## Strengths

1. The section abandons oversimplified AmpC mnemonics in favor of
   organism-specific risk.
2. It explicitly separates induction potential from hydrolytic stability.
3. Recommendations incorporate:

   * resistance mechanism;
   * PK/PD;
   * clinical outcomes;
   * source control;
   * illness severity;
   * stewardship.
4. Cefepime guidance is unusually operational because it specifies an MIC boundary
   and links that boundary to high-exposure dosing.
5. The authors explicitly describe major weaknesses in the ceftriaxone and
   piperacillin-tazobactam evidence rather than obscuring uncertainty.
6. The section distinguishes expected microbiologic activity from appropriate
   stewardship use for newer β-lactams.

## Limitations

1. Many key recommendations depend on observational evidence and mechanistic
   inference.
2. No standardized clinical AmpC diagnostic method limits phenotype assignment.
3. Species are heterogeneous in both biology and available evidence.
4. Cefepime preference lacks direct randomized comparison with carbapenems.
5. Ceftriaxone resistance estimates are methodologically heterogeneous.
6. Piperacillin-tazobactam evidence is internally conflicting.
7. Some guidance for newer agents is based primarily on in-vitro activity and
   stewardship logic rather than dedicated AmpC clinical trials.
8. Clinical evidence for H. alvei is especially sparse.
9. Contemporary US estimates of ESBL co-production remain incomplete.

## Central appraisal question for final synthesis

Does the combination of mechanistic plausibility, resistance-emergence risk,
PK/PD, and mostly observational clinical evidence justify the degree of
recommendation certainty communicated by the panel—particularly for:

* cefepime as preferred therapy;
* avoidance of ceftriaxone in invasive disease;
* avoidance of piperacillin-tazobactam in invasive disease?

The section generally communicates uncertainty appropriately, but the final SEA
should compare its evidentiary threshold with that used elsewhere in the
guideline.

# 21. Practice-translation synthesis

The AmpC section can be compressed into five clinically reusable principles:

### 1. Think species, not mnemonic

High/moderate-risk:

* E. cloacae complex
* K. aerogenes
* C. freundii
* H. alvei

Lower-risk:

* M. morganii
* Providencia spp.
* S. marcescens

### 2. Think induction AND hydrolysis

A weak inducer can still fail if AmpC readily hydrolyzes it.

### 3. Cefepime is the key carbapenem-sparing β-lactam

Use when:

* MIC ≤8 µg/mL;
* appropriate high-exposure dosing is used;
* ESBL is not identified.

### 4. Avoid agents vulnerable to derepressed AmpC in invasive disease

Particularly:

* ceftriaxone / cefotaxime / ceftazidime;
* piperacillin-tazobactam.

### 5. Non-β-lactams preserve a mechanism-independent pathway

When susceptible and clinically appropriate:

* TMP-SMX;
* fluoroquinolones;
* aminoglycosides;
* selected oral UTI agents.
