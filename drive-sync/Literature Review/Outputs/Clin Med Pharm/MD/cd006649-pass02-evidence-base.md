> This pass evaluates the composition and interpretability of the evidence base. It focuses on how the cancer subgroups were obtained, how the 15 RCTs differ clinically and operationally, how follow-up and outcome availability vary, how the review judged study-level risk of bias, and how missing subgroup data constrain the synthesis. Comparison-specific effect estimates remain primarily the responsibility of Passes 3–5.

---

# 1. Pass purpose

Pass 0 identified the evidence-base architecture as a major interpretive constraint. Pass 2 therefore asks:

1. What actually entered the review after screening?
2. How were the cancer-subgroup data for each trial obtained?
3. Which studies contributed directly to the main analyses versus sensitivity analyses?
4. How heterogeneous were the included VTE populations, LMWH products, UFH regimens, treatment settings, follow-up periods, and subsequent anticoagulation?
5. How complete was cancer-specific follow-up?
6. What do Figures 2–3 and the detailed risk-of-bias tables actually show?
7. Does the study-level risk-of-bias framework adequately represent the additional risk created by subgroup-data availability?
8. What potentially eligible cancer-subgroup evidence was unavailable?
9. Are there source-internal counting or classification ambiguities that must remain unresolved until final synthesis?

No final whole-source score or practice recommendation is assigned here.

# 4. Cancer-subgroup derivation: provenance of the 15 RCTs

All 15 included studies contained people with cancer as **subgroups** within broader VTE trials. None was described as a cancer-only randomized trial.

The review identifies four distinct subgroup-data provenance routes.

## 4.1 Directly reported cancer-subgroup data

Three studies directly reported cancer-subgroup data:

- **Prandoni 1992**
- **Simonneau 1993**
- **Van Doormaal 2009**

Using the review's cancer-subgroup counts, these account for approximately **519 of 1,615 participants (32.1%)**.

## 4.2 Cancer-subgroup data reported in follow-up publications

Three studies had follow-up publications reporting the subgroup data:

- **Breddin 2001**
- **Hull 1992**
- **Merli 2001**

Using the review-level participant accounting, these contribute approximately **319 participants (19.8%)**.

## 4.3 Cancer-subgroup data obtained from study authors

Two studies required author-provided cancer-subgroup data:

- **Prandoni 2004 (GALILEI)**
- **Wells 2005**

Together these account for **269 participants (16.7%)**.

## 4.4 Cancer-subgroup data recovered from prior systematic reviews

Seven studies did not report the cancer-subgroup data directly, so this Cochrane review used values reported in two previous systematic reviews:

- **Büller 1997 (COLOMBUS)**
- **Duroux 1991**
- **Koopman 1996**
- **Levine 1996**
- **Lindmarker 1994**
- **Lopaciuk 1992**
- **Simonneau 1997 (THESEE)**

These seven studies account for approximately **508 participants (31.5%)** of the review-level cancer population.

The review **excluded these seven studies from the main analysis** and used them in sensitivity analyses.

**Source anchor:** Included studies, physical PDF p. 14 / printed p. 12.

### Pass 2 appraisal

This is the most consequential evidence-provenance limitation in the review.

Approximately one-third of the review-level cancer population is represented by subgroup data that the current review did not recover directly from the trial report, follow-up report, or investigator. Instead, those data were inherited from prior secondary syntheses.

That does not make the values invalid, but it creates an additional provenance layer:

**primary RCT → earlier systematic review → current Cochrane review**

The decision to keep those seven trials out of the main analysis is therefore methodologically important and appropriate to preserve in all later interpretation.

# 7. Breddin 2001 denominator issue

The source contains a notable denominator distinction:

- the **included-studies narrative** states that Breddin recruited **83 people with cancer**;
- the **Characteristics of included studies table** lists **137 (12%) people with cancer** in the trial subgroup;
- the characteristics table also shows a **third reviparin arm** in addition to the reviparin-BID and UFH comparison.

If all characteristics-table cancer subgroup counts are simply summed, the total is **1,669**, not the review-reported **1,615**. Replacing Breddin's 137 with the narrative value of 83 reduces the sum by **54** and exactly reproduces the review-level total of 1,615.

### Model inference — not explicitly resolved by the source

The 54-person difference plausibly reflects participants in the third Breddin reviparin regimen who were not part of the eligible LMWH-versus-UFH comparison used for this review. The inspected source sections do **not** explicitly state that reconciliation.

### Required handling

- Preserve **83** when reproducing the review's 1,615-participant evidence-base accounting.
- Preserve **137** when describing what the Characteristics table says about the broader Breddin cancer subgroup.
- Do not silently convert one number into the other.
- Revisit the Breddin primary/follow-up publication only if a later task requires definitive arm-level denominator reconstruction.

# 8. Intervention heterogeneity

The label “LMWH versus UFH” conceals substantial treatment heterogeneity.

## 8.1 LMWH products represented

Across the 13 LMWH-versus-UFH trials, the LMWH intervention includes:

- **reviparin** — Breddin 2001; Büller 1997;
- **nadroparin** — Duroux 1991; Koopman 1996; Lopaciuk 1992; Prandoni 1992; Prandoni 2004;
- **tinzaparin** — Hull 1992; Simonneau 1997;
- **enoxaparin** — Levine 1996; Merli 2001; Simonneau 1993;
- **dalteparin** — Lindmarker 1994.

Dose schedules vary from once daily to twice daily, and some are explicitly weight based while others are described only as weight-based regimens in the Cochrane table.

## 8.2 UFH regimens represented

UFH comparators vary in:

- route: predominantly IV, but some protocols transition to or use SC adjusted dosing;
- duration: approximately 5–10 days;
- target aPTT:
  - 1.5–2;
  - 1.5–2.5;
  - 1.5–3;
  - 2–3;
  - absolute targets such as 50–90 s or 55–80 s.

This is not one standardized UFH comparator.

## 8.3 Fondaparinux comparison

Van Doormaal 2009 compares weight-tiered fondaparinux with a **heparin comparator that includes either enoxaparin or UFH**. That pooled comparator should remain explicit in Pass 4; “fondaparinux versus heparin” is not synonymous with fondaparinux versus UFH alone.

## 8.4 LMWH-versus-LMWH comparison

Wells 2005 directly compares:

- tinzaparin 175 IU/kg daily;
- dalteparin 200 IU/kg daily.

This is clinically and methodologically distinct from the LMWH-versus-UFH question.

### Pass 2 appraisal

The review's comparison families are reasonable for synthesis, but the exposure categories are broader than the study-level regimens. Product, dose frequency, UFH target, route, and treatment setting differ enough that “LMWH” and “UFH” should be understood as **classes of regimens**, not uniform interventions.

This heterogeneity becomes particularly important if later forest-plot interpretation suggests treatment-effect differences across studies.

# 9. Treatment-setting heterogeneity and co-intervention structure

Several trials compare more than pharmacology alone.

Examples:

- Büller 1997: LMWH could be treated at home while UFH was administered in hospital.
- Koopman 1996: LMWH at home versus UFH in hospital.
- Levine 1996: enoxaparin primarily as outpatient therapy versus continuous IV UFH in hospital.
- Van Doormaal 2009: home-care drug administration.
- Wells 2005: outpatient treatment.

Most other studies do not report the setting in the Cochrane characteristics table.

### Appraisal

For open-label studies, home/outpatient LMWH versus inpatient IV UFH can introduce differences in:

- healthcare exposure;
- mobility;
- monitoring intensity;
- treatment burden;
- crossover/co-interventions;
- ascertainment behavior.

These differences may be part of the pragmatic value of LMWH, but they also mean the trial contrast is not always a pharmacologically isolated comparison.

The review's performance-bias ratings appropriately identify lack of blinding in most of these studies, but the later clinical interpretation should distinguish:

**drug effect** from **drug-plus-delivery-strategy effect**.

# 10. Subsequent anticoagulation

The review states that in **14 of 15 studies**, initial parenteral treatment was followed by oral anticoagulation for at least three months.

The dominant follow-on strategy was a vitamin K antagonist, typically with an INR target around 2.0–3.0.

Duroux 1991 differed: after day 10, long-term therapy followed the participating center's usual practice and could be either adjusted-dose SC UFH or oral anticoagulation.

### Appraisal

The initial randomized exposure often lasted only 5–10 days, while outcomes were measured over approximately three months. Therefore, later outcome differences reflect the consequence of the initial treatment strategy **followed by largely shared long-term anticoagulation**, not a three-month randomized comparison of the parenteral agents themselves.

This temporal structure is essential for interpreting mortality and recurrent-VTE estimates.

# 11. Population heterogeneity

## 11.1 VTE phenotype

The cancer subgroups include:

- proximal DVT;
- distal/calf DVT;
- upper-extremity DVT;
- PE;
- mixed DVT/PE populations.

Examples at the extremes:

- Simonneau 1997 is a PE population.
- Wells 2005 includes upper- or lower-extremity DVT.
- several studies excluded PE and focused on proximal DVT.

## 11.2 Cancer characterization

The review explicitly states that **none of the 15 studies specified the types of cancer** in the included cancer subgroups.

The Methods intended to capture:

- histology;
- tumor site;
- stage;
- time since diagnosis;
- life expectancy;
- current therapy;
- performance status.

The included-study evidence did not provide enough cancer-specific detail to support those planned analyses.

### Appraisal

This is a major limitation in external validity and effect-modifier assessment.

“People with cancer” encompasses substantial differences in:

- thrombotic risk;
- bleeding risk;
- prognosis;
- thrombocytopenia;
- cancer therapy;
- renal/hepatic function;
- tumor-specific biology.

Because those characteristics are not adequately represented, the review cannot determine whether treatment effects differ across clinically important cancer subgroups.

# 12. Follow-up heterogeneity and missing cancer-subgroup follow-up

Study-level follow-up ranges from approximately **90 days / 3 months to 6 months**, although the review's prespecified outcomes are evaluated up to three months after treatment initiation.

The review reports complete cancer-subgroup follow-up in most studies, with three exceptions:

- **Breddin 2001:** approximately 91% cancer-subgroup follow-up;
- **Duroux 1991:** no cancer-subgroup follow-up information reported;
- **Merli 2001:** no cancer-subgroup follow-up information reported.

For Duroux and Merli, the review analyzed available data assuming potentially missing data were missing at random.

### Appraisal

The review's detailed risk-of-bias tables assign **low risk for incomplete outcome data to all 15 studies**, including Duroux and Merli.

That judgment is source-reported and should be preserved, but the cancer-subgroup evidence has more uncertainty than the all-green incomplete-outcome row in Figure 3 visually suggests.

The important distinction is:

- **trial-level attrition judgment:** judged low risk by the review;
- **cancer-subgroup follow-up completeness:** incomplete or unreported in three trials.

Pass 2 considers the latter an additional interpretive limitation even though it did not change the review authors' formal risk-of-bias category.

# 14. Figure 3 — aggregate risk-of-bias pattern

Figure 3 visually summarizes the study-level judgments:

- sequence generation: essentially all low risk;
- allocation concealment: predominantly low risk, with 2 high-risk studies;
- participant/personnel blinding: predominantly high risk, with only 3 low-risk studies;
- outcome-assessment blinding: all low risk as formally rated;
- incomplete outcome data: all low risk as formally rated;
- selective reporting: all low risk;
- other bias: all low risk.

### Interpretation

The figure gives the appearance of an evidence base that is methodologically strong except for performance bias.

That is accurate **within the review's chosen study-level Cochrane domains**, but incomplete as a description of the cancer-specific evidence architecture.

The risk-of-bias graphic does not directly encode:

- whether cancer-subgroup data were published at all;
- whether they came from a follow-up publication;
- whether they were investigator-supplied;
- whether they were recovered only from another systematic review;
- whether cancer-subgroup follow-up was specifically available;
- whether subgroup selection/reporting was prespecified;
- whether cancer type/stage was known.

These are central to this review even though they are not red/yellow cells in Figure 2.

# 15. Selective reporting versus selective subgroup availability

The review formally judged selective outcome reporting as low risk for all 15 studies because the outcomes described in each study's Methods were reported in its Results.

At the same time:

- cancer-subgroup data were missing from **seven included studies** and had to be recovered from earlier systematic reviews;
- **11 additional potentially eligible studies** had cancer subgroups but lacked usable subgroup outcome data.

### Appraisal

These are not necessarily the same phenomenon as conventional selective outcome reporting.

A trial can report its prespecified trial-level outcomes correctly while failing to report a clinically important subgroup. Therefore:

> **Low risk of selective outcome reporting at the parent-trial level does not eliminate risk of cancer-subgroup availability bias in this review.**

This distinction should be carried into the final appraisal rather than treating the all-green selective-reporting row as evidence that subgroup evidence was comprehensively available.

# 16. Industry funding and "other bias"

The Characteristics tables report commercial or mixed commercial support for many included trials, including sponsors/manufacturers such as:

- Knoll;
- Sanofi-Choay / Sanofi Winthrop / Sanofi;
- Novo Nordisk;
- Pharmacia;
- Aventis;
- Gentium;
- Leo Pharmaceuticals;
- Sanofi/Organon.

Some studies report noncommercial or mixed support, and some do not report funding.

The review nevertheless assigns **low risk for “other bias” to every study** and states that no other bias was suspected.

### Appraisal boundary

Commercial sponsorship is relevant context but is not sufficient by itself to infer biased results. Pass 2 therefore records the funding pattern without overriding the review's formal risk-of-bias judgments.

The final appraisal may consider the combination of:

- sponsorship;
- open-label designs;
- incomplete subgroup reporting;
- absent protocols/registration in many older studies;

but should not convert those features into an unsupported causal claim of sponsor bias.

# 17. Missing cancer-subgroup evidence among excluded studies

The narrative states that among excluded full-text reports, **11 studies included people with cancer as subgroups but did not provide usable cancer-subgroup outcome data**:

1. Albada 1989
2. Belcaro 1999
3. Bratt 1990
4. Büller 2004
5. Fiessinger 1996
6. Harenberg 1990
7. Harenberg 2000
8. Holm 1986
9. Hull 2000
10. Luomanmaki 1996
11. Riess 2003

The detailed excluded-study table labels each of these as:

> “Data for cancer subgroup not available.”

The Discussion later states that these 11 studies could have contributed approximately **340 additional participants** to the meta-analysis.

That is substantial relative to the **1,615 currently included participants**.

### Quantitative perspective

If those 340 participants had been available, the cancer-participant evidence pool could have been approximately **1,955 participants**, an increase of about **21% over the included 1,615**.

This calculation is derived from the source-reported counts:

`1,615 + 340 = 1,955`

It does **not** imply that all 340 participants would have contributed to every outcome or comparison.

### Appraisal

This is a major evidence-completeness limitation because unavailable subgroup data can bias pooled estimates if subgroup availability is associated with outcome magnitude, intervention, publication characteristics, or investigator decisions.

The source itself explicitly acknowledges this possibility.

# 18. Full-text exclusion count inconsistency

The review contains an internal accounting ambiguity that should remain visible.

## Figure 1

- 131 full-text articles excluded.
- Categories:
  - 86 not population;
  - 17 not intervention;
  - 27 not design;
  - 1 no relevant cancer-subgroup data.
- These sum to **131**.

## Narrative text

The excluded-studies paragraph states:

- of the 131 excluded full-text articles, **11** had cancer subgroups but unavailable outcome data;
- the **remaining 123** articles were excluded for other reasons;
- it then lists category counts of 86 + 17 + 1 + 27, which themselves sum to **131**, not 123.

## Detailed excluded-study table

The table contains:

- the 11 studies explicitly labeled “Data for cancer subgroup not available”;
- **Wester 1996**, separately labeled “No relevant data for cancer subgroup”.

### Pass 2 conclusion on this issue

The source does not provide a clean reconciliation among:

- the 131 total full-text exclusions;
- the 11 unavailable subgroup-data studies;
- the “remaining 123” wording;
- the Figure 1 category counts.

This appears to be an internal reporting/counting inconsistency.

**Do not repair the flow diagram mathematically in the final SEA.** Report the inconsistency and preserve the clearly supported facts:

- 147 full texts were assessed;
- 15 RCTs were included;
- 11 potentially eligible cancer-subgroup studies lacked usable subgroup outcome data;
- those 11 could have added approximately 340 cancer participants.

# 19. Evidence-base strengths

Without assigning a final SEA score, Pass 2 identifies the following strengths:

1. **Randomized parent studies**
   - All 15 included studies are RCTs.

2. **Objective VTE context**
   - The review required objectively confirmed VTE and generally relied on objective testing/adjudication for recurrent thromboembolism.

3. **Structured risk-of-bias assessment**
   - All included studies were evaluated across explicit Cochrane domains.

4. **Strong sequence-generation profile**
   - All 15 were judged low risk for random sequence generation.

5. **Allocation concealment usually adequate**
   - 13 of 15 were judged low risk.

6. **Outcome assessment generally protected**
   - Formal detection-bias rating was low risk in all studies, with 14 explicitly described as blinded and Duroux treated as low risk despite probable assessor non-blinding.

7. **Sensitivity separation for weaker subgroup provenance**
   - Seven studies with indirectly sourced cancer-subgroup data were excluded from main analysis rather than silently pooled as equivalent evidence.

8. **Subgroup-data recovery effort**
   - The review used follow-up publications, investigator contact, and prior systematic reviews to recover otherwise unavailable cancer-specific data.

9. **Transparent acknowledgment of incomplete evidence**
   - The Discussion explicitly identifies the 11 unavailable subgroup studies and estimates the additional participant count they could have contributed.

# 20. Evidence-base limitations

The evidence-base limitations are more consequential than the largely green Figure 3 might initially suggest.

## 20.1 Cancer subgroup rather than cancer-specific RCTs

All included cancer evidence is subgroup evidence from broader VTE trials.

## 20.2 Sparse cancer characterization

Cancer type is not specified, and stage/treatment/performance status are inadequately available.

## 20.3 Secondary subgroup-data provenance

Seven of 15 included trials rely on cancer-subgroup data obtained from earlier systematic reviews.

## 20.4 Missing potentially eligible subgroup evidence

Eleven additional studies could not contribute because usable cancer-subgroup outcomes were unavailable.

## 20.5 Cancer-subgroup follow-up gaps

Breddin had ~91% subgroup follow-up; Duroux and Merli did not report cancer-subgroup follow-up.

## 20.6 Performance bias

Twelve of 15 trials were not blinded for participants/personnel.

## 20.7 Intervention heterogeneity

The LMWH class includes multiple agents, schedules, and doses; UFH protocols and aPTT targets vary.

## 20.8 Treatment-setting heterogeneity

Some comparisons pair home/outpatient LMWH with hospital IV UFH, so treatment setting and delivery strategy can differ with the drug.

## 20.9 Subsequent therapy dilutes isolation of the initial randomized exposure
