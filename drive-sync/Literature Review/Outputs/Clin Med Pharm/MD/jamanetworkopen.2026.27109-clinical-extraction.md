# Bacterial Infections in US Adults With Firearm Injuries — Clinical Extraction

**Citation:** Yang J, Tung CC, Harfouche MN, Baghdadi JD. *Bacterial Infections in US Adults with Firearm Injuries.* JAMA Network Open. 2026;9(8):e2627109. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.27109. Published August 3, 2026.

## Clinically important findings

- 24,024 adult firearm-injury hospitalizations were identified across 323 hospitals.
- Bacterial infection occurred in 1,236 patients (5.1%). Early infection during the index hospitalization occurred in 1,034 (4.3%), and late infection within 90 days was reported in 306 (1.3%). These timing groups overlap.
- Median antibiotic duration was 1 day (IQR 0–1) in the overall cohort and 9 days (IQR 5–17) among infected patients.
- Common organisms were *E coli* (20.9% of infected patients), *S aureus* (12.4%), and enterococci (12.4%).
- Adjusted early-infection risk was highest with head/neck/spine surgery (OR 5.18, 95% CI 3.96–6.78); lower abdominal surgery was also associated (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.40–2.24).
- Adjusted late-infection risk was increased with liver surgery (OR 3.83, 95% CI 2.25–6.51) and head/neck/spine surgery (OR 2.30, 95% CI 1.27–4.16).
- Index-hospitalization infection was not independently associated with mortality among patients surviving at least one night (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.64–1.16; P=.33).

## Stewardship implications

The paper supports targeted surveillance and diagnostic attention in higher-risk operative groups. It does not test prophylaxis duration or spectrum, so it does not support extending prophylaxis simply because a patient falls into a higher-risk surgical category. The contrast between 1-day median antibiotic exposure overall and 9 days among infected patients is best interpreted as prophylaxis versus treatment phases, not evidence for a longer preventive course.

## Limitations

Administrative coding, proxy measures for acute severity, a deliberately specific culture-plus-treatment infection definition, and incomplete follow-up limit causal interpretation. The article also contains a likely denominator inconsistency in the unadjusted mortality sentence: it reports 102 deaths among 1,134 infected patients as 8.3%, while 1,236 patients are reported elsewhere as infected. The adjusted mortality model is not affected by that arithmetic issue in the presented estimate, but the unadjusted denominator should not be reused without verification.
