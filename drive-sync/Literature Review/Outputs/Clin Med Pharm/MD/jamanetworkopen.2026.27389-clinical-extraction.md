# SNAP Benefit Cycles and Antiretroviral Therapy Adherence Among Adults With HIV — Clinical Extraction

**Citation:** Richterman A, Bair EF, Kinsey EW, et al. *SNAP Benefit Cycles and Antiretroviral Therapy Adherence Among Adults With HIV.* JAMA Network Open. 2026;9(8):e2627389. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.27389. Published August 5, 2026.

## Clinically important findings

- The analyzable cohort included 54 Philadelphia-area SNAP recipients with HIV and recent suboptimal ART adherence, contributing 5,609 person-days. Mean observed adherence was 66.9% (95% CI, 60.3%-73.5%).
- A Bayesian hierarchical change-point model placed the adherence change point 20 days after SNAP receipt (95% CrI, 7-27 days), with a 97.1% posterior probability that the adherence slope decreased afterward.
- After the change point, adherence declined by an estimated 1.0 percentage point per day (95% CrI, -2.1 to -0.1). The modeled peak-to-trough decline was 11.9 percentage points (95% CrI, 4.2-20.2).
- Leave-one-participant-out analyses were stable: estimated change points ranged from 19.3 to 19.9 days and peak-to-trough declines from 10.3 to 13.8 percentage points.
- SNAP disbursement timing was assigned by case-number rules across the first 10 business days, and baseline characteristics were balanced across early, middle, and late disbursement groups (global balance test P=.34).

## Clinical and implementation implications

The study suggests that adherence behavior can vary systematically with an intramonth resource cycle even when the medication itself has no cost-sharing. In practice, food insecurity, cash-flow constraints, and benefit timing are reasonable contextual factors to assess when unexplained adherence variability is suspected. The data do not establish that changing SNAP payment cadence, adding reminders, or altering ART management will improve adherence or viral suppression; those remain intervention questions.

## Limitations

The cohort was small, selected for recent missed ART doses, and drawn from one metropolitan area; 4 of 58 enrolled participants were excluded because they did not successfully use the electronic pill caps. The proposed scarcity and habit-formation mechanisms were not directly measured. The study used an adherence process measure rather than viral suppression or other clinical outcomes, and no adherence intervention was tested. Supplementary methods and the data-sharing supplement were referenced in the article but were not separately available through the current retrieval path.
