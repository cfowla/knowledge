The Effectiveness of Nirsevimab on Antibiotic Use in Children Using Target Trial Emulation

Clinical-practice appraisal of a retrospective sequential target-trial emulation evaluating whether nirsevimab receipt is associated with fewer antibiotic-prescribing events during infants’ first RSV season.

One-page synthesis

Bottom line: In 15,341 infants eligible for nirsevimab, 48.3% received it. Target-trial emulation estimated fewer antibiotic-associated respiratory encounters among recipients, with the clearest practical signal in outpatient disease. The primary ARTI endpoint showed a 14.4% relative reduction and a 2.8-percentage-point absolute risk difference at 6 months. The dramatic 69.4% relative reduction for antibiotic prescribing during RSV hospitalization corresponded to only a 0.1-percentage-point absolute difference and had a very wide 95% CI, so that endpoint should not dominate interpretation.
What the study addsPost-approval, real-world evidence that RSV passive immunization may reduce downstream antibiotic exposure, not only RSV disease. It explicitly designs the EHR analysis as sequential emulated trials aligned on eligibility, time zero, treatment strategy, and follow-up.
Strongest evidenceLarge cohort, two RSV seasons, 32 practices, shared EHR, clinically relevant antibiotic-exposure endpoints, adjusted marginal structural modeling, robust variance for repeated infant contributions, and 95% CIs for effect estimates.
Main threat to validityResidual confounding remains plausible because receipt was nonrandom. Nirsevimab recipients differed from nonrecipients in age, race, insurance, and other measured characteristics, while the final adjustment set did not include every measured socioeconomic or perinatal variable.
Most important interpretive limitThe outcomes measure antibiotic prescribing associated with respiratory diagnoses, not inappropriate antibiotic use. Reduced prescribing is stewardship-relevant, but the study cannot determine what fraction of avoided prescriptions were unnecessary versus clinically indicated.
Best use: Support the concept that RSV immunization can have an antibiotic-sparing co-benefit and strengthen population-level stewardship messaging. It is not evidence to broaden eligibility beyond current RSV-immunization guidance.

Section condensation — background and methods

Background / rationale

The authors frame RSV prevention as a potential antimicrobial-stewardship intervention because viral ARTIs commonly generate antibiotic prescriptions, and RSV can mimic or predispose to bacterial syndromes such as AOM and pneumonia. Prior nirsevimab trials established prevention of medically attended RSV disease and hospitalization, but antibiotic prescribing was not a primary endpoint.

The study therefore asks whether nirsevimab receipt reduces subsequent antibiotic exposure during the first RSV season in ambulatory and inpatient settings.

Study design and setting

The retrospective cohort came from 32 CHOP primary-care practices using a common Epic EHR. The 2023–2024 season began 16 October 2023 and the 2024–2025 season began 3 October 2024, with both ending 31 March. The source states that different start dates reflected nirsevimab availability within the network.

Eligibility required birth between April and September and a primary-care visit within 14 days of life. Children with reported maternal RSV vaccination were excluded. The network used clinical decision support to identify age/weight-eligible patients and check contraindications.

Sequential target trial emulation

The authors divided each RSV season into six monthly sequential trials. At each baseline, eligible infants without prior nirsevimab receipt or the outcome could enter; those still eligible could contribute to multiple trials until an outcome occurred. Treatment assignment reflected nirsevimab receipt at time zero versus no receipt.

The design targets an intention-to-treat causal contrast and relies on consistency, positivity, and conditional ignorability/no unmeasured confounding. Continuous dates were discretized to monthly intervals, creating an implicit one-month grace period. Robust sandwich variance estimators addressed repeated infant contributions.

Outcome ascertainment and covariates

Primary outcomes were first antibiotic-prescribing events linked to outpatient ARTIs, outpatient bronchiolitis, or RSV-associated hospitalization. Because respiratory viral testing is uncommon in outpatients, the broader ARTI and bronchiolitis diagnoses served as clinically relevant proxies; hospitalized children with respiratory symptoms were tested for RSV.

Measured baseline variables included practice, sex, race, ethnicity, language, gestational age, birthweight, insurance, age, and complex chronic conditions. Final outcome models were reported as adjusted for age, sex, race, medical complexity, practice membership, and RSV-season period.

Statistical analysis

Marginal structural models estimated cumulative risks under the two strategies. Discrete-time hazards were modeled with pooled logistic regression and follow-up indicators. Nirsevimab effectiveness (NE) at six months was defined as 1 − risk ratio; risk differences and pointwise 95% confidence intervals were also estimated. Delta-method CIs were used for NE.

Analyses used R 4.3.2 and the TrialEmulation package. Data were extracted 25 July 2025, and 100 randomly selected EHR charts were manually reviewed to validate data fields. Data are not publicly available.

SEQUENTIAL EMULATED-TRIAL WORKFLOW
1. Identify primary-care attendee cohort eligible for first-season nirsevimab.
2. At each monthly trial baseline, exclude prior nirsevimab receipt and prior outcome.
3. Classify strategy at time zero: nirsevimab vs no nirsevimab.
4. Follow for first outcome or administrative end of RSV season, up to 6 months.
5. Allow still-eligible infants to contribute to later sequential trials.
6. Fit pooled-logistic marginal structural models with baseline adjustment.
7. Use robust sandwich variance for within-infant correlation.
8. Derive cumulative risks, risk differences, and NE = 1 − RR at 6 months.

Key identifying requirement: no important unmeasured confounding plus positivity and consistency.

Results and quantitative interpretation

Of 18,805 infants born in the relevant calendar windows, 16,058 had a primary-care visit within 14 days and 717 were excluded for maternal RSV vaccination, leaving 15,341 eligible infants. Nirsevimab was administered to 7,413 (48.3%); 7,928 did not receive it. Mean age at RSV-season start was lower among recipients (3.06 vs 3.92 months). Recipients also had lower proportions of Black infants (21.8% vs 26.5%) and publicly insured infants (35.3% vs 40.6%), among other smaller differences.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. This is a high-value clinical infectious-diseases and stewardship paper because it asks a novel, actionable secondary-effect question using a deliberately causal observational design. It should influence how the benefits of RSV prevention are discussed, but not be treated as randomized proof of antibiotic-overuse reduction.
Relevance
9/10

Directly links RSV prevention to antibiotic exposure and stewardship. Evidence basis: primary antibiotic-associated respiratory outcomes in routine pediatric care. Limit: pediatric first-season population. Would rise: demonstration of downstream resistance, adverse-event, or health-system effects.

Novelty
8/10

Moves beyond RSV hospitalization efficacy to antibiotic use as an explicit outcome in post-approval practice. Evidence basis: target-trial emulation across two seasons. Limit: prior pooled trial data already suggested lower antibiotic prescribing for any indication. Would rise: mechanistic adjudication of avoidable versus indicated antibiotics.

Method strength
7/10

Strong observational design with aligned time zero, sequential eligibility, model-based cumulative risks, robust variance, and chart validation. Limit: nonrandom exposure, untestable ignorability, and incomplete reproducibility details in the main article. Would rise: published code/weights, diagnostics for positivity, full covariate-balance analyses, and external replication.

Evidence strength
7/10

Consistent direction across related outcomes and CIs that exclude the null for primary estimates. Limit: EHR proxies, no adjudication of antibiotic appropriateness, and very imprecise rare-event hospitalization estimate. Would rise: prospective or randomized antibiotic-use endpoint with external validation.

External validity
6/10

Includes 32 academic/community practices and a socioeconomically diverse cohort. Limit: one regional health system, only April–September births with early network engagement, and exclusion of maternal-vaccine recipients. Would rise: multi-system replication including birth-hospital prophylaxis pathways and broader geography.

Implementation value
7/10

Useful for stewardship messaging and RSV-immunization program evaluation. Limit: does not establish eligibility, product choice, cost-effectiveness, or an effect for newer clesrovimab. Would rise: current multi-product comparative data and operational/cost analyses.

Critical appraisal

  • Residual confounding is the central validity issue. Recipients were younger and differed in race and insurance distribution. The source reports adjustment for age, sex, race, medical complexity, practice, and RSV-season period, but not every measured socioeconomic/perinatal characteristic.
  • Healthcare-seeking behavior is a plausible unmeasured confounder. Families more likely to obtain prophylaxis may differ in propensity to seek care, accept prescriptions, or remain within the network. The authors acknowledge healthcare utilization as an unmeasured factor.
  • The stewardship endpoint is antibiotic exposure, not appropriateness. The paper does not adjudicate bacterial coinfection, guideline concordance, spectrum, duration, or whether a prescription was unnecessary.
  • Outpatient endpoints are syndromic rather than RSV-confirmed. That improves practical capture of antibiotic exposure but weakens mechanistic attribution specifically to prevented RSV.
  • Hospitalization results require absolute-risk framing. NE 69.4% sounds substantial, but the absolute risk difference is 0.1 percentage point and the CI is wide.
  • Reproducibility is incomplete. Data are not public, exact outcome ICD-10/antibiotic definitions are in unavailable supplementary tables, and study-specific code is not provided in the main article.
  • Selection narrows generalizability. Requiring a primary-care visit within 14 days and restricting to April–September births produces an established-care cohort rather than all U.S. infants eligible for RSV immunization.
Do not overclaim: The results do not show that nirsevimab “prevents inappropriate antibiotics” as a directly measured endpoint. They show fewer modeled first antibiotic-prescribing events associated with respiratory diagnoses among nirsevimab recipients, under observational causal assumptions.

Practice translation and external verification

Practice implication supported by the study: For infants who already meet RSV-immunization criteria, prevention may yield an additional antibiotic-sparing benefit. This strengthens the public-health and stewardship rationale for achieving recommended RSV protection; it does not independently justify changing eligibility.

Current U.S. RSV-immunization context — externally verified

Current CDC/ACIP and AAP guidance has evolved beyond the product landscape represented in this 2023–2025 cohort. For infants younger than 8 months entering their first RSV season who are not protected through maternal RSV vaccination, nirsevimab or clesrovimab are current long-acting monoclonal-antibody options. Nirsevimab remains the antibody recommended for eligible high-risk children entering a second season. This means the study’s antibiotic-sparing estimate is directly about nirsevimab and should not automatically be transferred to clesrovimab, even though both prevent RSV disease.

The first study season also overlapped the documented 2023–2024 U.S. nirsevimab supply shortage. That external context matters for causal appraisal because product availability can influence who receives treatment by calendar time and site. The paper aligns sequential trials by calendar time and adjusts for practice and RSV-season period, but the main-text adjustment list does not explicitly name season-year or report positivity diagnostics.
