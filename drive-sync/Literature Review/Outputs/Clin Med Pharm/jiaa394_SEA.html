Clinical / practice question

Question

What organizational structures, expertise, interventions, diagnostics, surveillance, metrics, and feedback processes should healthcare institutions use to implement antifungal stewardship?

Population / setting

Healthcare facilities using systemic antifungals, particularly centers caring for critically ill or immunocompromised patients and managing invasive candidiasis or invasive aspergillosis.

Intervention / comparator

AFS program components versus usual care or less-structured stewardship. Comparators vary across the cited literature and are not standardized by this statement.

Outcomes emphasized

Appropriateness, antifungal exposure and cost, time to optimal therapy, diagnostic quality, guideline/bundle adherence, resistance surveillance, TDM, process measures, and - where feasible - patient outcomes.

Section-by-section condensation

Framing and scope

The statement adapts seven global antimicrobial-stewardship core elements to antifungal practice. It focuses mainly on invasive candidiasis and invasive aspergillosis, while arguing that the framework is portable to other mycoses and care settings. The authors emphasize that antifungal prescribing differs from antibacterial stewardship because diagnosis is often uncertain, high-risk populations are clinically complex, drug interactions and PK variability are substantial, and use volume is lower and more heterogeneous.

The burden argument is substantial: the source reports systemic antifungal exposure in nearly 3% of US hospital admissions and 7.7% of ICU admissions, with an estimated 30-50% of prescriptions potentially optimizable or inappropriate. The paper therefore frames AFS as both a patient-safety and systems problem rather than a cost-control initiative alone.

Core element 1 - Leadership

AFS should be visible in budgets, strategic plans, performance priorities, job descriptions, and annual goals. The statement treats leadership commitment as an enabling condition for staffing, information technology, and organizational reach, while allowing AFS to exist either as a dedicated program or as an extension of a broader AMS program depending on local case volume and complexity.

Supporting data are mostly indirect or observational. The paper cites a 2015 NHSN survey in which 48.1% of 4,569 US acute/critical-access hospitals met all seven CDC core elements and leadership commitment was among the weakest elements; an English AFS survey identified staff time (67%) and competing priorities (48%) as major barriers. Staffing requirements specific to AFS remain undefined.

Core element 2 - Accountability and responsibilities

The recommended operating model is multidisciplinary and deliberately crosses service lines. Core expertise includes mycology, diagnostics, antifungal PK/PD, dosing, duration, drug interactions, toxicities, susceptibility, surveillance, and TDM, with ID physicians and ID-trained pharmacists preferred when available. Specialty champions from hematology/oncology, transplant, intensive care, and related services are treated as operational partners rather than external recipients of stewardship advice.

This section is strongest as an implementation argument: it recognizes that antifungal decisions can affect chemotherapy, transplantation, immunosuppression, outpatient access, and other essential therapies. It also gives alternatives for resource-limited hospitals - network sharing, contracted expertise, collaborative organizations, telehealth, or locally trained personnel - rather than making tertiary-center staffing a prerequisite.

Core element 3 - Expertise in infection management

Diagnostic stewardship is central. The statement recommends timely culture and non-culture diagnostics, but repeatedly warns that biomarkers must be interpreted against pretest probability, host factors, specimen type, and concurrent therapy. Negative results can support de-escalation in selected settings; indiscriminate screening in low-risk populations can create false positives and unnecessary imaging or antifungal treatment.

The quantitative message is Bayes-sensitive: the source suggests limited utility when pretest probability is below ~10% and notes typical invasive candidiasis rates of 1.8-7.8% in many ICUs and probable/proven mold disease below 3% in total hematology populations. Routine galactomannan screening during posaconazole prophylaxis was cited as producing false-positive results in 13.8% of patients in one study. Rapid diagnostics are presented as most useful when coupled to AFS action.

Core element 4 - Education and practical training

Education is necessary but insufficient alone. Programs should target recurrent errors in distinguishing colonization from infection, choosing prophylaxis versus empiric treatment, interpreting microbiology, and dosing/monitoring antifungals. The preferred model links education to bedside or workflow interventions rather than relying on passive teaching.

The source reports 40% inappropriate antifungal use in one tertiary hospital, about half of asymptomatic candiduria patients treated in a four-center cohort of 305 patients, and underdosing of antifungals - particularly fluconazole - in roughly one-third of septic patients in a PK point-prevalence study. These examples support targeted education but do not establish that education alone changes hard outcomes.

Core element 5 - Actions for responsible use

The highest-value actions are infectious-diseases consultation when feasible, point-of-care guidelines/bundles, post-prescription review and feedback, and systematic prescribing-quality evaluation. The paper favors a collaborative model such as handshake stewardship over purely restrictive preauthorization, especially because antifungal decisions are complex and often require immediate initial therapy followed by diagnostic refinement.

The source cites an 11-center candidemia bundle involving 648 nonneutropenic patients, with clinical success of 92.9% among bundle-compliant patients versus 75.8% without compliance (P=.011). It also cites a children's-hospital handshake intervention associated with a 12.1% reduction in antifungal DOT/1,000 patient-days (131 to 109). These are compelling implementation signals but remain largely observational or quasi-experimental evidence rather than randomized program evaluations.

Core element 6 - Monitoring and surveillance

Local surveillance should capture standardized IFD definitions, defined populations, species-level identification when possible, timely reporting, and mechanisms to disseminate findings. The section adds susceptibility testing, cumulative susceptibility reports, rational diagnostic utilization, medication interaction screening, and timely triazole TDM. Importantly, surveillance must account for culture-negative disease and the difficulty distinguishing colonization from invasive infection.

The paper gives clinically useful cautions: NCBTs can be most informative at intermediate pretest probabilities; a biomarker-plus-stewardship strategy was associated with a 90% reduction in inappropriate antifungal starts in one ICU experience; drug interactions were reported in approximately 88% of hospitalizations involving mold-active triazoles. TDM is recommended because exposure can be highly variable, especially with critical illness, altered absorption, extracorporeal support, obesity, pediatrics, interactions, or suspected toxicity/failure.

Core element 7 - Reporting and feedback

AFS should measure actual exposure rather than expenditures alone, using DOT or DDD with appropriate denominators, and should benchmark only against comparable facilities and harmonized data sources. Because antifungal use is much less frequent than antibacterial use, small numbers can make aggregate trends unstable; in oncology units, continuous prophylaxis can conversely obscure clinically meaningful variation.

Programs should therefore combine consumption metrics with process and patient-level outcomes and provide direct feedback to prescribers. The statement specifically warns that hospital size, patient mix, route capture, perioperative documentation, and purchasing-versus-administration data can invalidate comparisons. This is a strong methodological section because it treats measurement design as part of stewardship rather than as post hoc reporting.

Conclusions

The paper's central claim is that AFS should be a structured, explicit extension of antimicrobial stewardship, but with added diagnostic, PK/TDM, specialty-collaboration, surveillance, and measurement requirements. It does not present one universal staffing model or intervention package; instead, it provides a modular framework that should be tailored to local IFD burden, expertise, and resources.

Its research agenda makes the evidence gaps explicit: staffing thresholds, sustained education, telehealth, diagnostic stewardship, AI/NLP, treatment-stop rules, ambulatory AFS, patient-level audit timing, resistance prediction, quality scores, and failure analysis remain unsettled. The statement is therefore best read as a mature program-design framework with uneven evidentiary depth across individual recommendations.

2Develop ongoing collaboration with the clinicians who most often manage IFD, including specialty champions and recurring clinical rounds.

Core element 3 - Expertise in infection management

#Recommendation, condensed
1Centers frequently managing IFD should have timely access to conventional and non-culture-based diagnostics for Candida and Aspergillus.

Core element 4 - Education and practical training

#Recommendation, condensed
1Use targeted education within a multifaceted AFS program to address interpretation of microbiology, colonization versus infection, prophylaxis versus empiric therapy, and antifungal dosing/monitoring.

Core element 5 - Actions for responsible use

#Recommendation, condensed
1When possible, obtain infectious diseases consultation for major IFDs such as fungemia, invasive aspergillosis, mucormycosis, and cryptococcal meningitis.
2Develop local guidelines, care pathways, or treatment bundles that sequence diagnostic and therapeutic actions at the point of care.
3Make ongoing post-prescription review/feedback or handshake stewardship a core intervention.
4Systematically evaluate antifungal prescribing quality and use the resulting data to refine interventions.
5Develop and evaluate tools that increase AFS impact while reducing resource burden.

Core element 6 - Monitoring and surveillance

#Recommendation, condensed
1Establish or adapt local surveillance systems for fungal infections.
2Provide timely antifungal susceptibility testing in centers that routinely manage IFD.
3Where susceptibility testing is routine, develop cumulative antifungal susceptibility reports despite the limited AFS-specific evidence base.
4Promote rational diagnostic testing and communicate culture and non-culture test results to the AFS team for real-time intervention.
5Screen medication records for antifungal drug interactions, including when concomitant therapies are started or stopped.
6Provide timely triazole therapeutic drug monitoring in centers routinely managing IFD.

Core element 7 - Reporting and feedback

#Recommendation, condensed
1Track antifungal drug use.
2Use benchmarking when comparable data are available.
3Assess patient-level outcomes where feasible, not only drug consumption and process measures.
4Feed AFS data directly back to prescribers in an interpretable format.

Table 3 - Sample care bundles for invasive candidiasis and invasive aspergillosis

Invasive candidiasis - at start

  • Obtain 2 high-volume blood cultures (40 mL) before therapy.
  • Remove existing central venous catheters within 24 h of diagnosis when appropriate.
  • Start an appropriate antifungal within 12 h of culture, considering local epidemiology and dosing.
  • Ophthalmologic examination within the first week of diagnosis (see currency note below).

After start

  • Daily follow-up blood cultures until clearance.
  • Echocardiography for persistent fungemia, fever, or new cardiac symptoms.
  • Reassess efficacy at 3-5 days and reconsider therapy when identification/susceptibility data are available.
  • Continue therapy at least 2 weeks after blood-culture clearance, longer with organ involvement.
  • Step down to oral fluconazole when the course is favorable and susceptibility is documented.

Invasive aspergillosis - at start

  • Repeat serum galactomannan twice in patients not on mold-active azole prophylaxis.
  • CT of chest and/or sinus/brain guided by symptoms.
  • Early bronchoscopy (within 48 h) with BAL cytology/culture and BAL galactomannan; transbronchial biopsy if feasible.
  • Select and dose initial antifungal therapy using prior exposure and local epidemiology.
  • Systematically screen drug interactions whenever a triazole is started or stopped.

After start

  • Periodic serum galactomannan as an adjunct response marker when applicable.
  • TDM for voriconazole and posaconazole, and possibly isavuconazole, to document exposure.
  • Reassess appropriateness using microbiology/culture/histology.
  • Repeat chest CT after ~3-4 weeks and thereafter according to response.
  • Step down to an oral triazole in a favorable clinical course.

The table functions as a workflow rather than a complete protocol: it sequences critical early diagnostics/therapy and follow-up reassessment. It should be locally updated against current disease-specific guidelines before implementation.

Targeted current-practice verification (2026)

This section is external verification, not part of the 2020 source.

Disease-specific IDSA guidance

The official IDSA practice-guideline pages still present the 2016 candidiasis and 2016 aspergillosis guidelines. This reinforces the value of the 2020 statement as a stewardship framework, but also means its disease-specific examples should be cross-checked rather than assumed to reflect a newer IDSA update.

Sources: https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/candidiasis/ and https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/aspergillosis/

Ophthalmology conflict

Table 3 includes routine ophthalmologic examination within the first week of candidemia. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's 2021 recommendation does not support routine ophthalmologic consultation after systemic Candida septicemia and instead recommends evaluation when ocular signs or symptoms are present. This is a concrete example of why bundle elements require current multidisciplinary reconciliation.

Source: American Academy of Ophthalmology Recommendations on Screening for Endogenous Candida Endophthalmitis. Ophthalmology. 2021. DOI 10.1016/j.ophtha.2021.07.015.

Candida auris relevance has increased, not decreased

CDC guidance updated in 2026 continues to emphasize C. auris as a highly transmissible, frequently resistant healthcare pathogen; current CDC material recommends susceptibility testing for clinical cases and notes increasing echinocandin- or pan-resistant reports. The source's emphasis on surveillance, species identification, susceptibility testing, and stewardship remains highly relevant.

Source: CDC Clinical Overview of Candida auris, updated Feb 26, 2026: https://www.cdc.gov/candida-auris/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html

Implementation / practice takeaways

Minimum viable AFS

  • Named accountability inside AMS.
  • Access to ID/pharmacy/mycology expertise.
  • Targeted antifungal review with post-prescription feedback.
  • Drug-interaction screening.
  • Institutional pathways for high-volume/high-risk use.
  • Local IFD surveillance and basic antifungal-utilization reporting.

Next maturity layer

  • Rapid diagnostic results routed to stewards.
  • Timely susceptibility testing and antifungal antibiograms/CASRs where data are adequate.
  • Timely TDM with PK-informed dose adjustment.
  • Prospective bundle adherence metrics.
  • Patient-level outcomes and balancing measures.

Good CDS targets

  • Diagnostic-test appropriateness by host risk/pretest probability.
  • Triazole interaction and start/stop alerts with pharmacist review.
  • TDM eligibility and follow-up workflow.
  • Culture/biomarker result routing to stewardship.
  • Candidemia/aspergillosis bundle checklists with explicit versioning.
  • Antifungal-use outlier detection normalized for unit/patient mix.

What not to automate blindly

  • Biomarker-positive = treat logic without pretest probability.
  • Automatic discontinuation based on one negative test.
  • Uncontextualized antifungal-use benchmarking.
  • Static bundle elements that are not reconciled with current specialty guidance.
  • Drug-interaction alerts without a management pathway.
Bottom line: The paper is most valuable as an architecture document for AFS. Its durable contribution is the closed-loop model - expertise + intervention + surveillance + measurement + feedback - rather than any single 2020 treatment detail.
