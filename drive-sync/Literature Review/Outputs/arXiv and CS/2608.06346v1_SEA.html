One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first for agent-debugging and reliability research.

TRAJDEBUG is a strong systems paper because it reframes critical-error localization as an error lifecycle: detect evidence-grounded triggers, cluster them around the violated reference object, determine whether they are repaired or terminally relevant, and only then perform causal attribution. Its strongest contributions are the explicit state model, a realistic long-horizon benchmark extension, extensive ablations/error analysis, and a demonstration that diagnoses can be converted into repair hints or reusable failure memory.

Core thesisFailure diagnosis improves when local errors are grounded in citable evidence and tracked through resolution/terminal impact rather than judged holistically over a long trajectory.
Primary resultTRAJDEBUG reaches 34.11% macro-average exact-step accuracy versus 25.69% for direct prompting with the same Qwen3 backbone (+8.42 points).
Long-horizon resultOn SWE-Bench Pro (119.7 steps average), exact-step accuracy is 24.41%; the paper reports more graceful degradation than baselines as trajectory length grows.
Application resultPer-trajectory repair improves average success from 78.07% to 88.87% (+10.80 points); held-out failure-memory transfer improves by 5.70 points on average.
Major strengthAblations isolate multi-granularity compression, trigger grounding, and state classification; additional compute-matched and candidate-retention analyses clarify where gains come from.
Major constraintAbsolute exact-step accuracy remains low and the pipeline is expensive: Table 10 reports ~1.38M tokens per trajectory, about 40× direct prompting.
Independent appraisal — most important caution. TRAJERRBENCH labels are human-majority labels, but annotators were shown pre-annotations from GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.6 plus benchmark failure details. Model–human exact-match agreement reaches 76.8% for Gemini on τ²-Bench. The paper measures agreement but does not establish how much those suggestions anchored the final labels. SWE-Bench Pro additionally retains 86 of 110 initially annotated trajectories only when at least two annotators agree, reducing coverage of the most ambiguous failures.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract + 1. Introduction

Source report. The paper frames critical error detection as finding the earliest decisive error that is causally linked to terminal failure, not merely the first local mistake or the error nearest the end. It argues that long trajectories create two problems: evidence needed to judge a step can be scattered across distant context, and failed trajectories contain many local errors that may later be repaired, remain dormant, or propagate.

The proposed solution is TRAJDEBUG, an evidence-grounded lifecycle framework paired with TRAJERRBENCH, a new 486-trajectory benchmark from τ²-Bench and SWE-Bench Pro. The paper claims best overall benchmark performance and reports downstream success gains when diagnoses are used as feedback.

2. Pilot Study

Source report. The task formulation follows the decisive-error counterfactual: a step is decisive if correcting its action, while holding prior steps fixed and allowing the remainder to proceed correctly, would turn failure into success; the critical error is the earliest decisive step. Across existing benchmarks, average model localization accuracy declines as trajectory length grows.

In a 50-trajectory sample containing 1,373 steps, annotators identify 381 local errors (7.62 per failed trajectory). Excluding the 50 critical errors leaves 331 non-critical errors: 205 (61.9%) are later repaired, 104 (31.4%) persist to the end, and 22 (6.6%) remain unrepaired but dormant. This directly motivates modeling downstream state rather than treating every detected error as a critical candidate.

3. TRAJDEBUG

Source report. The method first creates high-, medium-, and low-detail trajectory views so local verification can retain precise evidence while distant history is compressed. Error triggers are then detected as explicit conflicts between a current wrong commitment and a verbatim reference from the task, visible history, the same step, or an anomalous environment response. Triggers also receive an execution-phase label (planning, reasoning, action, observation, verification).

Related triggers are clustered by the concrete violated reference object. Each instance is classified by whether it is resolved and whether it leaves a terminal footprint, yielding Clean Resolution, Costly Resolution, Manifest Active, or Latent Active. Only Costly Resolution and Manifest Active instances are retained for final candidate-guided causal attribution.

4. TRAJERRBENCH

Source report. TRAJERRBENCH contains 400 failed τ²-Bench trajectories and 86 failed SWE-Bench Pro trajectories, averaging 29.3 and 119.7 steps. Three annotators independently label each trajectory; majority agreement determines the released critical step. Reported Fleiss’ κ is 0.91 for τ²-Bench and 0.67 for retained SWE-Bench Pro trajectories.

Critical errors tend to occur mid-to-late in these benchmark subsets. Reasoning is the dominant error phase (60.7% τ²-Bench; 57.1% SWE-Bench Pro). τ²-Bench is nearly balanced between Task Conflict (52.4%) and History Conflict (46.3%); SWE-Bench Pro is more task-constraint dominated (73.4% Task Conflict; 24.1% History Conflict).

5. Experiments

Source report. Evaluation spans WhoAndWhen, AgentDebugBench, τ²-Bench, and SWE-Bench Pro, for 869 evaluated trajectories total. Exact critical-step accuracy is the primary metric. Direct prompting is instantiated with seven advanced models; AgentDebugger, CHIEF, AgentRX, and TRAJDEBUG use Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking as the common backbone for multi-agent/pipeline comparison, with temperature 0.

TRAJDEBUG achieves the best macro-average exact-step accuracy (34.11%) and ranks first on five of seven subsets. Removing multi-granularity compression produces the largest ablation drop (38.58 → 17.56 average on τ²-Bench + SWE-Bench Pro); removing trigger grounding or state classification also lowers performance. Length analysis shows all methods degrade, but TRAJDEBUG retains >20% accuracy in the longest bucket while the best baseline is described as ~14%.

6. Application: feedback for agent improvement

Source report. In per-trajectory repair, failed trajectories are diagnosed and the resulting feedback is injected into GLM-5.1 before re-running the same task. TRAJDEBUG raises average success from 78.07% to 88.87%, outperforming vanilla debugging and self-reflection.

In a more realistic transfer setting, one-third of trajectories form a failure-memory pool and two-thirds are held out. TRAJDEBUG yields improvements of +6.06, +6.57, and +4.48 points for airline, retail, and SWE-Bench respectively, whereas comparison methods are less stable and sometimes reduce success.

7–10. Related work, conclusion, limitations, ethics

Source report. The paper positions itself between step-level process supervision, taxonomy/constraint debugging, graph/causal attribution, and replay/spectrum approaches. Its claimed distinction is explicit lifecycle state tracking with verbatim evidence. The conclusion presents critical error detection as an interface for inference-time improvement.

Authors acknowledge dependence on LLM reasoning/calibration and pipeline false negatives. They recommend manual verification for high-stakes use. Ethical notes cover intellectual property, anonymization intent, MIT release plans, and AI-assisted wording; funding is reported from the 2025 Tencent Rhino-Bird Joint Research Program.

Appendix A–B. Annotation and benchmark details

Source report. Pilot local-error annotation uses stringent evidence requirements and unanimous agreement, yielding moderate Fleiss’ κ (0.538) for local-error identification and lower agreement (0.378) for downstream-state labels. TRAJERRBENCH annotation uses three-model pre-annotations plus full task/failure information to aid humans; final critical-step labels use human majority vote.

SWE-Bench Pro initially had 110 annotated trajectories, but experiments use 86 with at least two-of-three agreement. The appendices quantify agreement, error phases, subtypes, and conflict categories. This is valuable transparency, but the filtering means the released SWE subset underrepresents cases where critical-step attribution itself is maximally ambiguous.

Appendix C. Case study

Source report. A 49-step airline trajectory contains 15 triggers clustered into 9 instances, 4 of which remain candidates. TRAJDEBUG and human annotators both select step 25: the agent commits to upgrading only one flight despite a reservation-level uniform-cabin policy.

The example demonstrates the intended distinction between earlier but recoverable/costly mistakes and a later terminally relevant constraint violation. Subsequent payment retries are treated as downstream manifestations rather than the root cause.

Appendix D–E. Implementation and additional experiments

Source report. Compression thresholds are th1 ≤3000 characters, th2 ≤1200, th3 ≤600. The paper publishes detailed prompts for compression, trigger annotation, instance clustering, state labeling, and causal attribution. Direct prompting truncates each step to 2,048 characters and caps trajectory context at 16,384 tokens; increasing the per-step cap to 4,096 or 8,192 reduces truncation but does not improve accuracy.

Supplementary analyses show 50.2% average accuracy in the asymmetric [GT−3, GT] window and 72.8% for TRAJDEBUG in the symmetric [GT−5, GT+5] window. Table 10 documents high inference cost; Table 12 shows the true critical step is retained in the candidate set in 42.0% of trajectories; Table 13 attributes remaining failures mostly to trigger misses (42.1%) and attribution misses (40.1%), with state misses at 17.7%.

Method and workflow reconstruction

Failed trajectory→Multi-granularity compression→Evidence-grounded triggers→Reference-object clustering→Lifecycle state→Candidate set F(τ)→Causal attribution
Trigger evidence contractA trigger requires a wrong-content quote and a reference quote. Categories: Task Conflict, History Conflict, Intra-Step Conflict, Environment Anomaly. This is designed to suppress unsupported “the agent seems wrong” diagnoses.
State modelClean Resolution = repaired/no footprint; Costly Resolution = repaired but recovery consumes >50% of trajectory; Manifest Active = active with irreversible/semantic footprint; Latent Active = active without downstream footprint.
Candidate ruleOnly Costly Resolution and Manifest Active instances enter F(τ). Final selection is not simply the earliest candidate; the attribution head compares terminal responsibility.
Implementation detailLocal trigger inspection uses high-detail current/local context, medium-detail near history, and low-detail distant history. State/attribution stages selectively expand relevant steps while falling back to compressed views.
Important modeling choice. “Critical” is operationalized as an earliest decisive, failure-responsible step. That is useful for debugging, but it is still a benchmark label derived from counterfactual human judgment rather than an experimentally executed intervention on every trajectory.

Average critical-step localization accuracy falls as trajectory length increases, while local-error density rises. Pilot annotation finds 7.62 local errors per failed trajectory; among 331 non-critical errors, 61.9% are repaired, 31.4% persist, and 6.6% are dormant.

Figure 3 — TRAJDEBUG framework

The central workflow: detect task/history/intra-step conflicts, cluster by reference object, classify lifecycle state, retain terminal-relevant candidates, then select the critical step. This is the paper’s most reusable architectural contribution.

Figure 4 — Critical errors often occur later in TRAJERRBENCH

The heatmap shows τ²-Bench and especially SWE-Bench Pro shifted toward mid/late critical errors relative to several older benchmarks, consistent with more extensive information gathering before a decisive failure.

Figure 5 — Accuracy degrades with trajectory length

All systems degrade sharply across length buckets. TRAJDEBUG stays above 20% in the longest 141+ bucket, while the strongest baseline is described as roughly 14%, supporting the claim of better long-context robustness without eliminating the length problem.

Figure 6 — τ²-Bench failure anatomy

Among agreed labels, reasoning dominates (60.7%), followed by planning (14.8%) and observation (13.5%). The leading subtypes are InvalidInference, WrongChoice, and MissingAssumptionCheck; Task Conflict and History Conflict are nearly balanced.

Figure 7 — SWE-Bench Pro failure anatomy

Reasoning is again dominant (57.1%), with more verification/action involvement than τ²-Bench. WrongChoice is the most prominent subtype, and Task Conflict (73.4%) substantially exceeds History Conflict (24.1%).

Evaluation and appraisal

9/10
Relevance

High relevance to long-horizon agent reliability, trace debugging, and feedback-loop design.

Evidence basis: directly targets failure attribution in tool-use and coding agents. Limit: not a general agent safety evaluation. Would rise: production traces and broader operational validation.
8/10
Novelty

The lifecycle-state decomposition is a useful contribution beyond “flag errors then ask an LLM to pick one.”

Evidence basis: explicit reference-object clustering, repair state, terminal footprint, candidate attribution. Limit: components build on known prompting, compression, taxonomy, and causal-debugging ideas. Would rise: stronger formal causal validation.
7/10
Method strength

Broad benchmark suite, ablations, compute-matched comparison, detailed prompts, and stage-wise error analysis are strong.

Evidence basis: seven subsets, 869 trajectories, multiple baseline families. Limit: no statistical uncertainty/significance reporting; human labels are model-assisted; direct prompting is capped at 16,384 tokens. Would rise: long-context matched baselines, blinded annotation, CIs/repeated runs.
7/10
Evidence strength

Results consistently support an advantage, but the absolute diagnosis rate remains modest.

Evidence basis: 34.11% exact-step macro-average, 50.2% [GT−3, GT], ablation and repair gains. Limit: candidate retains the gold step only 42.0% of the time; no uncertainty estimates. Would rise: independent replication and intervention-based causal validation.
6/10
External validity

Diverse benchmarks help, especially tool use and software engineering, but these are still benchmark rollouts.

Evidence basis: embodied, web, information-seeking, customer-service, coding tasks. Limit: specific datasets/models; no production deployment or broader language/domain sampling. Would rise: live systems, organizations, and independent model families.
8/10
Implementation value

The paper is unusually buildable: prompts, thresholds, state definitions, case study, and failure decomposition are explicit.

Evidence basis: Appendix D/E specification and repository link. Limit: ~1.38M tokens/trajectory is expensive and release availability is not verified here. Would rise: cheaper implementation, latency/cost benchmarks, released reproducible artifacts.

What the evidence supports

  • A structured lifecycle pipeline outperforms holistic direct prompting on the reported benchmark suite.
  • Multi-granularity history handling is a major contributor to performance in the tested long trajectories.
  • Error-state filtering is particularly useful on SWE-Bench Pro, where many detected errors can be non-terminal or repaired.
  • Better localized diagnoses can improve re-execution and can be aggregated into transferable failure memory in the tested application setup.

What the evidence does not establish

  • Production-grade root-cause accuracy: exact-step macro-average is only 34.11%.
  • Low-cost deployability: the reported inference budget is ~40× a direct prompt.
  • Unbiased ground truth: TRAJERRBENCH annotation is model-assisted, and the hardest SWE-Bench Pro disagreements are excluded from the retained benchmark.
  • General causal validity: “decisive error” is a counterfactual annotation construct, not a systematically executed intervention for every trajectory.
  • Superiority under fully long-context direct-prompt baselines: the direct baseline is restricted to a 16,384-token trajectory context, while the proposed method repeatedly accesses compressed views.

Numerical and reporting checks

Comparator ambiguity in §5.2. The narrative states +13.00 points on ALFWorld and +6.97 on SWE-Bench Pro “over corresponding direct baselines.” Table 1 gives TRAJDEBUG = 26.00 on ALFWorld while direct-prompting scores range 9.00–19.00, so the possible difference is 7–17 points depending comparator. The +13 figure is not uniquely recoverable from the table and should be clarified. The SWE-Bench Pro +6.97 difference is consistent with 24.41 − 17.44.

Macro vs micro averages. Table 1’s 34.11% is a macro-average across seven subsets. Table 12’s 41.8% is a trajectory-level micro-average. The paper explicitly distinguishes these, so they should not be compared as if they were the same metric.

Bias / validity audit

Annotation anchoringThree strong models provide pre-annotations visible to human annotators; high exact-match rates make anchoring plausible even though no model uniformly matches labels.
Ambiguity selectionOnly 86/110 SWE-Bench Pro trajectories with majority critical-step agreement are retained, improving label reliability but narrowing the benchmark away from highly ambiguous cases.
Context baseline fairnessDirect prompting uses a 16,384-token maximum. Table 8 varies per-step truncation but does not test a fully long-context direct baseline.
UncertaintyNo confidence intervals, bootstrap intervals, statistical tests, or multi-seed variance are reported for accuracy/success-rate differences.
Contamination / leakageNo explicit benchmark-contamination analysis is reported for the evaluated foundation models.
Funding / affiliationThe work includes Tencent Hunyuan authors and Tencent Rhino-Bird funding; this is disclosed in the source, but no separate conflict-of-interest analysis is reported.

Implementation and research takeaways

1. Preserve reference objectsFor debugging, store not just “error type” but the exact violated constraint, observation, or prior commitment. That gives later stages a stable object for repair-state tracking.
2. Separate local error from terminal relevanceDo not escalate every wrong step. Track whether it was explicitly repaired, whether later behavior depends on it, and whether it leaves an irreversible or semantic footprint.
3. Use hierarchical contextKeep high fidelity around the step under judgment and compress distant history, but preserve constraints, IDs, tool outputs, and unresolved commitments across all compression levels.
4. Make candidate loss observableInstrument trigger miss, state-filter miss, and attribution miss separately. TRAJDEBUG’s own analysis shows trigger recall and final attribution are the largest remaining bottlenecks.
5. Treat failure memory as a reusable artifactThe transfer experiment suggests concise diagnosis-derived rules may outperform generic self-reflection when carried to held-out tasks.
6. Budget the diagnostic systemAccuracy gains come with large token cost. A production implementation should profile latency, cost, caching, retrieval, and whether compression can be made deterministic or cheaper.

Trajectory → Evidence-backed Trigger[]

Best use: architecture for auditable agent postmortems, error taxonomy experiments, long-horizon evaluation, and failure-memory research.
