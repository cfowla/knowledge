One-page synthesis

Core thesis. A frontier model with strong static medical knowledge can acquire better procedural clinical behavior—especially information gathering, management planning, communication, and resistance to premature closure—by optimizing complete simulated encounters rather than isolated answers.

ResidencyRL initializes from Gemini 3.5 Flash and trains with online Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) over a heterogeneous action space: up to 60 dialogue turns plus 8 structured clinical/documentation tool calls. Training uses 49,870 general telehealth scenarios, 5,000 targeted history-taking scenarios, and 2,583 adversarial safety scenarios (83%/15%/2%). An LLM-based patient simulator exposes progressively disclosed information, behavioral variation, and adversarial resistance. A structured Gemini 3.1 Pro autorater supplies trajectory-level reward across diagnostic accuracy, management, intake, communication, documentation, style, and critical safety penalties.

The strongest evidence is not the in-domain reward-aligned evaluation but the repeated transfer pattern. On AMIE Mx, ResidencyRL improves management reasoning from 80.1% to 88.4%, patient communication from 83.7% to 92.2%, and clinical skills from 79.6% to 84.8%. In a separate clinician-curated telehealth harness, blinded physicians prefer the trained model in 87.6% of 97 overall comparisons, including a 90.7% win rate for completeness of information gathering. Specialist oncology evaluations also favor the trained agent on completeness, clinical accuracy, actionability, and overall quality. External AgentClinic and CRAFT-MD results move mostly in the expected direction, although individual differences there are not statistically significant.

Methods

Training environment and scenario curriculum

The scenario pipeline samples demographic, behavioral, and clinical attributes; generates detailed cases with Gemini 3.1 Pro; performs LLM-as-judge coherence review; and deduplicates patient summaries with character-trigram TF-IDF using a cosine-similarity threshold of 0.9. Presenting complaints span 81 conditions across respiratory/cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, genitourinary/reproductive, gastrointestinal, and other primary-care/telehealth domains. Complexity levels 1–5 progressively add comorbidities, atypical symptom overlap, medication interactions, social confounders, and broader differentials.

Two targeted extension packs shape the policy toward failure modes the authors consider clinically important. History-taking cases hide a diagnostic pivot until the model asks a sufficiently specific question (social/lifestyle, medication specifics, symptom characterization, or exposures). Adversarial safety cases span nine categories including emergency escalation, boundary enforcement, atypical presentations, pharmacologic limits, abuse/vulnerability recognition, age verification, autonomy, crisis de-escalation, and resistance to patient manipulation. Adversarial difficulty levels determine how persistently the simulator withholds or distorts critical information.

Agent action space and patient simulator

The environment combines natural-language conversation with a structured clinical API: chart review, diagnosis, ranked differential, urgency, management plan, patient-facing summary, SOAP documentation, and termination. This makes the trajectory broader than history-taking alone. The patient simulator is Gemini 3.5 Flash conditioned on the scenario and encounter history, with instructions governing health literacy, reactive disclosure, pacing, and adversarial behavior. Tool calls, the policy’s internal reasoning, and the evaluation rubric are hidden from the patient simulator.

Reward design

R = R_primary − R_penalty, bounded to [−3, 3]
R_primary = 3 × (2/9 diagnosis + 3/9 management + 1/9 communication
                 + 1/9 documentation + 1/9 intake + 1/9 style)

Twenty-six Likert sub-axes are split across topic-specific Gemini 3.1 Pro judge calls. Management receives the largest weight (3/9), followed by diagnosis (2/9); communication, documentation, intake, and style each receive 1/9. Critical penalties include hallucinated clinical data (−2), contraindicated actions (up to −3), plan leakage, missed critical questions, under-triage, and missed red flags. Deterministic system penalties cover parsing failures, missing termination, and excessive conversational length.

Reinforcement learning

The policy is optimized with online multi-turn GRPO from Gemini 3.5 Flash. Each scenario receives parallel episodes, the complete trajectory is scored only after completion, and group-relative advantages update the model without a separate value function. Training behavior shifts toward longer and more computationally intensive encounters: median conversational length rises from approximately 18 to 23 turns by step 100, and total agent steps stabilize near 52.

Reproducibility boundary. The paper describes the environment, reward structure, scenario mixture, evaluation instruments, and GRPO conceptually, but does not provide the training codebase. Exact internal implementation and infrastructure are proprietary, and standard training details such as a fully reproducible hyperparameter/compute recipe are not exposed.

4.3 Specialist oncology transfer

Three hundred clinician-curated oncology scenarios cover 240 solid-tumor cases across 21 cancer types and 60 hematologic malignancy cases across 10 subtypes. The agent was not trained on oncology or specialist workflows. Automated head-to-head grading favors ResidencyRL on completeness (34.8% wins vs 10.5% baseline), clinical accuracy (26.0% vs 9.1%), actionability (21.6% vs 7.8%), and the overall composite (42.9% vs 18.6%), while differences in case relevance and safety/triage are uncommon and not significant.

A blinded oncologist review of 100 cases reports more targeted histories, earlier red-flag identification, more systematic exposure/family/genetic-risk elicitation, and more explicit sequencing of diagnostic workup. A recurring weakness is bundling multiple questions into one turn, which reviewers note may overwhelm patients.

4.5 Clinician-curated AMIE Telehealth harness

In 299 out-of-domain clinician-designed scenarios, both models run inside the same expert-optimized AMIE Telehealth Harness originally tuned for the base model. Automated grading shows statistically significant improvements on 8 of 10 metrics after FDR correction. The largest deltas are Naturalness (2.52→3.24) and Overall Clinical Rubric Score (3.93→4.43). Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the work as a transition from static medical knowledge to sequential clinical process. It reports adversarial diagnostic accuracy improving from 81.0% to 88.0%, missed red flags decreasing by 31% relatively, and clinician preference of 87.6% in side-by-side evaluation. It also claims transfer across AMIE Mx, AgentClinic, and CRAFT-MD.

The abstract correctly includes the central caveat: prospective real-world workflow validation is still necessary before clinical utility can be established.

1. Introduction

The authors motivate clinical encounters as long-horizon partially observed decision problems where information must be actively elicited. They distinguish medical knowledge from clinical process and identify premature closure, adversarial manipulation, and patient communication as sequential failure modes poorly captured by static QA benchmarks.

The contribution is positioned around three claims: long-horizon multi-turn RL can improve complete encounter behavior; these improvements generalize across specialties and environments; and targeted training can reduce dangerous shortcuts such as missed red flags.

2. Related Work

The paper situates ResidencyRL between foundational medical LLMs, AMIE-style conversational systems, long-horizon agentic RL, and clinical RL systems such as Doctor-R1, DoctorAgent-RL, DiagAgent, and SALUS. Table 1 emphasizes that prior medical RL generally uses shorter horizons or narrower action spaces.

The authors’ key distinction is not merely dialogue length: ResidencyRL jointly optimizes inquiry, documentation, diagnosis, management, safety, and communication in a single trajectory.

3. Methods

Methods combine a generated/deduplicated scenario curriculum, a stateful patient simulator, structured clinical tools, an analytic multi-axis LLM autorater, and online GRPO. The scenario system intentionally creates information asymmetry and adversarial patient behavior so reward depends on asking the right questions rather than only producing a plausible final answer.

The reward design is unusually explicit for a clinical-agent paper, but the verifier is also the major source of validity risk: the same general family of automated judging signals both trains and evaluates part of the system.

4. Results

In-domain performance improves broadly, especially intake completeness, management, and adversarial safety. More persuasive are out-of-domain gains on AMIE Mx, oncology, and clinician-curated AMIE Telehealth, including blinded physician preferences. AgentClinic/CRAFT-MD mostly trend in the same direction but are not individually significant.

The result pattern is coherent with the proposed mechanism: gains are largest where active information gathering, management reasoning, communication, and free-form synthesis matter; static multiple-choice performance changes little.

5. Discussion, limitations & future directions

The authors interpret the transfer pattern as evidence for procedural generalization rather than memorization of domain facts. They explicitly acknowledge verifier Goodharting, metric saturation, simulator fidelity gaps, text-only US-English telehealth scope, lack of long-term patient outcomes, lack of multimodal/physical interaction, automated adversaries, and proprietary infrastructure.

Future directions include broader multi-actor clinical settings, EHR-grounded and multimodal simulation, simulation of downstream treatment effects, multi-visit training, and stronger verification strategies. These are not minor extensions: several correspond directly to the main barriers between benchmark competence and deployable clinical systems.

6. Conclusion

The paper concludes that simulation-based multi-turn RL can make a medically knowledgeable frontier model more effective at clinical-process tasks. The strongest source-supported form of that conclusion is “more competent in the tested simulated and benchmark settings,” not “validated as a clinician.”

Data availability is partial: several benchmark datasets are open, while the actual codebase and internal training infrastructure are not released because of internal dependencies and stated medical-safety concerns.

Table 1 — Comparison with prior clinical simulation/RL systems

ResidencyRL is differentiated by a horizon up to 68 actions, mixed dialogue + documentation tools, online GRPO, approximately 57K generated cases including adversarial scenarios, and an objective spanning complete diagnostic consultation and management rather than a narrow order/query subtask.

Table 2 — Reward structure

Management is weighted 3/9, diagnosis 2/9, and intake/communication/documentation/style each 1/9. Critical safety violations can dominate reward through penalties up to 3 points, creating an explicit safety-sensitive objective rather than treating safety as an unweighted secondary rubric.

Table 3 — Evaluation overview

The evaluation suite covers 200 held-out telehealth cases, 200 held-out adversarial cases, 120 AMIE Mx cases (360 visits), 300 oncology cases, AgentClinic-MedQA (215), AgentClinic-MIMIC-IV (200), CRAFT-MD (1,273), and 299 clinician-curated AMIE Telehealth cases with a 97-case valid human SxS subset.

Table 4 — In-domain evaluation

Broad improvements appear across management, communication, and screening completeness, with the notable exception that under-triage does not improve. Safety gains are largest for information omission (critical questions/red flags), not for every unsafe-action endpoint.

Table 6 — AgentClinic / CRAFT-MD

Results are directionally favorable in the more conversational/free-response settings but do not individually reach significance. Visit multiple-choice accuracy is slightly lower after training. This table appropriately tempers the stronger transfer claims from AMIE and clinician review.

High-value appendix findings

Appendix A — Training coverage and complexity

Table A1 enumerates 81 presenting complaints across five broad domains. The set includes routine respiratory/cardiovascular complaints, musculoskeletal issues, reproductive/genitourinary care, gastrointestinal problems, and a wide “Other” bucket spanning mental health, metabolic disease, dermatology, refills, and general telehealth complaints. Table A2 increases difficulty from textbook cases (level 1) to high-comorbidity, actively misleading, polypharmacy-heavy, socially confounded cases (level 5).

Appendix B — Autorater specification (Tables A3–A6)

The appendix exposes the rubric in enough detail to understand the reward target: six weighted axes, sub-axis weights, dimension-specific 1–5 anchors, and eight safety/error flags. This is a major methodological strength because “clinical quality” is decomposed into auditable criteria rather than represented as a single opaque scalar.

Appendix C.7 / Figure A1 — Autorater calibration and bias

Critical calibration result. Cases clinicians preferred for ResidencyRL had larger autorater deltas than cases they did not prefer (median +0.61 vs +0.31; p<0.01), so the autorater contains meaningful relative signal. However, autorater deltas were systematically positive even when clinicians did not favor the trained model; the mean overall delta was +0.63. The authors attribute this plausibly to optimization against similar automated signals during training.

This appendix materially changes interpretation: automated effect sizes should not be treated as unbiased estimates of true clinical preference. The human SxS result is therefore essential rather than decorative validation.

Appendix D — Oncology grading

The oncology set uses specialist-written reference management plans and six weighted 1–5 axes: clinical accuracy (25%), safety/triage (20%), case relevance (15%), completeness (15%), actionability/specialist value (15%), and clarity/calibration (10%). This creates a clinically oriented transfer test rather than simply reusing the training reward unchanged.

The first case illustrates resistance to an implausible, patient-demanded surgical framing: the trained model probes the claim and persists with safety checks rather than anchoring on the patient’s self-diagnosis. The second case is a TIA presentation in which the baseline escalates quickly but terminates after minimal history and later fabricates past medical history/medications in the SOAP note. These cases are useful for failure-mode understanding, but they are selected illustrations. Their evidentiary role is explanatory rather than quantitative.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first. High-value work for clinical-agent training, evaluation, and safety methodology. Treat it as strong evidence that multi-turn simulation RL can improve simulated clinical process, not as evidence that the resulting model is ready for independent patient care.

Implementation value

The scenario, reward, safety, and evaluation architecture is highly reusable conceptually.

Limit: proprietary code/models/infrastructure prevent direct reproduction. Would raise: open environment, reward code, fixtures, and reproducible GRPO recipe.

Main strengths

  • Evaluation triangulation: reward-aligned held-out tests, separate OOD benchmarks, specialist transfer, an independent agentic harness, and blinded clinician review.
  • Reward transparency: clinically meaningful axes and explicit safety penalties rather than a single opaque preference score.
  • Failure-mode targeting: hidden-information and adversarial scenario packs directly train against premature closure and incomplete history-taking.
  • Mechanistic coherence: the largest gains occur where the training intervention should matter—information gathering, management reasoning, communication, and free-response synthesis.
  • Self-critique in the paper: the authors quantify verifier bias rather than ignoring it.

Main limitations

  • Simulation-to-clinic gap: no real patient outcomes, physical examination, multimodal data, procedures, treatment-response observation, or team coordination.
  • Verifier entanglement: similar LLM-mediated scoring signals are used for training and multiple evaluations; Appendix C.7 confirms a positive shift toward the trained model.
  • External benchmark uncertainty: AgentClinic and CRAFT-MD improvements are directional but individually nonsignificant.
  • Residual safety failures: missed critical questions remain 43.5% and missed red flags 31.5% in the adversarial set; under-triage does not improve.
  • Reproducibility: proprietary models/infrastructure, no released codebase, and an incomplete public training recipe constrain replication.
  • Population/environment scope: US, English, text-based telehealth scenarios only.
Do not overclaim: “87.6% clinician preference” is a preference between two simulated-agent encounter outputs inside a specific harness, not a comparison with practicing clinicians and not a patient-outcome endpoint.

Implementation / research takeaways

1. Train the process, not just the answerFor agentic CDS or clinical assistants, optimize complete trajectories that include elicitation, decision points, documentation, and safety-netting.
2. Build capability-specific scenario packsGeneral-case simulation alone is unlikely to train rare but safety-critical behaviors. Hidden-history and adversarial packs act as targeted curricula.
3. Keep reward multidimensionalSeparate diagnosis, management, intake, communication, documentation, style, and hard safety penalties so one high-performing axis cannot mask a dangerous failure elsewhere.
4. Evaluate beyond the training harnessReward-aligned scores should be treated as optimization diagnostics. External environments and human review are needed to assess transfer.
5. Audit the verifier explicitlyMeasure how automated score deltas relate to independent experts and look for systematic shift after RL. This paper’s positive-bias finding is an important model for verifier governance.
6. Track residual failure ratesRelative improvements can coexist with unacceptable absolute safety failure rates. A 31% red-flag reduction still leaves nearly one-third of adversarial red flags missed.

Best use

Architecture/reference paper for clinical-agent simulation, long-horizon RL, structured reward design, safety curriculum generation, and evaluation governance.

Do not use for

Claims of autonomous clinical deployment readiness, real-world patient safety, superiority to physicians, or validated treatment effectiveness.

High-value next experiments suggested by the evidence

  • Prospective real-patient or standardized-human-patient evaluation with independent adjudication.
  • Verifier-independent endpoints and cross-family judges to quantify reward-model overfitting.
  • Ablations by horizon length, adversarial-pack inclusion, reward-axis removal, and safety-penalty weighting.
  • Multimodal/EHR-grounded environments with actual test results and treatment-response transitions.
  • Open or reproducible smaller-scale environment to test whether the method generalizes beyond Gemini/internal infrastructure.
