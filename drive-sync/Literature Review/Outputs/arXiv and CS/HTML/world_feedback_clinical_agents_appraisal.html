One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first for work on clinical agents, FHIR tool-use evaluation, deterministic verifier design, and RL/SFT training strategy. Do not use for practice; it is benchmark-methodology evidence, not clinical-care evidence.

Core thesis: Reinforcement learning from deterministic “world feedback” can be attractive for clinical protocol-execution tasks, but the feedback channel must be clean and the base model must already have enough task and format capability for reward variance to exist. The authors argue that prior MedAgentBench versions had a reward shortcut: a do-nothing agent could pass many no-action cases, making inaction an RL-dominant strategy.

Main contribution: The paper constructs MedAgentBench-v3 (MAB-v3), a 508-task corrected benchmark with a lower silent-finish ceiling, then uses Qwen3-8B experiments to show two barriers to pure RL: zero-gradient task types when the base model never succeeds, and flat landscapes when exact clinical codes are required but undiscoverable by exploration. The practical prescription is supervised fine-tuning to inject codes and FHIR format, followed by RL to learn conditional behavior.

Most useful ideaReport silent-finish/no-tool baselines and action/no-action splits before using a clinical agent benchmark as a training reward.
Most important limitationThe trained-model results come from one small base model, one held-out split, pure GRPO from scratch, one epoch, and no SFT+RL experiment.
Best reuseBenchmark audit checklist, verifier design pattern, reward-shaping caution, and taxonomy for RL-learnable vs SFT-needed tasks.
Do not overclaimDo not infer that RL is inferior to SFT generally, that MAB-v3 reflects live EHR deployment, or that frontier model scores are directly comparable with trained Qwen3-8B scores.

Section condensation

Abstract and Introduction

The paper frames clinical protocol execution - checking labs, applying thresholds, and issuing structured FHIR orders - as a natural setting for RL from world feedback. The promise is that clinical subject-matter experts encode the decision logic once into a verifier, after which unlimited rollouts can be graded automatically without per-episode human annotation.

The authors immediately narrow the claim. They do not address open-ended clinical reasoning; the target is administrative or standing-order execution. Their headline finding is diagnostic: MAB-v2 had a 41.7% silent-finish ceiling, corrected MAB-v3 reduces that to 8.9%, and Qwen3-8B pure RL reaches only 18.2% pass@1 compared with 34.1% for rule-based SFT.

Related Work

The source positions MedAgentBench, FHIR-AgentBench, and HealthAdminBench as adjacent clinical-agent evaluation work. Its claimed gap is not a new clinical task set alone, but the use of benchmark validity as a training-signal prerequisite.

The RL framing is borrowed from verifier-based RL successes in domains such as mathematics. The distinction here is that clinical FHIR tasks are heterogeneous: some tasks have learnable decision boundaries, while others require exact identifiers or lookup formats that do not provide a useful reward gradient.

MedAgentBench-v3: restoring the feedback signal

The authors identify four benchmark problems: branch imbalance, a high silent-finish ceiling, undocumented format requirements, and a wall-clock bug in CT follow-up grading. MAB-v3 applies context patches, freezes the problematic timestamp, labels silent-finish tasks across the source set, and caps no-action/action branches at 1:1 within corpus and task type.

The resulting MAB-v3 contains 508 tasks from 600 original MAB v1/v2 tasks: 463 action-required and 45 no-action instances across 20 task types and about 100 anonymized patients. This design lowers the do-nothing baseline from 41.7% to 8.9%, making inaction less attractive as a reward-hacking shortcut.

Methods: environment, SFT, and GRPO

The FHIR environment uses a fixed HAPI FHIR server snapshot, deterministic responses, and a rule-based verifier. Reward decomposes into terminal correctness, partial action credit, and penalties for spurious action or no-tool finishing. The authors use a native <tool call> function interface for trained models, while frontier baselines are evaluated in the official MAB harness.

SFT demos are generated programmatically by a rule-based agent and validated by the verifier; 354 of 401 training demonstrations pass. RL uses GRPO from the base Qwen3-8B model, not from SFT, with four generations per prompt, per-task-type advantage normalization, beta 0.05, temperature 1.8, max eight episode steps, and one epoch.

Results

On all 508 tasks in the official harness, frontier models achieve 50.4-78.7% pass@1 for most listed models, with GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro near 78%. No-action pass rates are generally higher than action pass rates, suggesting many models are better at abstention than correct clinical action.

On the 107-task held-out split, base Qwen3-8B achieves 16.6% pass@1; rule-based SFT reaches 34.1%; pure GRPO reaches 18.2%. The SFT/RL gap is interpreted as a format-knowledge and base-capability limitation rather than a failure of the general world-feedback concept.

Analysis and Discussion

The taxonomy divides tasks into decision tasks, lookup tasks, and format-knowledge tasks. Decision tasks can provide reward variance because correct and incorrect actions are connected to observable values. Lookup tasks can teach tool use and parsing but are usually more efficiently learned from demonstrations. Format-knowledge tasks require exact codes such as SNOMED or NDC identifiers, so wrong-code attempts all receive similar negative rewards and do not reveal the correct discrete target.

The authors conclude that SFT and RL are complementary: SFT should inject format, payload structure, and exact clinical codes; RL should refine conditional behavior and no-action calibration. They explicitly limit the work to protocol-execution tasks and note that the benchmark uses about 100 anonymized patients, explicit task instructions, and some per-type counts too small for stable inference.

Figure 2 - World-feedback loop for clinical FHIR agent training

Embedded crop from page 3 of the evaluated PDF.

Inputs: Clinical task, patient MRN, task type, instruction.
Policy: Qwen3-8B with LoRA generates native tool calls.
Environment: Offline HAPI FHIR server snapshot with read, write, utility, and finish tools.

Failure modes: Reward hacking through silent finish; zero-gradient task groups; flat landscape for exact-code tasks.

Reward equation

r = r_terminal + r_action + r_penalty
r_terminal = 1.0 if the verifier passes.
r_action ∈ {0.10, 0.25} for partial credit on resource type and POST structure.
r_spurious = -0.15 for off-target POST on a no-action task.
r_skip = -0.20 for finish with no tool use.
Appraisal: This is deliberate reward shaping. It creates gradients for early exploration and penalizes over-action, but it also means the training signal is partly engineered rather than purely clinical-world correctness.

Confidence in appraisal: Moderate-high for methodological interpretation; low for deployment implications.

Implementation and research takeaways

  1. Make no-tool baselines mandatory. Any clinical-agent benchmark intended for RL should report silent-finish and do-nothing performance before training begins.
  2. Split action and no-action results. Aggregate pass@1 can hide clinically important caution bias or over-action.
  3. Audit grader time dependencies. Wall-clock functions can silently change labels and corrupt training feedback.
  4. Document exact FHIR payload conventions. If the verifier requires a bare string, a two-element array, or a specific code system, the task context or demonstrations must expose that requirement.
  5. Use SFT for codes and payload schemas. Exact SNOMED/NDC identifiers are not discoverable through sparse reward unless the environment exposes the target in some other way.
  6. Use RL after format injection. The paper’s own results support RL as a conditional-behavior refinement method more than a from-scratch format-learning method.
  7. Track reward-variance dead zones. Report the fraction of task groups with zero reward standard deviation; all-fail rollouts do not train the model.
  8. Do not conflate benchmark passing with clinical safety. A passing FHIR POST in a static environment is not evidence of safe execution in a live EHR.
