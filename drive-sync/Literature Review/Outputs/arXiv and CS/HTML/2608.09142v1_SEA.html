One-page synthesis

Core thesis. The paper argues that an 8B-parameter oncology-adapted LLM can approach local oncologist performance on colorectal-cancer treatment-plan ratings when domain pre-training, model merging, supervised fine-tuning, agentic NCCN retrieval, and a hierarchical safety-oriented RL reward are combined.

GatorOnco is built from Meta-Llama-3.1-8B. The authors report a 282B-token biomedical pre-training corpus, including 166B tokens of UF Health clinical narratives, then merge the adapted model with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct to preserve instruction following. Post-training uses general medical reasoning examples, oncologist-curated CRC cases, guideline-derived QA, and GRPO reinforcement learning. The agent can decide when to search an indexed representation of NCCN colon v4.2025 and rectal v3.2025 guidelines, retrieve semantically relevant chunks, and continue generation using the retrieved evidence.

The local benchmark contains 395 CRC cases, partitioned in Methods as 276 training, 40 validation, and 79 held-out test cases. On the test set, GatorOnco reports single-treatment macro-F1 0.924, multi-treatment F1 0.913, and exact treatment-set matching in 73/79 cases (92.4%). For narrative plans, its arithmetic mean across seven automatic metrics is 0.6002 versus 0.5805 for Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, 0.5765 for MedGemma-27B-Text-it, and 0.4626 for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

In the blinded evaluation, five UF Health oncologists rated GatorOnco, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and oncologist-authored plans. GatorOnco was statistically comparable to oncologist-authored plans for correctness (4.09 vs 4.11; P=0.921), currency (4.04 vs 3.98; P=0.478), and safety (4.22 vs 4.22; P=0.9995), and higher for readability (4.46 vs 4.19; P<0.01) and completeness (3.91 vs 3.52; P<0.01). These findings are substantial for a local prototype, but the evidence remains benchmark- and rater-based rather than prospective, external, or outcome-based.

Most important caution. The paper contains internal reporting inconsistencies: Figure 1 labels the UF Health clinical corpus as 126B tokens while the text states 166B; Results sometimes calls 316 cases the training/development set while Methods specifies 276 training + 40 validation; and the Figure 3 caption says six treatment options while the paper defines and plots five. These do not erase the reported findings, but they reduce confidence in reporting precision and should be resolved before downstream reuse.

Section condensation

Abstract

The paper positions treatment planning as a high-stakes synthesis problem requiring current guidelines and individualized reasoning. It introduces GatorOnco as an agentic CRC planning model trained with large-scale biomedical and UF Health clinical text, domain adaptation, and guideline retrieval.

The abstract emphasizes blinded human evaluation: significantly better performance than open-source baselines and statistical parity with UF Health oncologists for correctness, currency, and safety, with higher readability and completeness. The abstract's central claim is therefore not only benchmark superiority but local expert-level plan quality under a blinded rating protocol.

Introduction

The introduction argues that oncology treatment planning remains manual, nuanced, and vulnerable to guideline discordance because disease biology, prior therapy, comorbidity, biomarkers, and rapidly changing standards must be synthesized. Deterministic CDS is presented as insufficiently personalized, while general-purpose LLMs are described as limited by guideline grounding, safety, catastrophic forgetting during adaptation, and lack of autonomous retrieval planning.

The proposed response is a domain-adapted open-source model with oncology-specific data and clinician involvement, plus an agentic RAG loop that can generate subqueries against current NCCN guidelines. The stated research question is whether this combination can produce guideline-concordant CRC plans at a level comparable to local experts.

Results - cohort construction

From 3,915 CRC patients in the UF Health IDR, the investigators filtered and deduplicated records to identify initial oncology encounters with treatment planning. Three oncologists curated 395 cases and annotated delivered therapy, potentially appropriate therapies, narrative plans, and supporting evidence. Methods later specify 276 training, 40 validation, and 79 held-out test cases.

Table 1 shows close balance between the 316-case development set and 79-case test set on age, sex, race, AJCC 7th stage, and primary tumor site, with all reported two-sided P values non-significant. The test set was 60.8% rectal and 39.2% colon cancer; stage III and IV together accounted for 75.9% of test cases.

Results - model development

Continual pre-training on the biomedical corpus produces GatorTronLlama, which is merged with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The merged model is then post-trained using guideline-derived QA, general medical reasoning examples, and CRC cases. Agentic GRPO training allows interleaved search over an NCCN guideline index.

The safety-oriented reward is hierarchical: high-level modality correctness gates reward for lower-level regimen details. This encodes the authors' view that an incorrect therapeutic modality should invalidate otherwise plausible regimen-level text, an attractive safety design principle even though deployment safety is not tested.

Results - treatment-option determination

For single-treatment determination, GatorOnco reports macro-F1 0.924, above baselines reported in the 0.748-0.910 range. Figure 3 gives GatorOnco per-option F1 values of 0.964 surgery, 0.979 chemotherapy, 0.933 radiotherapy, 0.889 targeted therapy, and 0.857 palliative care.

For multiple treatment options, GatorOnco reports F1 0.913 and exact set matching in 73 of 79 cases (92.4%); other LLMs matched 55-68 cases (69.6-86.1%). These are deterministic label/set outcomes and are the cleanest quantitative performance results in the paper.

Results - narrative treatment plans

GatorOnco has the highest arithmetic mean across BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE-L, BERTScore, BLEURT, AlignScore, and UniEval: 0.6002. It outperforms the 8B base comparator by 29.7% relatively and is modestly above the larger MedGemma-27B and Llama-3.3-70B baselines on the overall mean.

The strongest reported values include BERTScore 0.8948, BLEURT 0.8670, AlignScore 0.4847, and UniEval 0.9105. METEOR is an exception to across-the-board superiority: MedGemma-27B scores 0.3570 versus 0.3251 for GatorOnco. The paper appropriately supplements these imperfect automatic metrics with clinician ratings.

Results - blinded oncologist evaluation

Five UF Health oncologists rated 237 randomly mixed plans derived from 79 cases: GatorOnco plans, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct plans, and oncologist-authored ground truth. Inter-rater Krippendorff alpha was 0.626 correctness, 0.679 currency, 0.645 safety, 0.757 readability, and 0.849 completeness.

GatorOnco was statistically indistinguishable from oncologist-authored plans for correctness, currency, and safety, while scoring higher on readability and completeness. However, the human evaluation did not include the stronger MedGemma-27B or Llama-3.3-70B automatic-metric comparators; it also compares polished generated narratives against clinical-note ground truth, a format difference the authors themselves acknowledge may favor AI for readability.

Discussion

The authors interpret the findings as evidence that domain alignment and high-quality clinical data can matter more than parameter scale for specialized high-stakes tasks. They emphasize model merging as a response to catastrophic forgetting, agentic RAG as a response to guideline currency, and clinician involvement as essential for safety-oriented adaptation.

They also explicitly restrict the conclusion: single disease, single institution, prompt sensitivity, no deployment maturity, and a need for fail-safe behavior, uncertainty quantification, deferral policies, and workflow integration. The paper further argues that static guideline PDFs impose machine-readability costs and advocates structured, change-aware guideline releases.

Methods - data and annotation

The clinical pre-training corpus draws on UF Health notes from 2011-2025, spanning more than 60 million encounters and 126 departments. CRC cohort notes were excluded from pre-training by patient identifier. Public corpus components include FineWeb, MIMIC-III, The Pile biomedical subsets, Meditron guidelines, and Healix-Shot.

The curated CRC cohort required adults with confirmed primary CRC and at least one note within a ±1-year diagnostic window. Each accepted case was independently annotated by at least two oncologists, disagreements were adjudicated, and a senior oncologist resolved persistent disagreement. Historical plans could be updated to contemporary NCCN standards before reasoning-trajectory distillation.

Methods - retrieval and training

NCCN colon v4.2025 and rectal v3.2025 PDFs were converted to narrative and stepwise decision representations, chunked at 256 ± 50 tokens, embedded with E5-large-v2, and stored in FAISS. Metadata included cancer type, stage, metastatic status/site, and MSI/MMR. Initial metadata annotation was assisted by ChatGPT-5 and reviewed by two oncologists.

Agentic reasoning follows an iterative search loop: internal reasoning identifies an information gap, emits a search action, receives retrieved guideline chunks, and resumes reasoning. GRPO optimizes grouped samples. The regimen reward uses entity-level Dice similarity but is conditioned on correct high-level modality, preventing reward for detailed regimens under the wrong treatment class.

Methods - evaluation

Automatic evaluation compares GatorOnco with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, MedGemma-27B-Text-it, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct using the same prompts and settings. Classification uses per-option F1 and exact treatment-set matching; narrative evaluation uses lexical, semantic, and faithfulness metrics.

The blinded clinician study includes only GatorOnco, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and oncologist-authored plans. Mixed-effects analysis uses rater and sample random intercepts, with dimension-specific Aligned Rank Transform and Holm-Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon comparisons. Ethics approval covered retrospective de-identified data with waived consent.

GRPO and hierarchical oncology reward

Inputs: patient case + indexed NCCN evidence + expert modality/regimen labels.
Process: sample grouped outputs -> compute relative rewards -> agent searches guideline index as needed -> apply high-level modality gate -> compute regimen entity-set similarity only when modality is correct.
Output: policy optimized toward correct modality and more accurate regimen constituents.
Failure mode targeted: fluent regimen details under an incorrect therapeutic modality.
Failure modes not solved by this study: calibrated uncertainty, safe deferral, external validity, guideline-parser errors, workflow failure, prospective clinical impact.
Human review: oncologists participated in case curation, annotation, reward design, trajectory review, and blinded evaluation.

Strengths

  • Explicitly separates high-level therapy choice from regimen details in the reward, a clinically sensible failure-containment design.
  • Uses held-out patient cases and blinded oncologist review rather than relying only on automatic text metrics.
  • Implements dynamic guideline retrieval rather than static context injection, directly targeting guideline currency.
  • Includes a parameter-matched baseline, a medical-domain baseline, and a much larger general model for automatic evaluation.
  • Authors explicitly acknowledge deployment gaps rather than treating benchmark parity as readiness.

Critical limitations and risks

  • No external validation: all cases, annotation, and human evaluation come from one health system.
  • No clinical outcomes: evaluation measures agreement/ratings, not patient benefit, toxicity, error interception, or decision quality in live care.
  • Evaluator/gold-standard coupling: UF Health oncologists both supply the local reference framework and evaluate plans; this is appropriate for local validation but weak for generalization.
  • Comparator gap in human review: only the 8B Llama baseline is included alongside GatorOnco and expert plans; stronger automatic-metric baselines are absent from human comparison.
  • Format bias: generated text is compared with clinical-note ground truth, plausibly favoring LLMs on readability and completeness.
  • Prompt sensitivity: directly reported by the authors and unresolved.
  • Prototype safety: no uncertainty calibration, deferral policy, prospective fail-safe testing, or workflow monitoring.
  • Reporting precision: 126B vs 166B token conflict, 316 vs 276+40 development wording, and five vs six treatment-option caption mismatch.
  • Reproducibility constraints: proprietary UF Health pre-training corpus is not public; the cited supplement is absent from this PDF.

What would materially strengthen the evidence

Independent external validation at multiple health systems; prospective silent-mode and clinician-in-the-loop trials; case-level error taxonomy with severity; calibration and abstention/deferral metrics; robustness across prompts and guideline versions; direct comparison with stronger baseline models in blinded review; fully reproducible retrieval/parser evaluation; and patient-centered or workflow outcomes.

Implementation and research takeaways

Build-usefulUse a hierarchy that gates fine-grained regimen reward on correct high-level therapeutic intent.
Retrieval-usefulAnnotate guideline chunks with oncology-specific routing metadata before semantic retrieval; do not rely on vector similarity alone.
Evaluation-usefulCombine deterministic set-level treatment metrics, narrative faithfulness metrics, blinded specialist review, and inter-rater reliability.
Safety-usefulTreat uncertainty, abstention/deferral, fail-safe behavior, and workflow escalation as separate deployment requirements rather than inferred from benchmark accuracy.
Best use: architecture/evaluation reference for agentic clinical CDS, especially dynamic guideline retrieval and hierarchical safety-oriented reward design.
Do not use for: asserting that an autonomous LLM is ready to select CRC treatment in clinical practice or that expert-level Likert ratings predict patient outcomes.
