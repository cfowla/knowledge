One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. The paper is unusually relevant for agent-harness evaluation because it tests whether an LLM can improve executable orchestration code over a long research loop rather than merely solve static tasks. Its most useful contribution is the combination of controlled attribution, held-out task evaluation, integrity controls, trajectory analysis, and policy-transfer testing.

Core thesis. Evo-Bench attempts to measure an evolver model's intrinsic ability to improve a shared executable policy harness while holding the underlying policy model and evolution protocol fixed. The authors build 160 visible validation tasks and 448 held-out evaluation tasks across Search, Office, and General domains, using auxiliary harnesses to preferentially select tasks whose performance tracks harness quality. Nine evolver models start from the same minimal CodeAct harness and receive up to 20 evaluations, 1,000 steps, and 48 hours.

Main finding. All nine evolved harnesses improve the 29.7 CodeAct Overall baseline. GPT-5.6 Sol leads at 46.3 (+16.6), followed by Claude Opus 4.8 at 45.8 (+16.1), but neither exceeds the 47.5 composite human-engineered baseline overall. Gains are sharply domain dependent: Search improves dramatically; Office remains resistant; General is the strongest evidence that evolved structures can beat the manual comparator, with GPT-5.6 Sol and Qwen3.7-Max each scoring 59.4 versus 56.3.

Why it matters. The benchmark exposes a distinction between rapid local capability addition and sustained systems engineering. Strong evolvers quickly add tools, routing, recovery, and domain-specific policies, but later iterations often plateau, regress, or underuse the budget. Appendix audits show concrete failure modes in revision selection, smoke testing, causal diagnosis, and architectural search. These details make the paper more useful for harness design than the headline leaderboard alone.

Primary evidence constraints. The main leaderboard is based on one evolution run per model; no confidence intervals or replicated-run variance are reported. The benchmark is constructed using performance sensitivity to a selected set of auxiliary evolved harnesses, which improves signal but can couple the benchmark to the capabilities represented by those harnesses. Main experiments also fix one policy model and one common judge, with only limited policy-transfer ablation.

Section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame harness evolution as a distinct agent capability: autonomously improving the executable code that structures model reasoning, tools, memory, and workflow. Evo-Bench is proposed to separate that capability from base-model strength and task-specific overfitting.

The abstract reports gains as large as 16.6 Overall points, strong Search and General performance, weak Office evolution, early saturation, and cross-policy transfer of evolved harness structures.

1. Introduction

The paper argues that modern agent performance depends materially on the harness, not only the policy model. Existing self-improvement evaluations are said to confound model strength, use narrow tasks, or fail to test long-horizon code-centric evolution on held-out tasks.

The proposed solution is to fix the policy model and evolution protocol, let candidate evolvers iteratively revise a shared harness using validation evidence, and measure the frozen revision on a disjoint suite.

2. Related Work

The authors position Evo-Bench orthogonally to prompt optimization, workflow optimization, self-referential scaffold refinement, evolutionary search over executable code, trajectory-guided optimization, and AI-R&D benchmarks. The emphasis is benchmarking evolver capability rather than proposing a new optimization algorithm.

The claimed differentiator is long-horizon evolution of one general-purpose executable harness with controlled policy attribution and sensitivity-calibrated validation/evaluation splits.

3. Evo-Bench

The benchmark formalizes a fixed policy model π wrapped by an editable harness Ht and a separate evolver E operating through a fixed evolve harness. Validation evidence accumulates across iterations; the final harness is frozen and evaluated only once on held-out tasks. The visible suite has 160 tasks and held-out evaluation has 448.

The benchmark aggregates Search, Office, and General domains. The evolve harness supplies tools, context/state management, asynchronous evaluations, research skills, experiment tracking, and budget accounting. The seed policy harness is deliberately minimal.

4. Benchmark Construction

Stage 1 creates a 320-task auxiliary set favoring low baseline performance and long interaction horizons, then evolves 73 harness variants with four frontier evolvers and selects 12 representative harnesses. Stage 2 evaluates 2,329 candidate benchmark tasks under those harnesses.

Task harness sensitivity is the Pearson correlation between task performance across auxiliary harnesses and leave-one-task-out overall harness quality. Non-positive-sensitivity tasks are generally removed; remaining tasks are stratified by difficulty, prioritized by sensitivity, and split into aligned validation and evaluation sets.

5. Experiments

Main experiments fix DeepSeek-V4-Flash as policy model and use 20 iterations, 1,000 evolver steps, and 48 hours. Search/Office use one rollout per task; Claw-Eval uses three. Qwen3.7-Plus is the common LLM judge when required. Each evolver is run once.

GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 4.8 lead at 46.3 and 45.8 Overall versus CodeAct 29.7 and Artificial harness 47.5. Search sees the largest gains, Office the smallest, and General produces the clearest manual-baseline exceedance. Cost and trajectory analyses reveal major differences in exploration efficiency and budget use.

6. Ablation Studies

Budget scaling from 24h/10 iterations/500 steps to 48h/20 iterations/1,000 steps improves both Overall and Anytime Validation scores for Qwen3.7-Max and GLM-5.2. GLM rises rapidly then plateaus; Qwen3.7-Max scales more steadily.

Policy-transfer experiments replace the fixed DeepSeek policy with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and GLM-5.2. Evolved harnesses still improve substantially over their CodeAct baselines, supporting transferability beyond a single policy model, although this remains a limited set of policies and evolvers.

7. Conclusion and 8. Future Work

The authors conclude that harness-evolving capability is measurable and already substantial but not yet consistently superior to manual systems. They emphasize controlled attribution, long-horizon iteration, and transfer alignment.

Planned extensions include coding tasks, scientific-research tasks, broader framework compatibility, and more models.

Appendices A-E

The appendices materially strengthen reproducibility and appraisal. They specify the evolve harness, CodeAct seed, task-selection distributions, auxiliary-harness selection, model configurations, cost accounting, leakage/reward-hacking controls, detailed failure trajectories, and fixed prompt interfaces.

Most importantly, Appendix C documents how protected benchmark information is isolated and how confirmed retrieval violations are zeroed, while Appendix D shows specific evolution failures such as catastrophic verifier regressions, premature plateau declaration, and local-search saturation.

Implementation value

The paper exposes concrete design patterns and failure modes that can directly inform agent-harness research systems.

Strengths

  • Separates evolver from policy model and freezes the final harness before held-out scoring.
  • Uses sensitivity-aware task selection to make the benchmark responsive to harness quality.
  • Reports negative behavior—regressions, crashes, reward-hacking attempts, premature stopping—not only leaderboard gains.
  • Includes policy-transfer and budget ablations rather than relying solely on one fixed setup.
  • Appendix details are unusually actionable for designing experiment ledgers, snapshots, rollback, and integrity boundaries.

Key limitations / red flags

  • Single-run uncertainty: model rankings and deltas may be sensitive to stochastic evolution trajectories.
  • Selection coupling: benchmark tasks are selected using a set of evolved auxiliary harnesses, potentially emphasizing their represented capability dimensions.
  • Comparator interpretation: the Artificial harness is a composite of different domain-specialized systems, so the overall comparison is practical but not a clean matched architecture.
  • Judge dependence: some tasks use a fixed Qwen3.7-Plus judge; no judge-model sensitivity analysis is reported.
  • Seed headroom: the minimal CodeAct seed is intentionally sparse, so very large gains partly reflect adding missing basic agent infrastructure rather than only advanced harness research.
  • Construct boundary: “harness evolution” here should not be generalized to model-weight self-improvement or unconstrained recursive AI improvement.

Bottom line. Treat Evo-Bench as a high-value benchmark design and failure-analysis paper. The most defensible claim is that several current LLMs can materially improve a deliberately minimal executable harness under a controlled validation/evaluation loop. Claims about general autonomous systems engineering should remain provisional until replicated across runs, policies, evolve harnesses, and broader task families.

Implementation / research takeaways

Use immutable snapshotsEvery formal evaluation should bind to a content-addressed revision, and the system should automatically retain/restore the best validated revision.
Separate evidence from policy inputsValidation trajectories, answers, rubrics, and research notes should remain evolver-side and never leak into task-policy execution.
Require causal experiment notesBefore each costly iteration, record one falsifiable mechanism, intended change, local checks, and expected metric movement; discourage bundled edits.
Build cheap smoke testsThe Qwen verifier crash shows why syntax/unit/smoke checks should run before consuming a full benchmark evaluation.
Instrument budget utilizationTrack iterations, steps, wall time, rollout accesses, and reasoning/tool-use patterns; early stopping should be evidence-based rather than conversational.
Add architecture-reset triggersRepeated low-yield prompt/filter tweaks should trigger explicit reconsideration of planner, context, verifier, routing, or tool architecture.
Evaluate transfer, not just in-sample liftRe-run evolved harnesses across different policy models and task suites to distinguish general structures from policy-specific patches.
Keep integrity audits first-classReward-hacking detection should inspect delivered content and semantic trajectory behavior, not merely query strings or static rules.
