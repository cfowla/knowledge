One-page synthesis — source-reported findings

Core thesis reported by the authors: single-turn UQ methods can carry useful signal into agent trajectories, but transfer is uneven. White-box token-probability methods are highly sensitive to cross-turn aggregation; reflexive self-assessment is usually the strongest low-cost baseline; sampled-trajectory consistency is often the strongest family, with trajectory-equivalence and action-set measures frequently near the top.

The paper reframes the observation unit from a single generated answer to an interactive trajectory containing multiple actions, tool calls, messages, and state changes. It tests three families of UQ: white-box scores from action-token probabilities; black-box scores from agreement among resampled trajectories; and reflexive scores in which the agent evaluates its own completed trajectory. Every task has one greedy reference trajectory at temperature 0 and three sampled trajectories at temperature 0.7. Across BFCL-v4 and three τ²-bench domains, the authors evaluate discrimination, calibration, and selective-prediction behavior.

The most consequential result is heterogeneity. In the full white-box grid, 91/240 AUROC cells are below chance and 21 have bootstrap intervals entirely below 0.5. The same LNSP-min rule is strong on gpt-4o-mini retail (AUROC 0.72) but anti-correlated on telecom (0.24). Black-box consistency is more competitive overall: Qwen3.5-9B airline TER reaches 0.868 AUROC, but gpt-4o-mini telecom TER falls to 0.342. Reflexive scoring is cheaper and often strong; on BFCL-v4 P(True) spans roughly 0.746–0.852 AUROC across models. No method is uniformly reliable.

Practical pattern reported by the study: white-box scoring is the only zero-extra-call option when action-token log probabilities are available; reflexive scoring needs one self-evaluation pass; black-box consistency requires extra trajectories and is most compelling when resampling is already part of evaluation or a higher-cost failure-ranking signal is justified.
Central limitation: these are completed-trajectory measurements on four benchmark settings. The work explicitly warns against treating a transferred score as a deployment safety gate without target-model/domain validation.

Section-by-section condensation — source claims

Abstract & Introduction

The paper argues that single-turn confidence is an incomplete unit for tool-using agents because uncertainty can arise at multiple decision points and errors can propagate through a trajectory. It asks whether three established single-turn UQ families still classify trajectory success when the model can clarify, call tools, update state, and make intermediate decisions.

The stated contributions are a controlled cross-model/domain comparison, trajectory adaptations of white-box and black-box scorers, and empirical evidence that transferability is mixed rather than uniform.

Related Work

The authors position the study among output-consistency UQ, token-probability confidence, reflexive self-assessment, long-form uncertainty methods, and agent-specific uncertainty propagation/control work. They distinguish their focus from propagation methods such as SAUP/UProp and from work using uncertainty to trigger agent behavior.

Methods — trajectory framing and white-box scoring

The agent trajectory is treated as a sequence of actions. White-box confidence is computed on the committed action-token span, using sequence probability, length-normalized sequence probability, entropy-based ATN@5, and in the appendix probability margin. Per-turn values are aggregated to trajectory level using first, last, mean, minimum, and position-weighted early/late rules.

The interface is intentionally text-action based so action tokens and their probabilities are observable across the heterogeneous model/provider stack. The authors later test whether non-white-box findings survive native tool calling in a targeted ablation.

Methods — black-box and reflexive scoring

Black-box scorers compare each greedy trajectory with three resampled trajectories. NCP compares final messages; FAC compares first action type; ASC compares action-type sets; ADC compares action-frequency distributions; AEC compares ordered action sequences; TER asks a separate Gemini judge whether two trajectories reach the same task-relevant outcome. The TER judge does not see hidden acceptance criteria or reward.

Reflexive scoring asks the scored agent to assess its own trajectory before any subsequent user confirmation. P(True) uses the probability assigned to TRUE; verbalized confidence uses a Yes/No decision plus a stated probability.

Experiments

The evaluation covers 200 BFCL-v4 multi-turn tasks and τ²-bench retail (114), airline (50), and telecom (114). Five models span OpenAI and Together-hosted systems. τ²-bench uses a fixed gpt-4.1-mini simulated user at temperature 0. The primary metric is AUROC, with AUPRC, PRR, ECE, and task-clustered bootstrap uncertainty as complementary measures.

Main results

White-box results are highly aggregator-dependent, especially in τ²-bench. The largest τ² white-box point estimate is about 0.725, yet the same base score can move near 0.23 with another aggregation. By contrast, action/trajectory consistency frequently performs well, especially on airline, while reflexive scoring is often strong for much lower compute cost. The relative winner remains model- and domain-specific.

The authors emphasize uncertainty around rankings: intervals are often wide, and the main inferential weight is placed on recurring family-level patterns and selected paired comparisons rather than bolded single-cell maxima.

Discussion & Conclusions

The authors conclude that agent UQ is not merely single-turn UQ applied to longer context. White-box aggregation choices can reverse rankings; reflexive self-assessment is a practical baseline; and trajectory consistency can provide stronger signal but costs additional runs. They recommend per-deployment validation rather than transfer by default.

Future work proposed includes step-level outcome labels, experiments that actually act on uncertainty during execution, larger sampling budgets/decoding sweeps, and broader agent environments.

Limitations & Ethical Considerations

Limitations include narrow benchmark scope, reward/label noise, simulator involvement in sampled trajectories, dependence on a text-action measurement interface for white-box scoring, modest per-domain sample sizes, dependence of TER on one judge family, and omission of observation/reasoning-token uncertainty and online intervention evaluation.

The ethical section explicitly distinguishes post-hoc success classification from preventive safety control. Because some scores anti-correlate with success, the authors warn that unvalidated use as a safety gate could create false assurance.

Appendices A–J

The appendices add probability margin and position-weighted aggregation, SAUP-inspired controls, benchmark grading details, full AUROC/AUPRC/PRR/ECE grids, cheap turn/tool-count baselines, bootstrap intervals, TER judge validation and judge-swap sensitivity, failure-failure diagnostics, agent-only prefix resampling, native tool-calling robustness, and the exact prompts for P(True), verbalized confidence, and TER.

Independent evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: a strong empirical map of where trajectory-level agent UQ transfers, fails, and becomes costly. Do not use it as evidence that any evaluated score is a validated online safety gate.

Relevance

9/10

Rationale: directly addresses agent reliability, uncertainty, selective prediction, and tool-use evaluation.

Evidence basis: five models, four agentic datasets, three UQ families, multiple reliability metrics.

Limiting factor: benchmark-only post-hoc evaluation.

Would rise: demonstrated online control benefits or broader deployment settings.

Novelty

8/10

Rationale: the value is the systematic trajectory-level comparison and agent-specific consistency constructions, not a wholly new UQ theory.

Evidence basis: cross-turn aggregation study, TER, action-structured consistency, failure-failure diagnostics.

Limiting factor: many ingredients adapt established single-turn techniques.

Would rise: a principled trajectory-native estimator with stronger theoretical/empirical advantage.

Method strength

7/10

Rationale: careful multi-metric design, bootstrap uncertainty, paired comparisons, judge validation, and targeted ablations support the core claims.

Evidence basis: Tables 3, 9, 10–13 and Figures 1–3.

Limiting factor: modest domain task counts and many model-scorer comparisons with unresolved rankings.

Would rise: larger task sets, preregistered primary comparisons, more external benchmark families.

Evidence strength

7/10

Rationale: the evidence strongly supports heterogeneity and deployment-specific validation; it is weaker for identifying a universal best scorer.

Evidence basis: repeated cross-domain sign changes, 21 resolved sub-chance white-box cells, paired/ablation results.

Limiting factor: reward noise, simulator confounding, and wide intervals.

Would rise: independent replication and cleaner ground-truth outcome labels.

External validity

5/10

Rationale: multiple models and domains help, but all settings remain structured tool-use benchmarks with simulated users.

Evidence basis: BFCL-v4 plus three τ² text domains.

Limiting factor: no web/embodied/open-ended environments and only one targeted native-tool interface ablation.

Would rise: diverse live or realistic deployments and model/provider replication.

Implementation value

8/10

Rationale: the paper gives concrete scorer definitions, prompts, compute accounting, and failure diagnostics that can guide an evaluation harness.

Evidence basis: equations, prompt tables, cost table, sample-budget and interface ablations.

Limiting factor: no evaluated online intervention policy and no universal operating threshold.

Would rise: released end-to-end code plus validated abstention/escalation policies.

Main strengths

  • Does not hide negative transfer: anti-correlation and scorer failures are foregrounded.
  • Separates discrimination, selective prediction, and calibration rather than treating “confidence” as one construct.
  • Includes multiple diagnostics aimed at common confounds: judge quality, simulator involvement, trajectory length, sample count, and action interface.
  • Compute accounting makes the performance/cost trade-off explicit.

Main limitations

  • The task is prediction of benchmark reward after a trajectory completes, not preventive agent control.
  • Many apparent within-column winners are not statistically resolved.
  • Consistency can encode repeated failure as well as repeated success; outcome equivalence improves but does not eliminate this ambiguity.
  • White-box methods rely on an action-token probability surface that is not uniformly available in native tool-calling APIs.
  • TER inherits external-judge competence and its validation is strongest for database-state equivalence rather than full semantic correctness.

Implementation / research takeaways

Evaluation harness

Treat UQ family, aggregation rule, model, domain, and compute budget as explicit configuration dimensions. Never collapse “confidence” into one scalar method without validation.

Low-cost baseline

If a transcript self-check is acceptable, reflexive scoring is the paper's strongest practical low-cost starting point. It still requires target-domain calibration checks.

When to pay for samples

Trajectory consistency is most defensible when repeated trajectories already exist or when the application can justify the extra model runs for stronger failure ranking.

White-box caution

Action-token probability UQ should be treated as an interface-dependent measurement. Validate the aggregation rule; do not assume mean/min/last transfers.

Failure taxonomy

Add explicit repeated-failure diagnostics. High self-consistency can mean a stable wrong policy, especially in low-success regimes.

Next experiment

The most valuable extension is prospective: compute uncertainty during execution, apply a defined abstain/clarify/escalate policy, and measure whether task success or safety actually improves.

What not to overclaim: the paper does not validate a universal uncertainty threshold, does not establish calibration across deployments, and does not show that its post-hoc scores prevent harmful tool actions.
