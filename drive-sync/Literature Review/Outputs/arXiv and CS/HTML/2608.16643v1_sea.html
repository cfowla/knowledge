One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Standard pointwise metrics can materially overstate LLM performance in clinical error detection because models may achieve acceptable F1 by defaulting toward one class. Evaluating naturally paired erroneous/clean notes exposes this failure: 13 of 15 tested open-weight models had mean Both-Correct Rate (BCR) below the 25% balanced random-pair baseline.

The paper exploits a property of clinical error-detection benchmarks that ordinary scoring ignores: injected-error notes have clean counterparts that differ by a small, clinically meaningful change. The authors define Both-Correct Rate (BCR), which counts a pair as successful only when the model flags the erroneous note and clears its clean counterpart. They also define an independence ratio comparing observed BCR with the BCR expected from sensitivity × specificity, and Evidence Contrastive Analysis (ECA) to determine whether a failed pair reflects inability to locate relevant evidence or inability to convert located evidence into the correct judgment.

Across 240 model–dataset–configuration runs, balanced accuracy ranged from 0.45 to 0.71 and F1 reached 0.80, yet 178/240 runs (74%) had F1 >0.5 while balanced accuracy was ≤0.6. The pairwise picture was substantially worse: only Qwen 3-32B (28.0% mean BCR) and UltraMedical 70B (25.7%) exceeded the 25% balanced random baseline. Thirty-two of 60 model–dataset averages (53%) occupied the authors’ “deception zone” of F1 ≥0.6 with BCR <25%. Error-flag rate strongly correlated with F1 (r=+0.853) while inversely correlating with BCR (r=−0.488); direct F1–BCR correlation was near zero (r=−0.058).

High-value finding. Many failures are judgment failures rather than pure localization failures. On MS-Test, ECA found that models localized the error-relevant content on 46–87% of failed Pred1 pairs (mean 70%), while the pure “Both-Hit” localization-judgment gap comprised 26–49% (mean 38%). This suggests that a model can cite the right sentence in both notes and still label both as erroneous.
Primary limitation for practice translation. The evaluation is zero-shot, benchmark-based, confined empirically to substitution-form errors, and largely restricted to open-weight models. It therefore supports a stronger evaluation protocol, not autonomous clinical error-detection deployment.

Section condensation

Abstract & Introduction

The authors argue that clinical error-detection benchmarks have an underused contrastive structure: an error-injected note often has a corresponding clean note. Aggregate metrics score notes independently and can therefore reward a model that defaults toward “error” or “no error” without reliably distinguishing the two. The study’s central question is whether pairwise evaluation changes the apparent ranking and interpretation of LLM performance.

Three contributions are framed: quantify the discrimination failure using BCR; characterize bidirectional and language-dependent prediction bias; and use ECA to determine whether failed discrimination arises because the model misses relevant evidence or because it misjudges evidence it has already surfaced.

Related Work

The paper situates itself across clinical error-detection benchmarks, prediction-bias literature, contrast sets/signal-detection ideas, and evidence/representation gaps in LLMs. The conceptual bridge is that clinical benchmarks created by controlled error injection naturally permit minimal-pair analysis, yet published evaluation has largely emphasized pointwise F1/accuracy-like metrics.

Methods — datasets and task

Four paired test sets were evaluated in English, Chinese, and Japanese. The task was restricted to binary error flagging; models saw each note independently, never side-by-side. Pairing was applied only after inference to calculate BCR. MS-Test had 286 matched pairs from 597 notes; MEB-EN 104 pairs; MEB-CN 100 pairs; MRT-JA 190 pairs, with a many-to-one structure because 190 error notes map to 105 unique clean notes.

The framework is designed to generalize to substitution, insertion, and omission errors, but the empirical evaluation is confined to substitution-form errors available in the public paired benchmarks.

Methods — models, prompting and decoding

Fifteen instruction-tuned LLMs across five families and three size tiers (3–8B, 27–32B, 70B) were tested; five were medical-domain models. All evaluations were zero-shot. Models ≤27B used bf16; two 70B models used fp8 via vLLM due to memory constraints.

Each model–dataset combination was run under a 2×2 prompt/decoding perturbation matrix: neutral vs conservative prompt, and greedy vs sampling decoding. Outputs followed a structured Evidence / Analysis / Confidence / Error format, enabling both verdict scoring and ECA. Cross-configuration means and standard deviations were used to expose instability rather than rely on one benchmark prompt.

Methods — evaluation framework

Pointwise metrics included balanced accuracy, F1, precision, recall/sensitivity, specificity, MCC, and error-flag rate. BCR required both members of a matched error/clean pair to be correctly classified. A random classifier on balanced pairs yields a 25% BCR reference; MRT-JA’s prevalence-calibrated random reference is 22.9%. The independence expectation is sensitivity × specificity, and the independence ratio is observed BCR divided by that expectation.

ECA examines cited evidence on failed Pred1 pairs. Evidence is matched to the ground-truth error/correction sentence using substring or ≥60% coverage logic, with character-level matching for Chinese/Japanese. This produces categories that distinguish attention/localization failure from the localization-judgment gap.

Results — traditional metrics vs pairwise discrimination

Across 240 runs, balanced accuracy ranged 0.45–0.71 and F1 reached 0.80. The paper reports 178/240 runs (74%) with F1 >0.5 despite balanced accuracy ≤0.6. Mean BCR was below 25% for 13/15 models. Only Qwen 3-32B (28.0%) and UltraMedical 70B (25.7%) exceeded that balanced random reference, while even those models failed most pairs.

Scaling helped inconsistently. Qwen increased from 17.0% mean BCR at 4B to 28.0% at 32B but dipped to 12.1% at 8B; Llama increased from 10.2% at 3B to 20.0% at 70B. Medical specialization often helped more than scaling: MedGemma 27B reached 23.1% versus 10.2% for Gemma 3-27B, although MediPhi 4B was an exception.

Results — language, prompts and localization

Prediction bias was not a single global model property. Eight of 15 models changed bias category across datasets, and seven crossed parity across languages/datasets. Qwen 3-8B moved from no-bias on Chinese (error-flag rate 0.36) to yes-bias on Japanese (0.69). Seven models also switched from yes-bias under neutral prompting to no-bias under conservative prompting on at least one dataset, showing that prompt wording can reverse default tendencies without necessarily improving discrimination.

Localization was much stronger than pairwise judgment. Mean true-positive localization was reported as 87% on MEB-EN, 70% on MEB-CN, 69% on MS-Test, and 42% on MRT-JA. On MS-Test, models found error-relevant content in most Pred1 failures yet still misclassified the clean counterpart; Neither-Hit attention failure averaged only 18%.

Results — metric mediation and robustness

Across the 60 model–dataset four-configuration means, error-flag rate correlated +0.853 with F1 and −0.488 with BCR, while F1–BCR correlation was −0.058. Thirty-two entries (53%) had F1 ≥0.6 but BCR <25%. On MS-Test, the three highest-F1 models (Gemma 3-4B, Llama 3.2-3B, Mistral 7B; F1 >0.65) were also the three lowest-BCR models (4.5–5.9%).

Observed BCR was below independence expectation for all 60 model–dataset entries; mean independence ratio was 0.729, ranging by dataset from about 0.621 (MEB-CN) to 0.844 (MEB-EN). Appendix robustness checks found parse-failure imputation changed mean BCR by <0.5 percentage points and preserved the 13/15 below-random conclusion. ECA rankings were stable to overlap thresholds and embedding-based rescoring; prompt/configuration variation could nevertheless shift absolute BCR markedly, including a 36.2-point range for Llama 3.1-70B on MEB-EN.

Discussion & limitations

The authors recommend paired metrics as an additional benchmark-reporting and procurement/governance gate in safety-critical clinical NLP. They explicitly do not present BCR as a replacement for prospective clinician-in-the-loop evaluation. The localization-judgment gap motivates contrastive fine-tuning or decomposed localization→judgment pipelines; high-recall yes-biased models might have a role only as pre-filters with mandatory human review and alert-fatigue monitoring.

Important limitations are zero-shot-only evaluation, two prompt variants, one sampled run per configuration, an open-weight sweep only through 70B with one proprietary reference point, possible training-data contamination, empirical restriction to substitution errors, ECA matching limitations on multi-sentence Japanese structures, and no mechanistic analysis of why cited evidence fails to produce the correct verdict.

Strengths

  • Uses the benchmark’s causal construction (error vs clean counterpart) instead of discarding it during evaluation.
  • Separates discrimination from marginal class tendency and demonstrates a concrete rank-ordering failure.
  • Crosses model families, sizes, medical specialization, languages, prompts, and decoding modes.
  • Explains failure rather than merely scoring it through ECA and independence analysis.
  • Performs targeted robustness analyses for parse failures and localization criteria.

Principal concerns

  • The 25% BCR “random” reference is intuitive for balanced independent guesses but is not the only meaningful comparator; the independence ratio is the more general diagnostic and should be emphasized in downstream use.
  • Paired benchmarks may be easier or harder than naturally occurring errors depending on how injected sentences differ from clean counterparts; success on BCR is therefore a necessary discrimination check, not sufficient clinical validation.
  • MRT-JA’s many-to-one structure can induce dependencies, and ECA’s sentence-level matching is less granular for its multi-sentence construction.
  • No direct clinician comparison, prospective workflow outcomes, alert burden, calibration utility, or patient-safety endpoints are measured.
  • The single GPT-5 mini point is illustrative only; it cannot support a proprietary-vs-open model conclusion, and contamination cannot be excluded.

Bottom line

Use now: yes, as an evaluation design pattern. Add pairwise discrimination metrics when error/clean contrast pairs are available; keep pointwise metrics for complementary information. Do not use: as evidence that any tested model is safe for autonomous clinical error detection. Confidence in appraisal: high for the benchmark-method conclusion; moderate for generalization to real clinical workflows.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Benchmark designPreserve clean/error pairing through evaluation. Report BCR plus sensitivity, specificity, F1/MCC, error-flag rate, and an independence reference rather than a single aggregate score.
Model procurementUse paired discrimination as a pre-deployment gate to detect systems whose aggregate performance is produced by default class tendency. Treat failure as disqualifying for autonomous use, not as a minor metric discrepancy.
Multilingual evaluationDo not assume a model’s response bias transfers across languages. Measure error-flag rate and pairwise discrimination separately for each language/site population.
Prompt testingPrompt wording can reverse bias direction. Evaluate across a small, prespecified prompt/decoding perturbation set and report variability, rather than optimizing a single prompt against one metric.
Failure analysisWhen evidence-bearing outputs are available, separate localization from judgment. A model that consistently cites the right sentence but reaches the wrong clean/error contrast may require a different intervention than one that never finds the relevant evidence.
Clinical deployment boundaryPairwise benchmark success should precede—not replace—prospective workflow evaluation, clinician adjudication, calibration/threshold analysis, alert-fatigue monitoring, and broader error-type testing.
Pipeline relevance. For a literature/CDS evaluation stack, the paper is especially useful as a template for “paired golden tests”: retain minimally changed correct/incorrect fixtures and require consistent decisions across both members, rather than validating only isolated examples.
