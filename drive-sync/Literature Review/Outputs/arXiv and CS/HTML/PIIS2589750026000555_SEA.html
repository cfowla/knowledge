One-page synthesis

Core thesis: G-PROG uses a single baseline colour fundus photograph to estimate the subsequent 2–5-year G-RISK progression slope. The study reports strong internal discrimination and moderate-to-good external discrimination across five independent clinical datasets, while also showing construct agreement between the G-RISK slope and longitudinal visual-field/RNFL biomarkers.

Why it matters. The work targets a clinically important gap: estimating future glaucoma progression at diagnosis rather than waiting for serial visual fields or OCT. Its strongest contribution is the combination of a longitudinal image-derived structural target, patient-level leakage controls, explicit external testing across multiple centres/cameras, and a low-cost input modality.

What limits it. G-PROG is trained against a model-derived surrogate (G-RISK slope) observed under active treatment, not an untreated natural-history endpoint or patient-centred outcome. Regression fit is modest (R2 frequently near zero or negative despite useful threshold discrimination), external performance is heterogeneous, subgroup/fairness evaluation is incomplete, and the missing appendix prevents full reproducibility appraisal.

Section condensation

Summary and research in context

The authors frame rapid glaucoma progression as a resource-allocation and vision-preservation problem and introduce G-PROG to predict 2–5-year progression from a baseline CFP. They report 161,827 images from 127,962 visits involving 13,913 patients across six departments. Progression is defined by longitudinal G-RISK slope, and 20 model configurations are explored.

The literature-context panel positions the work as distinct from prior perimetry-centred or binary progression models by using a deep-learning-derived structural slope as ground truth. The paper explicitly treats prospective and untreated-cohort evaluation as future validation rather than established clinical utility.

Introduction

The introduction emphasizes heterogeneity in glaucoma progression and limitations of existing longitudinal markers: visual-field mean deviation is clinically meaningful but variable, whereas OCT RNFL thinning is structurally objective but can encounter floor effects in advanced disease. The proposed value proposition is prediction from a single examination.

Prior deep-learning studies are described as having limited prediction windows or lacking external testing. G-PROG is presented as a CNN that forecasts a continuous structural progression slope from baseline CFP rather than only classifying current disease or future binary progression.

Methods — cohorts, target construction, and training

Leuven served as the development/internal-validation site, with Brussels, Liège, Tampere, Mainz, and Hangzhou reserved for external testing. Adults needed at least three CFP-containing visits for an eye. Images were disc-centred, and auxiliary CNNs screened optic-disc presence, blur/image quality, and laterality. The G-RISK slope was computed by within-eye linear regression of serial G-RISK predictions and served as the regression target.

G-PROG used EfficientNet-B0. Twenty configurations varied interval and inclusion constraints; Leuven data were split 60/20/20 at the patient level. Eight internally selected models were averaged for external testing without recalibration. The accessible main text does not expose all configuration parameters because many details are delegated to the missing appendix.

Methods — evaluation and validation

Primary discrimination analysis treated G-PROG’s continuous slope prediction as the ROC score across G-RISK slope thresholds from 0 to 0.075, reporting AUC with DeLong 95% CIs. R2, EVS, Pearson r, MAE, and MAE improvement were additional regression metrics. Performance was stratified by visual-field severity, but formal sociodemographic/fairness metrics were not computed.

The authors separately assessed whether G-RISK slope agreed with visual-field MD slope and average RNFL slope, then generated saliency maps from the best internal single configuration after registering images to the optic disc.

Results — internal and external prediction

Internal validation reached AUC 0.98 (95% CI 0.97–1.00) across all intervals and 0.92 (0.85–0.98) in intervals with baseline G-RISK above 0.6; the internal test set reached 0.91 and 0.96 in the corresponding analyses. However, R2 ranged from -0.59 to 0.11 and was positive in only 9 of 20 experiments, while predicted-versus-observed slope correlation was 0.42 in validation and 0.39 in test data.

External maximum AUCs varied by site. Across all intervals, reported maxima were 0.79 Brussels, 0.78 Liège, 0.62 Tampere, 0.81 Mainz, and 0.86 GRAPE; in baseline G-RISK ≥0.6 subsets they were 0.73, 0.76, 0.74, 0.75, and 0.77 respectively. These are useful discrimination results but show substantial heterogeneity and, for GRAPE, wide uncertainty due to a very small cohort.

Results — biomarker agreement and explainability

G-RISK slope discrimination against MD-slope progression reached maximum AUCs from 0.67 to 0.82 depending on threshold/stratum, with stronger agreement after excluding very advanced baseline disease. Agreement with average RNFL slope was stronger, with peak AUCs around 0.99–1.00, although the RNFL analysis involved far fewer eyes because OCT slope data were sparse.

Average saliency maps repeatedly emphasized the optic disc in internal and external datasets, with weaker recurrent saliency inferior to the disc. This is anatomically plausible but does not by itself establish causal feature use or protect against image/site confounding.

Discussion, limitations, and conclusion

The authors interpret the results as evidence that single-CFP risk stratification could identify faster progressors and potentially prioritize monitoring or enrich clinical trials. They also argue that G-RISK slope may be a practical structural biomarker because it requires only serial fundus photographs and avoids OCT segmentation dependence.

The major limitations are consequential: G-RISK’s upper bound can create a ceiling effect; more densely followed intervals may create selection bias; thresholds require refinement; the progression target reflects actively managed eyes and geographically variable treatment; ethnicity was unavailable; and prospective untreated validation is absent. The conclusion is therefore best read as a promising research result rather than a deployment-ready clinical tool.

Disclosures and data/code availability

Funders were reported as having no role in design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, writing, or submission. The conflict-of-interest statement discloses commercial ties surrounding G-RISK/Mona.health, including transfer of the model and author financial relationships; these disclosures do not invalidate the results but increase the importance of independent replication.

GRAPE is public, while other datasets require collaboration agreements or controlled/on-site access. A public G-PROG repository is reported to contain a minimal working example, whereas trained models are available on academically reasonable request.

Implementation value

Rationale: The workflow, backbone, QC strategy, code example, and model ensemble are described enough to inform system design.

Evidence basis: Public code example is reported; quality-control models and external deployment conditions are discussed.

Principal limiting factor: Weights are request-only, appendix details are missing from this PDF, and no prospective clinical workflow impact was tested.

Would raise the score: Reproducible weights/configuration files plus prospective usability and resource-allocation evaluation.

Main strengths

  • Five external clinical datasets rather than single-site cross-validation alone.
  • Patient-level data splitting and explicit avoidance of post-hoc recalibration in external testing.
  • Multiple metrics expose the difference between threshold discrimination and continuous slope prediction.
  • Construct validation against both functional (MD) and structural (RNFL) longitudinal markers.
  • Heterogeneous cameras, follow-up schedules, and sites provide a nontrivial generalisation stress test.

Main limitations

  • Model-derived G-RISK slope is a surrogate ground truth, not untreated natural-history progression or a patient-centred outcome.
  • Observed slopes come from actively managed eyes, so treatment and local practice alter the target.
  • R2 is weak despite attractive AUCs; the intended use therefore depends on risk stratification thresholds rather than accurate absolute progression-rate estimation.
  • External performance is heterogeneous, and GRAPE has only 58 participants with broad CIs.
  • Ethnicity/fairness analyses were unavailable, and appendix-only sensitivity/configuration details could not be audited from the retrieved PDF.
Claim boundary: The paper supports “predictive signal generalises across several cohorts.” It does not establish that using G-PROG changes target IOP selection, reduces visual loss, improves resource allocation, or is safe across demographic subgroups.

Implementation and research takeaways

Build pattern worth reusing
Separate image-quality/laterality/disc-localisation QC from the predictive model; split by patient; freeze external sites; report both discrimination and regression fidelity.
Validation pattern worth reusing
Test across heterogeneous cameras/sites without recalibration, then expose site-level performance rather than relying on pooled AUC.
Risk to avoid
Do not let a strong AUC at extreme thresholds obscure low R2 or imply accurate individual continuous forecasts.
Next evidence needed
Prospective multimodal validation, untreated or trial-control cohorts, calibration/decision-curve analyses, explicit subgroup/fairness reporting, and clinical workflow outcomes.
