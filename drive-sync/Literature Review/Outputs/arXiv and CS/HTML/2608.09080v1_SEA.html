One-page synthesis

Verdict: Read first. The paper is highly relevant as an evaluation-pattern paper: its strongest contribution is not a definitive ranking of models, but a compact stress-test design for asking whether confidence and abstention respond appropriately when information becomes ambiguous or explicitly insufficient.

The study tests five LLMs on 500 MedMCQA questions in two settings. Setting A adds increasingly strong linguistic uncertainty warnings (C0-C3) without changing the underlying question or answer choices. Setting B removes the correct option, inserts an “Insufficient information” choice, and treats selection of that option as safe abstention. The authors measure accuracy, self-reported confidence, logit-based confidence where available, calibration gap, ECE, UCER, and abstention/hallucination behavior.

The key result is a decoupling between task performance and confidence. Under uncertainty framing, accuracy changes little and confidence does not consistently fall; GPT-5 is slightly underconfident and comparatively well calibrated, while GPT-4o/GPT-4o-mini/LLaMA remain overconfident and Mistral-7B is severely miscalibrated. Under answer removal, correct abstention is low for every model (15.6%-38.2%), and high-confidence unsafe errors remain substantial, especially for LLaMA-3 (0.828) and Mistral-7B (0.760). The authors therefore argue that accuracy alone is inadequate for medical-AI reliability assessment.

Important QA finding: several answer-removal values are internally inconsistent. Table IV reports LLaMA-3 abstention 0.156 and hallucination 0.798 even though Equation (10) defines hallucination as 1-abstention (which would yield 0.844). LLaMA-3 mean confidence is 0.970 in Table III but 0.963 in Table IV/Fig. 5; Mistral-7B is 0.998 vs 0.997. Figure 5’s caption also references C0-C3 despite plotting answer-removal values. These issues reduce confidence in exact secondary numbers but do not erase the broad qualitative pattern.

Section condensation

Abstract and Introduction

The paper frames overconfidence as a safety problem in medical LLM use: incorrect answers are risky, but incorrect answers delivered with high certainty may be especially misleading. The authors distinguish accuracy from calibration and emphasize that standard medical QA benchmarks assume a correct option is always available, unlike real clinical settings where information may be incomplete or ambiguous.

The study’s stated contribution is a dual evaluation framework covering prompt-level ambiguity and explicit information insufficiency. It aims to test whether models reduce confidence and abstain when evidence no longer supports a definitive answer, using calibration and safety metrics rather than accuracy alone.

II. State of the Art

The review situates the work in LLM hallucination, confidence elicitation, token/logit probabilities, semantic uncertainty, calibration, and abstention. It argues that verbalized confidence may not reflect internal model uncertainty and that most prior work focuses on model knowledge rather than uncertainty caused by incomplete inputs.

The paper’s gap claim is therefore behavioral: uncertainty can come from the input, not only from model limitations, and current medical QA evaluations insufficiently test whether a model recognizes that distinction.

III. Methodology

Setting A transforms each question with graded uncertainty cues while keeping answer options and ground truth unchanged. This isolates the effect of a meta-level uncertainty warning on confidence and calibration. Setting B removes the ground-truth option and inserts an explicit abstention choice, turning abstention into the defined correct behavior.

Two confidence signals are collected: explicit self-report and normalized option-logit probability. ECE uses 10 bins; UCER measures high-confidence incorrect responses; abstention/hallucination are defined directly from the answer-removal choice. This is a useful separation of performance, calibration, and safety behavior, although self-reported confidence remains prompt-dependent.

IV. Experimental Setup

The authors randomly sample 500 four-option MedMCQA questions using a fixed seed, yielding 2,000 Setting-A evaluations across C0-C3. The correct answer is randomly replaced by the abstention option in Setting B to reduce positional bias.

GPT-5, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o-mini are accessed through APIs; LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 run locally with 4-bit quantization on an NVIDIA T4. Temperature is 0. The statistical plan uses paired McNemar tests for accuracy and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for self-confidence, two-sided alpha 0.05, with Holm-Bonferroni correction.

V-A. Results — Uncertainty Framing

Accuracy remains relatively stable across C0-C3. GPT-5 has the highest accuracy (0.820-0.836) and negative calibration gaps (-0.013 to -0.038), indicating slight underconfidence. GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and LLaMA-3 show persistent positive gaps; Mistral-7B remains near 0.50 accuracy while self-confidence stays near 0.97-0.99, producing gaps around 0.48.

Stronger uncertainty wording does not produce a consistent, material reduction in confidence. The source interprets this as weak sensitivity to prompt-level uncertainty. This setting tests responsiveness to uncertainty cues, not actual missing clinical content, so the result should not be generalized to all forms of clinical uncertainty.

V-B/C. Results — Answer Removal and Statistics

Correct abstention is low: GPT-4o 0.382, GPT-4o-mini 0.368, GPT-5 0.192, LLaMA-3 0.156, and Mistral-7B 0.238. Unsafe high-confidence error rates are 0.428, 0.424, 0.186, 0.828, and 0.760, respectively. Self-reported confidence substantially exceeds logit confidence for LLaMA-3 (0.970 vs 0.280 in Table III) and Mistral-7B (0.998 vs 0.657); GPT-based models are closer, though GPT-5 logit probabilities are unavailable.

Table V reports multiple significant pairwise McNemar comparisons. However, the Methods also specify Wilcoxon confidence comparisons, and those results are not presented in the main statistical table. Exact answer-removal values require caution because of the table/figure inconsistencies documented above.

VI. Discussion

The authors interpret the results as evidence that model-generated confidence often fails to represent epistemic uncertainty and that answer-generation bias persists even when abstention is explicitly available. They propose that next-token training and limited abstention supervision may contribute, but this causal explanation is speculative rather than directly tested.

The practical argument is stronger than the mechanistic one: clinical LLM evaluation should include calibration, abstention, and unsafe-error behavior, not only benchmark accuracy. Prompt warnings alone are unlikely to be a sufficient safety mechanism.

VII. Limitations

The authors acknowledge three main limits: reliance on one MedMCQA benchmark, synthetic prompt-based uncertainty rather than naturally occurring clinical ambiguity, and a fixed set of models in a static multiple-choice task. These constrain generalizability to real clinical decision support.

Additional appraisal limits are incomplete reporting of the planned Wilcoxon analyses, absence of uncertainty intervals/effect sizes, no code/data-availability statement in the PDF, incomplete model snapshot/API reproducibility details, and internal inconsistencies across Table III, Table IV, and Figure 5.

VIII. Conclusion and Future Work

The source concludes that current LLM confidence estimates do not reliably track uncertainty and that even proprietary models often fail to abstain when the correct answer is unavailable. Open-weight models in this evaluation show particularly severe overconfidence and unsafe-error rates.

The authors recommend calibration-aware training, explicit abstention mechanisms, and benchmarks that systematically manipulate missing or ambiguous information. The most defensible takeaway is evaluation-oriented: the paper provides a reusable stress-test template, not clinical deployment evidence.

Evaluation and appraisal

Relevance: 10/10

Directly targets clinical LLM reliability, uncertainty, calibration, abstention, and unsafe confident errors.

Evidence: Two complementary uncertainty tests on medical QA.
Limiting factor: Relevance does not establish deployment validity.
Raise/lower: Would fall if the user’s focus moved away from clinical-AI evaluation.

Novelty: 7/10

The dual framing-plus-answer-removal protocol is a useful synthesis of known calibration and abstention ideas.

Evidence: Explicit missing-answer stress test plus UCER/calibration analysis.
Limiting factor: The component techniques and metrics are established rather than fundamentally new.
Raise/lower: Higher with a new uncertainty-estimation method or validated clinical benchmark.

Method strength: 6/10

Controlled paired perturbations and a fixed 500-question sample support clean behavioral comparisons.

Evidence: Same questions across models/conditions; fixed seed; temperature 0; paired tests; Holm-Bonferroni.
Limiting factor: Single benchmark, synthetic cues, static MCQ task, incomplete reproducibility detail, and internal reporting inconsistencies.
Raise/lower: Higher with preregistered protocol, exact API snapshots, repeated runs/seeds, code, and independent replication.

Evidence strength: 6/10

The main behavioral pattern is visible across multiple models, but the source has reporting and statistical-completeness problems.

Evidence: Full Table II-Table V metrics; large gaps in answer removal; consistent direction for open-weight models.
Limiting factor: No uncertainty intervals, Wilcoxon results not shown, and several table/figure inconsistencies.
Raise/lower: Higher with corrected tables, confidence intervals/effect sizes, complete statistical outputs, and released predictions.

External validity: 4/10

The study does not establish behavior in real clinical workflows.

Evidence: MedMCQA spans medical subjects, but the experiments remain exam-style MCQ.
Limiting factor: Synthetic meta-warning prompts and forced answer-option removal differ from naturally incomplete clinical records and interactive care.
Raise/lower: Higher with multiple clinical datasets, open-ended tasks, multimodal inputs, clinician-facing simulations, and external sites.

Implementation value: 8/10

The stress-test design is straightforward to reuse in model evaluation pipelines.

Evidence: Clear perturbations, abstention target, ECE/calibration gap/UCER, paired-question design.
Limiting factor: Threshold choice and confidence elicitation require local validation; source inconsistencies need correction before reuse verbatim.
Raise/lower: Higher with released code, fixtures, exact prompts, and a validated clinical failure taxonomy.

Best use: design and critique of uncertainty/abstention stress tests for medical LLMs.
Do not use for: selecting a model for clinical deployment, estimating patient-level harm, or claiming that these exact model rankings generalize beyond this benchmark.
Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high for the study design and broad directional findings; moderate for exact answer-removal secondary values because of internal reporting inconsistencies.

Implementation / practice takeaways

Build

Add explicit “correct answer absent” fixtures to clinical-AI evaluations. Measure abstention, not just accuracy.

Measure

Track calibration gap/ECE and a safety metric such as high-confidence incorrect rate alongside accuracy. Predefine the confidence threshold.

Separate signals

Do not assume self-reported numeric confidence equals model-internal probability. Compare against logit or multi-sample uncertainty signals when available.

Avoid

Do not treat prompt warnings such as “information may be incomplete” as a sufficient uncertainty-control mechanism.

Validate locally

Use real clinical note incompleteness, open-ended reasoning, and workflow-specific abstention/escalation behaviors rather than only exam MCQs.

QA the benchmark

Release per-item predictions and validate internal arithmetic/captions; this paper’s Table III/IV/Fig. 5 discrepancies show why artifact-level QA matters.
