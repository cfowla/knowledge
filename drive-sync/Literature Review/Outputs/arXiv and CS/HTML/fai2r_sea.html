One-page synthesis

Core thesis. AI-assisted research should make claim-level provenance executable rather than merely disclose AI use after the fact. The proposed aiprov vocabulary extends PROV-O to record who or what performed each activity, which sources and prompts were used, what artefacts and claims were produced, what telemetry was available, and who granted each verification state. The method is packaged as an agent-operated skill and applied to production of this paper.

Source-derived synthesis
The central governance mechanisms are two invariants: no parentless claim, and a monotonic verification ladder whose highest rungs are human-only. The ladder distinguishes reference existence, AI content checking, durable source access, and human confirmation. The toolchain binds activities to git commits and content hashes, runs SPARQL conformance checks locally and in CI, regenerates disclosure and dashboard artefacts, and exposes a review queue intended to keep human judgement attached to the evidence it evaluates.

The self-demonstration reports a 3,323-triple graph, 244 logged activities, 8 claims, 75 ladder-backed sources, 222 commits, a 3,284-turn transcript, roughly 860.4 million cache-read tokens, and computed list-price cost near USD 1,641. It also records incidents, contested method changes, and provenance overhead. Bookkeeping is reported as 12.6% of provider requests, 11.8% of output tokens, 9.0% of computed cost, and 87 of 222 commits. The strongest evidence is therefore operational and introspective: the system produced a checkable record of itself.

Main constraint. The paper’s broader trust and portability claims remain weakly validated. The demonstration has one operator, one primary AI agent, one repository/domain, no independent source-verification model, no external replication, and provider telemetry that is still relayed through the agent/session record. Two internal numerical reconciliations also need resolution: 34 reported above-ceiling human rungs versus a narrative 23+13 split, and 206 Claude Code-associated activities in the disclosure/dashboard versus 242 AI activity-class counts in Table III.
Why it matters. The most build-useful contribution is not the prose argument about AI ethics; it is the concrete boundary between machine-checkable provenance and attributable human judgement, together with a CI-friendly invariant model that can fail closed when provenance is structurally incomplete.

Section condensation

Abstract

Source-derived
The abstract defines F(AI)2R as FAIR research with AI in the loop twice: AI participates in authoring and a second machine-readable audit pass records the resulting provenance. It introduces aiprov, the executable skill, and the paper-as-case-study design, emphasizing claim parentage and human-only verification rungs.

I. Introduction

Source-derived
The introduction frames the problem as missing machine-checkable provenance at claim granularity. Existing policy disclosures and documentation artefacts are presented as insufficient to recover who or what made a specific change, under which instructions, at what cost, and from which sources. The paper states three contributions: ontology/vocabulary, executable skill, and self-application.

II. Background and Related Work

Source-derived
The paper situates aiprov relative to W3C PROV-O/PAV, CRediT, model cards/datasheets, RO-Crate, PROV-AGENT, FAIR Digital Objects, nanopublications/micropublications, C2PA, in-toto, and Manubot. Its claimed distinction is a governance layer above provenance capture: claims become first-class resources, verification state is explicit, human-only rungs are enforced, and conformance failures can block a build. The novelty claim is deliberately bounded to the paper’s recorded search horizon.

III. The Dilution Crisis

Source-derived
The motivating argument is that generative AI lowers the marginal cost of producing publishable-looking text while review capacity remains scarce, increasing the value of provenance and verifiable metadata. The paper treats originality as a provenance property whose search horizon can be logged and rerun, and it distinguishes machine-checkable reference existence from judgement that a source actually supports a claim.

IV. The aiprov Method

Source-derived
The ontology extends PROV-O with human, AI, and tool agents; authoring, audit, build, and repair activities; and artefacts, claims, sources, transcripts, and prompts. Prompts are bound as plans, artefacts and prompts are hashed, and telemetry follows an omit-don’t-estimate rule. The verification ladder runs from unverified to human-read, with strict monotonic promotions and human-only top rungs.

The implementation uses a two-commit discipline so an activity can bind an already-existing commit while the graph update is recorded in a later commit. Seven SPARQL checks enforce claim parentage, attribution, human-only grant restrictions, audit-trail requirements, and source-access conditions. Report, extract, dashboard, and registry-search operations make the graph auditable and usable in CI.

V. The Executable Skill

Source-derived
The method is packaged as an agent skill. Setup begins with an ORCID iD, resolves identity information without guessing, derives repository identity from git, and scaffolds CI plus the manuscript. CI validates the graph, rebuilds the dashboard/disclosure/PDF, and blocks on hard conformance failure. Human verification can be staged through a live preview and authenticated issue workflow that transcribes, rather than decides, the human judgement.

The paper also separates structure from prose to make early decisions cheap to contest and presents an experimental-data workflow as a prospective design rather than a deployed example. Three additional features—portable reviewer registries, backward-closure propagation, and dispute routing—are clearly labeled as designs rather than shipped capabilities.

VI. Meta-Experiment: This Paper as Case Study

Source-derived
The self-demonstration includes one human operator, one primary AI agent, and CI as a tool agent. The graph and session record are treated as the evaluation object. Table II reports the graph state and telemetry; Table III reports the division of work. The process also records incidents rather than hiding them: accidental overwrites, malformed bibliographic data, blocked access, and operator-contested semantics.

Evidence for invariant enforcement includes a deliberately injected parentless claim that failed validation and an attempted AI grant of a human-only rung that the tool refused. Source content checks reportedly found three citation overreaches and repaired them. The paper also exposes measurement corrections: missing token telemetry was later recovered from provider session records, and cost was computed separately from a published price list rather than represented as provider-reported.

VII. Discussion and Limitations

Source-derived
The strongest acknowledged limitation is self-report honesty: repository-side hashes and CI make silent alteration harder, but the agent can still under-report or omit activity before it reaches the graph. The proposed architectural answer is a dual witness—provider-signed inference records plus repository-side commit binding—with optional third-party anchoring. The paper explicitly states that signatures can prove who attested to a record, not that a judgement is true.

The discussion also defends human-only rungs as a boundary marker rather than a claim of human infallibility, quantifies provenance overhead, reports missing energy telemetry, discusses method drift, self-citation transparency, regulation/compliance, industrial generalization, copyright, and possible post-paper scientific forms. Several ecosystem benefits are presented as hypotheses rather than measured results.

VIII. Conclusion

Source-derived
The conclusion restates the three contributions and argues that provenance should become the semantics of AI-assisted work. It positions integration into broader data infrastructure as the next step and lists future work in multi-agent attribution, energy accounting, registry integration, source vendoring, uncertainty-aware provenance, vocabulary registration, independent re-verification, and replication by another operator in another domain.

The paper closes with a strong scope statement: this tooling does not decide what AI-assisted science should become. It is intended to preserve a traceable record while social, editorial, legal, and scientific norms are still being negotiated.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. The paper is unusually implementation-dense for an AI provenance concept and supplies a concrete vocabulary, enforcement model, CI contract, failure behavior, public artefacts, and self-application record. It should be read as a build-oriented methods paper and hypothesis-generating demonstration, not as evidence that the approach is externally reliable or broadly effective.
Relevance
9/10

Strong fit for provenance-aware AI/ML research workflows, agent skills, scientific traceability, and human verification boundaries.

Evidence basis: executable skill, PROV-O model, CI validator, source ladder, self-demonstration. Limiting factor: relevance does not equal evidence. Would raise: production adoption or interop demonstrations.

Novelty
8/10

The combination of human-only verification rungs, executable-skill packaging, and self-application is distinctive within the paper’s recorded search horizon.

Evidence basis: explicit comparison with PROV-AGENT and related provenance lines. Limiting factor: novelty search is self-recorded and not independently verified here. Would raise: systematic external novelty review.

Method strength
6/10

The method is explicit, machine-checkable, versioned, and tested against defined failure cases, but the evaluation is a single self-applied demonstration.

Evidence basis: seven conformance checks, CI blocking, injected-failure examples, public record. Limiting factor: no independent evaluator or comparator. Would raise: preregistered external replications and adversarial tests.

Evidence strength
5/10

Evidence strongly supports “the system can record and validate this instance” but only weakly supports claims about general trust, scientific quality, or ecosystem effects.

Evidence basis: graph counts, transcripts, hashes, incidents, overhead metrics. Limiting factor: self-reporting, self-confirmation, internal count discrepancies. Would raise: independent verification and cross-tool/provider replication.

External validity
3/10

One operator, one principal AI agent, one session, one repository, and one paper/domain provide little basis for generalization.

Evidence basis: scope stated by the paper itself. Limiting factor: no second operator/domain. Would raise: multi-team, multi-domain, multi-provider evaluations.

Implementation value
9/10

High: the paper exposes concrete classes, invariants, state transitions, CLI failure behavior, CI integration, versioning, dashboarding, and release packaging.

Evidence basis: method/skill sections and figures 1–8. Limiting factor: some extensions are only designed. Would raise: stable API/spec, interoperability tests, and maintained external integrations.

Main strengths

Machine-checkable invariants; clear separation of reference resolution from content verification; explicit human-only trust boundary; provenance of prompts, transcripts, telemetry, artefacts, and incidents; fail-closed CI behavior; deliberate distinction between reported and computed telemetry; reproducible bookkeeping measurements; public project/data availability.

Main limitations

Single self-applied case; no independent audit of the provenance writer; same agent wrote and ai-confirmed citations; provider telemetry remains indirectly trusted; no energy measurement; no comparative baseline for productivity or quality; broad ecosystem/regulatory claims exceed what the demonstration measures; two visible numerical reconciliation problems weaken confidence in generated aggregate reporting.

What not to overclaim

Do not infer that human-only rungs make claims true, that content hashing prevents fraudulent omission, that provenance itself improves scientific validity, that the measured ~10% bookkeeping burden will generalize, or that the single demonstration validates regulatory compliance. The paper itself is strongest when it treats provenance as attributable evidence and a checkability boundary rather than as truth.

Implementation / research takeaways

Adopt the invariant pattern

Model inference
For any structured scientific-claim system, require every claim/atom to have a generating activity, an attributed agent, and an anchor/source relation before it can enter a validated state.

Separate machine and human review states

Model inference
Machine confirmation should not silently upgrade to a human-reviewed label. Treat human verification as an explicit grant by a named reviewer, with evidence handed over at the transition.

Make validation CI-native

Model inference
A small deterministic validator with a nonzero exit code is more operational than prose governance. Use it before publishing derived artefacts, dashboards, or disclosures.

Record provenance of provenance

Model inference
Version the schema/skill, bind activities to immutable commits/hashes, and distinguish reported telemetry from calculated telemetry. Aggregate reports should be regenerated from one source of truth.

Prefer evidence-before-judgement UX

Model inference
The review queue is a strong interface idea: present the claim, citing context, source access, and exact state transition together rather than asking reviewers to reconstruct context manually.

Test aggregation consistency

Model inference
The paper’s own count discrepancies show why generated disclosures and dashboards need contract/golden tests that reconcile per-agent, per-class, and total activity counts before release.

Best next evidence. An external replication should predefine the activity-count accounting, use an independent verifier for at least a sampled subset of source claims, include provider-signed or otherwise independently obtained usage telemetry, and compare two operators/domains. That would directly address the paper’s largest validity gaps.
