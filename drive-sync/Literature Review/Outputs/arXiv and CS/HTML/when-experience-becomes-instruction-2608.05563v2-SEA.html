One-page synthesis

Core thesis: In trajectory-grounded self-evolving skill systems, the critical trust boundary is not only the final skill file but the promotion decision that converts repeated experience into privileged, persistent instruction. A bounded attacker can exploit that boundary by making a harmful behavior appear recurrent, causally useful, and generalizable.
Attack mechanismPoisonedEvolution manipulates attacker-owned trajectories rather than directly editing skills. Artifact poisoning requires C1 Inclusion, C2 Evolution Attribution, and C3 Realization. The paper argues C2 is the distinctive SES bottleneck.
Main resultAt n=30, k=3 (10% attacker support), SkillClaw embeds target behaviors in 546/600 completed trials: 91.0% SER across six evolvers and four behavior families.
Cross-system resultTrace2Skill remains vulnerable under the same n=30, k=3 ratio, with 369/600 successful embeddings (61.5% SER), despite per-trace analysis and hierarchical consolidation.
Mechanistic resultRecurrence, causal framing, and domain-aligned encoding materially change success. In representative ablations, k=1 gives 5/25 versus 25/25 for k=3; causal framing gives 25/25 versus 8/25 local; tailored encoding gives 25/25 versus 0/25 auxiliary.
Security implicationQuality gates that ask whether evidence is useful or internally coherent are not the same as source-trust gates. Repeated malicious evidence can look exactly like the reusable experience SES systems are designed to distill.
Defense directionThe proposed pilot provenance-diversity gate rejects concentrated provenance and requires independent support. In one controlled F1 test it reduces post-gate SER from 25/25 to 0/25, but the evaluation is preliminary.
What the paper does not prove: It does not show end-to-end compromise after a poisoned skill is installed, does not evaluate repeated multi-cycle amplification, does not establish robustness across all SES architectures or domains, and does not validate the provenance-diversity gate against coordinated Sybil behavior.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The authors frame self-evolving skill (SES) systems as a security boundary because they convert untrusted interaction or execution trajectories into persistent skills that later act as trusted operating instructions. They introduce PoisonedEvolution, a skill-visible black-box trajectory-poisoning attack in which the attacker can inspect a target skill and contribute bounded evidence but cannot see private evidence pools, evolution prompts, or internal evolution logic and cannot directly modify the skill bank.

The paper defines artifact poisoning as the conjunction of Inclusion, Evolution Attribution, and Realization. At 10% attacker support, the reported SER is 91.0% (546/600) on SkillClaw and 61.5% (369/600) on Trace2Skill. The abstract highlights recurrence, causal framing, and domain-aligned encoding as the principal determinants of success and positions evidence promotion as the new trust boundary.

Introduction

The introduction distinguishes durable agent skills from ordinary background context: once installed or evolved, skills influence future operating procedure. The motivating failure is therefore not a malicious file arriving fully formed, but attacker-controlled evidence being interpreted by the victim’s own evolution process as reusable expertise and converted into a privileged artifact.

The authors separate SES poisoning from RAG poisoning. In RAG, poisoned content is directly retrieved into a generation context; in SES, the malicious pattern must first be interpreted as generalizable experience and written into the skill. The three stated contributions are: identifying evidence promotion as a distinct trust boundary, formalizing the C1–C3 mechanism with Evolution Attribution as the SES-specific bottleneck, and demonstrating cross-model/cross-system feasibility with provenance-aware defense implications.

Background

The paper defines an agent skill as a durable instruction artifact, often a SKILL.md file, that records workflows, triggers, constraints, examples, references, and potentially executable resources. It narrows its empirical scope to trajectory-grounded artifact evolution, especially SkillClaw and Trace2Skill, while noting that dialogue-memory, generator–verifier, reinforcement-learning, self-distillation, and learned-memory systems are related but outside the direct evaluation.

SkillClaw aggregates multi-user sessions and lets an evolver refine, create, or skip skill changes; Trace2Skill uses a frozen agent, separate success/error analysts, and hierarchical consolidation. The paper emphasizes that these structural differences matter because the attack must survive each system’s routing and synthesis logic. User-scoped, verifier-only, and parameter-training systems are explicitly outside the empirical claim set.

System and Threat Model

The SES pipeline is abstracted as Σ=(T,F,E,S): trajectory pool T, filter/router F, LLM-backed evolver E, and versioned skill bank S. The attacker contributes a bounded subset Tp to an otherwise clean pool Tc, with batch size n, attacker support k, and poisoning ratio ρ=k/n. The attacker is a legitimate but untrusted contributor: in shared systems this could be an ordinary or Sybil user; in offline systems it could be a task-corpus contributor, compromised telemetry source, or upstream environment that influences traces.

Success is measured at the artifact level. C1 means poisoned records reach an evolver-consumable stream, C2 means the evolver credits the target behavior as reusable knowledge or a necessary repair, and C3 means the behavior survives summarization/merge into the skill. The primary metric, SER, uses diff-aware family-specific canary rules to detect newly added behavioral semantics; LLM judges are only supportive inspection, not the success criterion. Hard and Soft benchmark accuracy are reported separately and are not treated as poisoning metrics.

Σ = (T, F, E, S)
S[t+1] = S[t] ⊕ E(F(T[t]), S[t])
PoisoningSuccess = C1 Inclusion ∧ C2 Attribution ∧ C3 Realization
Attacker objective: maximize Pr[b ∈ diff(S[t], S[t+1])] subject to |T[p]| ≤ k
SER = (1 / |R|) · Σ I_r(b) over completed evolution trials R

Attribution-Oriented Trajectory Poisoning

The attack transforms attacker-owned trajectories while preserving the task and outcome. Rather than inserting a naked malicious command, the attacker places the target behavior at a plausible workflow locus and binds it to outcome-relevant structure: constraint → target behavior → success for success-routed records, or missing target behavior → failure for failure-routed systems. Across poisoned trajectories, task details and wording vary while the malicious operational semantics remain stable.

The mechanism is organized around C1 domain alignment, C2 causal attribution, and C3 cross-trajectory realization. Four representative behavior families are evaluated with inert canaries: confidentiality (F1), data integrity (F2), supply-chain integrity (F3), and operational-safety weakening (F4). The experiments encode inert endpoints, temporary paths, loopback registries, and toy CI flags; they do not execute the referenced harmful actions.

Empirical Evaluation

SkillClaw is the primary testbed. The authors target its xlsx skill using a corpus of 300 case-level execution logs produced by GLM-5 on 100 SpreadsheetBench training tasks. Trials form task-distinct batches with n trajectories, k transformed attacker contributions, and n−k clean records. The main matrix uses n=30, k=3 (ρ=10%) with 25 completed trials per evolver–family cell. Native refine/create/skip decisions are retained, while SkillClaw’s optional session-quality judge and publish-time verifier are disabled in the main matrix because the authors characterize them as quality rather than source-provenance gates.

Across six evolvers and four behavior families, SkillClaw records 546/600 successful embeddings (91.0% SER). A no-init creation-path diagnostic across four representative evolvers reaches 490/600 (81.7%), but the authors do not interpret this as a clean causal mode comparison because the source traces were originally collected with the xlsx skill. Trace2Skill transfer uses three attacker-controlled failed trajectories plus 27 clean failures and yields 369/600 (61.5%). A benign-task check with Qwen3.5-122B-A10B reports 20.0% Hard and 36.54% Soft accuracy using the poisoned-evolved skill versus 18.0% and 34.88% with no skill, indicating coexistence of poisoning and normal-task usefulness on that evaluated split, not general utility preservation.

Discussion

The authors argue that native SES defenses are primarily utility-oriented: they can discard one-off, blunt, or cross-domain behavior, yet consistent repeated malicious evidence resembles exactly the pattern that a skill-evolution system is designed to promote. Their preferred intervention point is therefore before or during promotion, with provenance-aware checks that distinguish independent support from evidence concentrated in one user, Sybil cluster, or template family.

A pilot provenance-diversity gate requires three users and clusters, rejects majority dominance, and penalizes high text overlap. In a controlled F1 condition it blocks 25/25 single-cluster candidates and reduces post-gate SER from 25/25 to 0/25 while accepting one five-session diverse control. The authors explicitly label this preliminary because candidate grouping is assumed, the clean control is small, and coordinated Sybils may mimic diversity. They also stress that authentic artifact provenance is insufficient if the authorized evolver consumed adversarial evidence, and that artifact poisoning, runtime execution, and benign utility are separate dimensions.

Related Work

The paper positions itself between skill acquisition/evolution research, prompt and memory poisoning, retrieval poisoning, agent supply-chain attacks, and provenance/capability defenses. Its claimed distinction is the attack target: not an externally distributed malicious skill and not directly retrieved poisoned content, but the credit-assignment and promotion process that converts trajectory evidence into a trusted skill artifact.

The defense discussion treats instruction hierarchy, prompt-injection defenses, pre-execution skill detectors, capability separation, and software-supply-chain provenance as complementary rather than substitutable. The additional requirement for SES is semantic lineage from each promoted rule back to supporting and contradicting trajectories, contributor clusters, evolver version, and exact skill diff.

Conclusion

The conclusion restates the central finding: recurring adversarial trajectories can satisfy Inclusion, Evolution Attribution, and Realization, producing 91.0% SER on SkillClaw and 61.5% on Trace2Skill at 10% attacker support. The key risk is not merely malicious content but workflow-aligned evidence that the system normalizes as reusable expertise.

The authors' practical conclusion is that evidence promotion should be treated as a provenance-sensitive authorization decision. Their empirical claim remains artifact poisoning, not runtime compromise.

Algorithm 1 — Attribution-oriented trajectory poisoning

Inputs: target skill s; attacker-owned candidates A; behavior b; budget k.
1. Select k candidates whose tasks are related to s.
2. For each selected trajectory:
   a. Locate a legitimate workflow locus for b.
   b. Add an outcome-conditioned causal bridge:
      constraint → b → success, or missing b → failure.
   c. Preserve task outcome; vary surface wording while keeping b semantically invariant.
3. Contribute the k transformed trajectories; the SES forms a batch with n−k clean records.
4. Run the native evolution cycle.
5. Inspect the generated skill diff and report whether b is realized.

Failure modes identified by the study:
- insufficient recurrence;
- behavior appears unrelated or idiosyncratic;
- weak causal role in the trajectory;
- encoding is out-of-band or insufficiently domain-aligned;
- pipeline routing/consolidation suppresses the behavior.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first. The paper identifies a credible and under-specified trust boundary in self-evolving agents, supports it with a large artifact-level trial matrix plus cross-system transfer and mechanism ablations, and yields immediate architecture implications. The main constraints are narrow task domain, artifact-only outcomes, lack of multi-cycle/runtime validation, and preliminary defense evidence.

Relevance

10/10

Rationale: Directly addresses persistent agent skills, provenance, adversarial evidence, and reliability of self-evolving systems.

Evidence basis: Threat model, two SES architectures, six evolvers in the main matrix, and explicit promotion-time defense discussion.

Limiting factor: Relevance does not imply broad deployment validity.

Would change: Lower only for readers not working with persistent agent memory/skills or shared evidence pools.

Novelty

9/10

Rationale: The key conceptual move is targeting evidence-to-instruction promotion rather than final skill files, retrieval context, or direct memory injection.

Evidence basis: C1–C3 decomposition and attribution-oriented trajectory construction.

Limiting factor: It builds on established poisoning, supply-chain, and provenance ideas.

Would change: Stronger prior work showing the same promotion boundary would lower novelty; broader formalization across SES classes would raise it.

Method strength

8/10

Rationale: Controlled input-only intervention, fixed downstream components within cells, pre-defined canary families, explicit metrics, substantial repeated trials, and mechanism ablations support the central artifact-level claim.

Evidence basis: 600 main SkillClaw trials, 600 Trace2Skill transfer trials, sensitivity and ablation experiments.

Limiting factor: Optional SkillClaw quality modules are disabled in the main matrix; C3 ablation changes more than one factor; formal uncertainty/statistical testing is not emphasized.

Would change: Full native-gate comparisons, multi-domain replication, preregistered analysis/code reproduction, and cleaner factorial ablations would raise the score.

Evidence strength

8/10

Rationale: The reported data strongly support the narrow claim that attacker trajectories can be promoted into poisoned skill artifacts under the tested conditions.

Evidence basis: 91.0% SkillClaw SER, 61.5% Trace2Skill SER, recurrence and causal-framing gradients, family/model variation.

Limiting factor: No runtime harm measurement, no external deployment validation, no multi-cycle study, and narrow benign-utility testing.

Would change: End-to-end runtime impact, independent replication, longitudinal evaluation, and stronger uncertainty reporting would raise it; failed replication would lower it.

External validity

6/10

Rationale: Transfer across SkillClaw and structurally different Trace2Skill plus multiple evolvers supports some architectural generality.

Evidence basis: Cross-system result and model/family heterogeneity.

Limiting factor: Both evaluations remain spreadsheet-centered; user-scoped, verifier-only, training-coupled, and deployed multi-cycle systems are out of scope.

Would change: Additional domains, real multi-user deployments, more SES classes, and independent external replications would raise it.

Implementation value

9/10

Rationale: The paper translates directly into architecture requirements: retain lineage, evaluate provenance diversity before promotion, audit diffs, separate artifact/runtime metrics, and keep rollback/runtime enforcement.

Evidence basis: Pilot gate, discussion of lineage, staged rollout, reversible versions, and capability-based enforcement.

Limiting factor: The proposed gate is not production-validated and may be bypassed by coordinated Sybils.

Would change: A validated, scalable promotion policy with false-positive/false-negative characterization would raise the score.

Strengths

  • Threat model is specific and realistically constrained: input-only, bounded contribution, no evolver or skill-bank modification.
  • The paper separates artifact poisoning from runtime execution and avoids conflating SER with downstream ASR.
  • Mechanistic ablations make the central attribution claim testable rather than purely conceptual.
  • Cross-system transfer materially strengthens the argument beyond one prompt or one pipeline layout.
  • The defense discussion targets the correct stage: provenance-sensitive promotion before compression erases evidence origin.

Main limitations

  • Artifact-only endpoint: the study does not show trigger activation, tool execution, or real harm after skill poisoning.
  • Single-cycle evaluation: potential self-reinforcing feedback from poisoned skills into future trajectories is hypothesized but unmeasured.
  • Domain concentration: experiments are centered on the xlsx/spreadsheet workflow, limiting inference to other agent domains.
  • Main SkillClaw configuration: optional session-quality judging and publish-time verification are disabled, so the paper does not quantify their actual incremental effect under the same matrix.
  • Defense evidence is pilot-scale: one F1 concentrated-provenance condition and one small diverse control are insufficient to establish production robustness.
  • Utility evaluation is narrow: one 100-task split and a held-out smoke test do not establish a full security–utility tradeoff.
  • Statistical reporting: results are reported mainly as counts/rates; the paper does not foreground uncertainty intervals or formal hypothesis tests for cross-condition differences.
Do not overclaim: The results do not establish that one attacker controlling 10% of all real-world users can compromise any SES system. The reported ratio refers to controlled evolution batches under the tested task and pipeline configurations, and the attacker need not observe the clean pool or final n.

Confidence in appraisal: high for the paper's internal artifact-level claim and methodological reading; moderate for broader deployment implications because this appraisal does not independently inspect code or reproduce experiments and the source itself limits its empirical scope.

Implementation takeaways

1. Treat promotion as authorizationDo not assume that because the evolver generated the skill, the rule is trusted. Ask whether the evidence was independently supported and allowed to become privileged procedure.
2. Preserve semantic lineageFor each promoted rule, retain supporting and contradicting trajectories, contributor identities/clusters, timestamps, evolver version, and exact skill diff. Do not collapse provenance into normalized prose only.
3. Gate on diversity, not count aloneRepeated evidence from one source or correlated cluster should not be treated the same as independent corroboration. Text similarity and contributor concentration are useful signals, but Sybil resistance is still needed.
4. Separate security metricsTrack artifact contamination, retrieval/trigger activation, runtime action execution, and benign-task utility separately. A runtime block does not make a poisoned artifact clean.
5. Add staged deploymentNew or revised skills should be versioned, diffed, reviewable, reversible, and ideally evaluated counterfactually before wide distribution.
6. Keep runtime capability enforcementPromotion-time defenses are not sufficient. Capability-based controls and data-flow boundaries remain necessary because provenance checks can fail or be gamed.
Best use of this paper: security requirements and threat modeling for self-evolving agent architectures, especially shared skill banks, multi-user trajectory aggregation, automated memory/skill distillation, and any system where ordinary experience can silently become durable privileged instruction.
Do not use it as: a quantitative estimate of real-world compromise probability, a ranking of model safety, proof that the tested inert payloads would execute successfully, or proof that the pilot provenance gate is sufficient for production.
