One-page synthesis

Core thesis. A staged benchmark can do more than score a long-horizon agent: its aggregate stage diagnostics can route the next training curriculum while benchmark task content remains isolated from training rows. BaT operationalizes that idea with an asynchronous synthetic-data layer (Stage Bank) and an adaptive RL layer (BiCuRL).

What the system does. Stage Bank converts public workflow/stage specifications into leakage-checked fictional tasks, SFT slices, and executable RL sandboxes. BiCuRL evaluates the current policy on a fixed controller evaluation, selects the weakest stage, samples a fixed mixture of targeted-stage, other-stage, and E2E sandboxes, scores multiple rollouts with rubric/evidence checks, applies GRPO, and retains or falls back from candidate checkpoints. The trained policy is then paired with a fixed OpenHands execution layer to form a BaT Agent.

Main findings. On AutoMedBench-Lite, the 4B BiCuRL model scored 22.9 Overall versus 6.1 for its Instruct baseline; the 9B model scored 53.4 versus 19.9. In a matched 9B pool ablation, the full three-pool mixture reached 53.4 while E2E-only reached 31.9. As a complete agent system, BaT-9B Agent reached 79.6 on AutoMedBench-Lite versus 77.5 for Claude Opus 4.6 + Claude Code, while trailing the strongest shown systems on ABRA (70.6 vs 79.9) and MedXpertQA-Text (50.2 vs 65.0).

Transfer is the main caution. BaT-9B stayed within 3.4–5.8 points of its 9B Instruct baseline on AIME 2025, AIME 2026, and GPQA-Diamond and improved 3 of 5 long-horizon benchmarks (τ²-Bench, SWE-bench Verified, Terminal Bench 2.0). BaT-4B regressed on all eight external benchmarks, with several large drops. This makes model scale and task type central to interpreting the method.

Evidence boundary. The study’s own Limitations subsection says the evidence has “six limits” but actually enumerates only four before Related Work begins. This artifact preserves that inconsistency; it does not invent two missing limitations.

Section-by-section condensation

Abstract

The abstract introduces BaT as a recursive post-training system for long-horizon medical research agents. It identifies two linked components: Stage Bank, which produces content-isolated training states, and BiCuRL, which uses a fixed held-out evaluation to select a stage curriculum, scores rollouts with task rubrics, updates with GRPO, and returns candidates to evaluation. It reports more than doubling the Qwen Instruct Overall baselines and the BaT-9B Agent 79.6 AutoMedBench-Lite score.

1. Introduction

The motivation is stage-localized failure in long medical workflows: planning, setup, validation, inference, and submission errors are not adequately addressed by one trajectory-level reward. The research question is whether a structured benchmark can provide curriculum signals while its task content stays held out. The authors define four contributions: BaT as the full recursive system, Stage Bank as the data layer, BiCuRL as the adaptive optimization loop, and BaT Agents as trained policies plus a fixed execution environment.

2. Benchmark-as-Teacher / 2.1 Stage Bank

The staged benchmark is formalized as ordered stages, rubric/evidence contracts, and fixed held-out evaluation. Stage Bank’s Data Factory synthesizes fictional medical-imaging tasks from public workflow descriptions and stage contracts. A preflight excludes held-out identifiers, paths, reports, traces, answers, and evaluation-derived metadata. Aggregate recording statistics may guide synthesis; raw task content is stated not to enter prompts or rows.

SFT data are one-response slices from multi-turn teacher trajectories. RL data are executable stage-boundary sandboxes containing files, tools, intermediate artifacts, goals, recovery procedures, and rubrics. Rows are indexed with provenance and stable state IDs. The three pools—S-target, S-mix, and E2E—separate targeted practice, forgetting mitigation, and cross-stage coordination.

2.2 BiCuRL / 2.3 Agent

BiCuRL implements a bilevel loop. Controller evaluation returns Overall plus five stage scores; the router chooses the weak stage and builds a curriculum. The inner loop samples Stage Bank states, generates multiple rollouts, applies an LLM rubric verifier to execution evidence, and uses group-normalized GRPO. The outer loop re-evaluates, records scores and policy shift, and either accepts a candidate or falls back. Controller and final evaluation runs are disjoint, but the same benchmark family still supplies adaptive diagnostics.

Appendix A.4 makes the curriculum concrete: pool weights are 1/2 S-target, 1/4 S-mix, and 1/4 E2E; a KL trigger of 0.1 is estimated on 256 held-out Stage Bank prompts; the patience trigger is three consecutive score drops. The BaT Agent wraps the policy in fixed OpenHands public skills, keeping runtime engineering constant within BaT Agent comparisons.

3. Experimental Setting

AutoMedBench-Lite is the primary adaptive/diagnostic benchmark. Seven tracks are repeated 10 times, for 70 runs per system, and tested agents average 33 interaction turns. Task and Agentic are equally weighted into Overall; Agentic itself weights Plan/Setup/Validate/Inference/Submit at 0.25/0.15/0.35/0.15/0.10. ABRA contributes 655 radiology tasks across three tiers, eight task types, and 21 tools; MedXpertQA-Text evaluates text medical reasoning.

Experiments compare Qwen3.5 4B/9B Instruct, SFT, GRPO, and BiCuRL under local controls, plus broader system comparisons and external benchmarks. The Stage Bank includes 20,299 candidate prompt states; the synthetic E2E source pool has 1,008 rows; SFT starts use 4,608 rows. The matched pool ablation uses a 275-row contract.

4. Results

BiCuRL produces the strongest primary local results. For 4B, Overall moves from 6.1 baseline to 22.9 BiCuRL; for 9B, from 19.9 to 53.4. GRPO reaches 11.4 and 31.9, respectively. The best-so-far checkpoint logic matters because raw round scores fluctuate; the retained trajectories preserve accepted gains through round ten.

The full three-pool 9B run reaches 53.4, versus 31.9 for E2E-only; each drop-one-pool run is at least 26 points below full. Figure 6’s “tiny” ≤12B subset places BaT-9B first and BaT-4B third, while the broader 19-model Appendix table places BaT-9B third behind 27B and 35B models. On external transfer, 9B largely retains short-turn reasoning and improves τ²-Bench (+5.4), SWE-bench Verified (+2.8), and Terminal Bench 2.0 (+1.4), whereas 4B declines on all eight tests.

5. Discussion and Limits

The authors explicitly acknowledge that AutoMedBench-Lite is both diagnostic and adaptive: task content is isolated, but aggregate scores determine later curricula, preventing interpretation as an untouched final test. They propose a separate untouched test and measured semantic-leakage audit as future work. They also note incomplete per-run manifests in the external table, weak 4B transfer, and the system-level nature of top-tier comparisons.

A textual inconsistency remains: the subsection says “six limits” but only four are enumerated. This reduces confidence in the completeness of the stated limitations inventory but does not invalidate the reported experiments.

6. Related Work

The paper positions BaT at the intersection of staged medical/agent benchmarks, GRPO and process/rubric supervision, curriculum and iterative self-improvement, executable agent environments, and synthetic trajectory generation. Its claimed distinction is putting structured held-out benchmark diagnostics directly into the training-control loop while isolating benchmark task content.

7. Conclusion

The conclusion frames BaT as evidence that staged benchmarks can become evaluator-plus-teacher infrastructure. It reiterates the large AutoMedBench-Lite gains, the 79.6 BaT-9B Agent system score, and the scale-dependent transfer pattern. The appropriate interpretation is architectural promise with meaningful matched internal gains, not a universal claim that benchmark-adaptive post-training preserves general capabilities.

Appendix A.1–A.8

The appendix materially strengthens reproducibility. It describes Stage Bank construction and filtering, the multi-turn GRPO objective, conditions for migration to other staged benchmarks, SFT/runtime parameters, local-model evaluation, AutoMedBench-Lite tracks/weights, matched ablation controls, and external-benchmark protocols. It also clarifies that recorded serving throughput is descriptive because the source run summary omits remaining controls.

Evaluation and appraisal

Verdict: Read first — for architecture and benchmark-to-training control-loop design

Best use: designing staged agent post-training systems, rubric/evidence reward loops, curriculum routing, and reproducible benchmark-aware training infrastructure. Do not use for: claiming that AutoMedBench-Lite is an untouched generalization test, or that the 79.6 vs 77.5 system comparison isolates policy quality.

9/10
Relevance

Directly relevant to long-horizon agents, clinical/medical AI workflow evaluation, post-training, and verifier-driven learning.

Evidence basis: full staged agent workflow + medical benchmark setting. Limiter: narrow domain and specific staged-benchmark prerequisites. Would rise: successful replication in non-medical staged systems.

8/10
Novelty

The distinctive move is turning benchmark stage diagnostics into an explicit curriculum controller while isolating task content.

Evidence basis: Stage Bank + BiCuRL architecture and adaptive loop. Limiter: components draw on existing curriculum, GRPO, synthetic trajectories, and rubric rewards. Would rise: stronger comparative evidence that the coupled control architecture is uniquely necessary.

6/10
Method strength

Good matched local controls, detailed ablation contract, disjoint controller/final runs, and substantial appendix detail.

Limiter: adaptive use of the primary benchmark, no untouched final test, incomplete per-run manifests in external aggregates, and system-level top-tier comparisons. Would rise: preregistered untouched test + semantic leakage audit + fully matched system controls.

6/10
Evidence strength

Primary internal gains and pool ablation are large and quantitatively clear; transfer results expose meaningful failure modes rather than hiding them.

Limiter: external transfer is mixed, 4B regressions are severe, and the primary benchmark participates in adaptation. Would rise: independent replication and stable cross-benchmark gains.

5/10
External validity

External benchmarks broaden the picture, but the benefit is strongly scale- and task-dependent and the method requires staged benchmarks with usable rubrics/evidence contracts.

Limiter: one primary adaptive domain plus heterogeneous external protocols. Would rise: multi-domain, multi-backbone replicated training studies.

8/10
Implementation value

The paper provides an unusually concrete control loop, pool construction, data boundary, equations, training settings, fallback rules, and open-code claim.

Limiter: some operational details remain incomplete, including serving controls and independent verification. Would rise: released reproducible Stage Bank data/manifests and end-to-end scripts.

Main strengths

  • Architecture cleanly separates synthetic state production from policy updates.
  • Data-boundary design is explicit about what evaluation information may cross into training control.
  • Matched 9B ablation isolates pool composition better than the broader cross-system benchmark panels.
  • External benchmark section demonstrates negative transfer at 4B rather than presenting only favorable results.
  • Appendix exposes useful training, runtime, metric, and evaluation details.

Main limitations / risks

  • The primary benchmark is adaptive and therefore not an untouched final test.
  • No measured semantic-leakage audit is provided.
  • Cross-system medical comparisons confound policy and execution setting.
  • External aggregates combine local and published results with incomplete per-run manifests.
  • 4B catastrophic transfer losses raise a material capability-retention concern.
  • The paper’s own “six limits” statement is internally incomplete: only four are enumerated.

Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high. The full paper, appendix, figures, tables, equations, and bibliography were inspected. Confidence is limited by the study’s preprint status, adaptive evaluation design, and missing untouched final test.

Implementation / practice takeaways

1. Treat benchmark diagnostics as control signals, not final proof.

Once evaluation outputs route training, create a separate untouched test and record exactly which aggregate signals cross the boundary.

2. Preserve semantic training pools.

The matched ablation supports keeping targeted-stage, other-stage, and E2E states distinct rather than collapsing all training into one undifferentiated pool.

3. Make fallback a first-class policy.

Recursive training is not monotonic; checkpoint retention, patience, and a policy-shift guard can prevent one weak round from becoming the next initializer.

4. Version data and reward contracts.

Stage Bank rows carry provenance, stage keys, rubric items, evidence requirements, and stable IDs—useful design choices for deterministic audit and later replay.

5. Test capability retention by model scale.

The 4B/9B divergence suggests that post-training safety should include general reasoning and long-horizon regression suites, not only domain gains.

6. Separate policy claims from harness claims.

When comparing agents, freeze the execution layer or explicitly label the result as a system-level comparison; BaT itself follows this rule within BaT Agent comparisons.
