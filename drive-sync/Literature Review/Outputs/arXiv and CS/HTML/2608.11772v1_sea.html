One-page synthesis

Core thesis. Agent recovery should begin by diagnosing which recovery interventions are admissible for the observed failure mode, then spend additional recovery context only inside that restricted interface. DARC operationalizes this as a frozen task-family recovery harness selected before test evaluation.

What DARC changes: a failure is not treated as a generic trigger for reflection, more examples, longer manuals, or a broad playbook. Development failures are profiled to identify a dominant bottleneck; mismatched interventions are removed; training-set verifier success and cost are then used to choose a short fallback chain. The selected policy is frozen for deployment and stops once an intervention succeeds.

Where it is tested: ALFWorld is mapped to action validity, AppWorld to procedure breadth, and XBRL Finance to format precision. These mappings deliberately stress different recovery interfaces: action guards, procedural knowledge/fallbacks, and retrieval-demonstration budgets.

Section condensation

1. Introduction

The paper motivates diagnosis-guided recovery by contrasting broad language-agent tasks with coding environments, where compiler errors, test failures, and execution traces make failure types actionable. In non-coding agent settings, a single coarse failure can reflect incompatible causes—invalid actions, missing procedures, or strict-format errors—yet generic recovery recipes may indiscriminately append more context.

The authors identify three specific costs of this approach: intervention mismatch, recovery interference, and uncontrolled recovery cost. DARC is proposed to first restrict the recovery interface based on development-set failures and only then distill how much recovery evidence to spend.

2. Related Work

DARC is situated against execution-feedback self-correction, reflection and recursive critique, prompt optimization, generic context expansion, cascades/model routing, and adaptive retrieval. The paper’s distinction is the routing target: it routes among recovery interventions rather than among base models or arbitrary prompt variants.

The argument is also connected to work showing that irrelevant or poorly positioned context can degrade model behavior. This supports the design choice to prune recovery context before policy search instead of appending a universal bundle after failure.

3. Problem Setup: Diagnosis-Guided Recovery

A task family X produces agent outputs or trajectories evaluated by a binary task verifier and a nonnegative cost such as environment steps, latency, or retrieval budget. A recovery intervention is an executable correction signal—e.g., an action guard, procedural source, local induction rule, or retrieval budget—that can be evaluated by the verifier.

The recovery harness Hm consists of a diagnosed failure mode, an admissible intervention subset, and an ordered policy. The objective favors success while penalizing excess recovery cost, making two questions explicit: which recovery signals are actionable for this failure mode, and in what order should they be invoked?

4. DARC: Diagnosis-Guided Agent Recovery and Correction

Stage 1 profiles development failures and selects a dominant task-family failure mode plus a restricted intervention set. The diagnosis is frozen before test evaluation, preventing test-label leakage and keeping the harness auditable. Stage 2 builds an evidence matrix from training tasks, recording verifier success and cost for admissible interventions, enumerates bounded short-circuit policies, scores them with a success-cost objective, and freezes the best policy.

The paper notes two structural properties: the success term behaves as a monotone submodular coverage function over intervention subsets, and reducing the intervention set shrinks the finite candidate chain class. The latter supports a finite-sample search-risk argument but does not by itself prove transfer to new failure modes.

5. Experiments

The evaluation asks about performance, within-family transfer, the importance of correct diagnosis, and recovery cost. ALFWorld, AppWorld, and Finance are deliberately assigned different recovery diagnoses. Baselines include Base LLM, ICL, MIPROv2, GEPA, and ACE, with supervision/answer-label use explicitly separated; no reported method uses test labels.

Main results generally favor DARC on average, especially ALFWorld, AppWorld Test-Normal, and Finance. The authors explicitly avoid claiming uniform dominance: ACE is competitive or better on some AppWorld challenge metrics. For the headline AppWorld Test-Normal comparison, scenario-cluster bootstrap intervals and paired tests after Holm correction support the DARC gap for DeepSeek-V4-Flash.

6. Analysis and Ablation

Diagnosis ablations compare correct, generic, and mismatched intervention sets. Correct diagnosis wins across the reported settings, although AppWorld ablation confidence intervals overlap. The matched-information ALFWorld analysis directly tests privileged-information concerns: matched metadata gives only a small gain, random top-12 pruning can harm, and diagnosis as a bare label stays near base performance; the large gain appears with diagnosis-guided ranked recovery.

Additional analyses probe a weight-space curriculum (single-seed proof of concept), benchmark-specific mechanisms, retrieval-budget non-monotonicity, and cost/speed. The appendix factorial finds a strong interaction between restricted action view and recovery prompt, while the matched full-library cascade shows that diagnosis can preserve accuracy while greatly shrinking search space.

7. Conclusion and Limitations

The authors frame DARC as a shift from indiscriminate context expansion to recovery-interface design: use failures to determine what can plausibly repair the problem, then allocate recovery evidence selectively. They report improved performance and lower recovery cost in the tested settings.

Stated future work includes dynamic instance-level routing for mixed-mode failures, more sample-efficient policy distillation, and broader full-library cascade comparisons. Those directions also define the main boundary of the present evidence.

Appendices A–I

The appendices provide proofs for coverage/submodularity and the finite chain-class bound; explicit task-family-to-harness mappings and representative diagnosis traces; an AppWorld fallback trace; ALFWorld factorial decomposition; baseline definitions; a full-library matched diagnosis control; and automatic-diagnosis agreement on 72 development traces.

These appendices materially strengthen interpretability of the main claims because they expose what “diagnosis” means operationally, isolate the paired restriction+instruction mechanism, and show that diagnosis mainly improves search efficiency/stability in the matched ALFWorld cascade rather than obtaining accuracy from a different search procedure.

Algorithm 1 — DARC recovery-harness construction

Inputs: task family X; candidate intervention library R; training tasks D_train;
        development failure-profile data D_dev; cost penalty λ.
1. Profile base-agent failures on D_dev.
2. Diagnose dominant failure mode m and choose admissible R_m ⊆ R.
3. Evaluate each admissible intervention on D_train for verifier success and cost.
4. Enumerate bounded candidate chains and score success minus excess-cost penalty.
5. Freeze the highest-scoring task-family recovery policy before test evaluation.

Evaluation & appraisal

Verdict: Read first. Best use: architecture and evaluation patterns for selective agent recovery, especially when a failure can be mapped to a small set of executable interventions. Do not use it as evidence that static task-family diagnosis will generalize to heterogeneous production failures.
9/10

Relevance

Rationale: Directly addresses agent reliability, self-correction, failure diagnosis, routing, and cost-aware recovery interface design.

Evidence basis: The core framework and experiments are centered on failure-aware recovery across embodied, tool-use, and structured-extraction agents.

Principal limiting factor: The work is not clinical or biomedical and is evaluated only on research benchmarks.

Would move the score: Broader evidence on production agent systems would raise it; a narrower prompt-optimization framing would lower it.

8/10

Novelty

Rationale: The main contribution is the causal ordering “diagnose admissible recovery first, then distill how much recovery to spend,” rather than uniformly expanding context.

Evidence basis: A frozen task-family recovery harness, explicit admissible-intervention restriction, success-cost policy distillation, and diagnosis-isolation controls are all developed around this idea.

Principal limiting factor: The components draw on established ideas from routing, cascades, retrieval budgeting, and verifier-guided adaptation.

Would move the score: Instance-level learned diagnosis with convincing mixed-mode evaluation would raise it; evidence that the gains reduce to ordinary routing would lower it.

7/10

Rationale: The study uses multiple benchmarks, controlled adaptation budgets, supervision accounting, ablations, matched-information controls, scenario-cluster uncertainty analysis, and an ALFWorld full-library matched control.

Evidence basis: Tables 2, 6, 7, 13, and 14 explicitly probe label access, diagnosis mismatch, privileged-information concerns, component interaction, and the effect of diagnosis on search.

Principal limiting factor: The strongest matched full-library comparison and factorial decomposition are ALFWorld-only; several results are single-run benchmark evaluations.

Rationale: The framework is tested across three qualitatively different failure modes, but each benchmark family is assigned one dominant task-family diagnosis.

Evidence basis: ALFWorld, AppWorld, and Finance cover action validity, procedural breadth, and format precision, with within-family transfer tests.

Principal limiting factor: Static family-level diagnosis does not establish behavior under mixed, shifting, or previously unseen failure modes; all evidence is benchmark-local.

Implementation value

Rationale: The paper exposes a concrete harness contract: diagnose, restrict interventions, collect verifier success/cost, enumerate bounded chains, freeze a deployment policy, and short-circuit on success.

Evidence basis: Algorithm 1, Table 1 deployment semantics, Table 11 harness mappings, and the cost/ablation sections provide reusable design boundaries and failure checks.

Principal limiting factor: Exact implementation details for every intervention and complete released artifacts are not contained in the PDF itself.

Would move the score: A public reference implementation with per-task traces, configuration, and reproducibility scripts would raise it.

Main strengths

  • Clear architectural separation between diagnosis, intervention admissibility, policy selection, and deployment.
  • Supervision accounting explicitly distinguishes training success, answer labels, and test labels.
  • Multiple negative controls address the obvious alternative explanations: generic context, mismatched recovery, privileged metadata, arbitrary pruning, and full-library search without diagnosis.
  • Cost is treated as a first-class outcome rather than an afterthought.
  • The paper reports non-uniformity rather than hiding challenge metrics where ACE remains competitive.

Main limitations

  • One dominant failure mode is assigned per benchmark family; real agents often face mixed and evolving failure causes.
  • The strongest matched controls are concentrated in ALFWorld, limiting confidence that the same diagnosis advantage holds for procedural and formatting failures.
  • Uncertainty reporting is uneven: AppWorld receives scenario-cluster analysis, while many other comparisons are point estimates.
  • The automatic diagnosis evaluation uses a verifier-backed reference rather than an independently human-labeled gold standard in the reported table.
  • Weight-space extension evidence is single-seed and explicitly preliminary.
  • No production deployment, longitudinal failure distribution shift, or instance-level dynamic router is evaluated.
Important interpretation boundary: the paper supports “restrict recovery to failure-compatible interventions before spending more context” on the tested benchmark families. It does not establish that a generic diagnostic LLM can discover arbitrary latent failure modes or route correctly under novel mixed failures.

Confidence in appraisal: moderate-high. The complete primary PDF, including appendices and all main figures/tables, was inspected and reconciled; confidence is limited mainly by the paper’s own benchmark scope and lack of external replication in this task.

Implementation takeaways

1. Make failure actionable

Do not let “task failed” directly trigger a universal recovery prompt. Convert observable failure signatures into a small typed recovery interface.

2. Separate diagnosis from optimization

First decide what interventions are admissible; only then optimize ordering, budget, or escalation. This reduces search and avoids comparing incompatible recovery signals.

3. Freeze the test boundary

Development-set diagnosis and training-set verifier adaptation should be complete before test deployment. Keep test labels and hidden state out of the router.

4. Use short-circuit escalation

Start with the cheapest compatible recovery intervention; invoke richer context only after verifier-visible failure.

5. Measure recovery cost

Track steps, invalid actions, latency, retries, retrieval budget, and success per unit cost—not just task completion.

6. Test interaction, not components alone

A restriction layer and recovery instruction may be weak individually but strong together. Factorial ablations can distinguish interface synergy from additive prompt gains.

What to monitor in a real harness

  • Diagnosis stability and disagreement with deterministic/verifier signals.
  • Fraction of failures that do not fit any known failure mode.
  • Per-intervention marginal recovery rate and incremental cost.
  • Frequency of recovery interference after adding context.
  • Distribution shift in failure modes across releases.
  • Whether diagnosis restriction reduces search complexity without suppressing recoverable alternatives.
