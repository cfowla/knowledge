Section condensation

Abstract

The abstract frames the gap as inadequate evaluation of clinical LLMs in structured EHR-congruent formats. The authors propose a reusable pipeline that generates terminology-grounded HL7 FHIR R4 bundles from unstructured clinical text while reducing hallucinated codes and enforcing structural and semantic consistency.

Applied to MedCaseReasoning, the pipeline yields MedCase-Structured: 1,732 synthetic FHIR bundles derived from clinician-authored diagnostic cases. The headline result is lower diagnostic accuracy for LLMs on structured FHIR inputs compared with plain text, presented as evidence that deployment-aligned benchmarking is necessary.

1. Introduction

The introduction argues that EHR data are attractive inputs for CDSS but are heterogeneous, privacy-constrained, and increasingly represented through standards such as HL7 FHIR. Existing benchmarks and datasets either use unstructured text, fixed retrospective EHR data, or synthetic records that lack the fine-grained control needed to stress-test reasoning.

The authors position MedCase-Structured as a bridge between narrative diagnostic case reports and interoperable FHIR-style evaluation inputs. The key premise is that reasoning over coded resources is qualitatively different from reasoning over semantically continuous prose, so plain-text clinical benchmarks can overstate readiness for EHR-native use.

2. Related Work

The paper distinguishes itself from rule-based clinical information extraction, LLM-based text-to-FHIR tools such as FHIR-GPT and Infherno, and synthetic EHR generators such as Synthea. Those systems primarily reconstruct records or generate module-driven synthetic patients rather than produce controllable diagnostic benchmark cases.

It also contrasts MedCase-Structured with EHRStruct and FHIR-AgentBench, which evaluate LLMs on structured EHR/FHIR tasks but operate on fixed datasets. The authors claim novelty in controllable, text-driven generation of clinically realistic FHIR records designed for diagnostic reasoning evaluation.

3. Method

The method uses fixed-stage LLM calls rather than dynamic agentic tool use. GPT-5.4-mini is reported for extraction, Gemini 3.5 Flash for candidate reranking, and Claude Sonnet 4-5 for FHIR synthesis and semantic leak detection. Extraction and reranking use JSON-schema-constrained outputs; synthesis and leak detection use token caps of 64K and 4K, respectively; generation for the pipeline uses temperature 0.

The workflow separates concept extraction from code assignment. Concepts are embedded with SapBERT, retrieved from FAISS indexes of terminology preferred terms, reranked with source-quote context, validated for code-system and semantic-axis compatibility, then synthesized into FHIR R4 resources. FHIR schema checks, invariants, formatting checks, consistency checks, repair loops, and deterministic post-processing are used to improve structural validity.

3.1 Extraction

The first LLM stage converts free-text cases into a typed intermediate representation containing demographics, symptoms, findings, vitals, labs, medications, procedures, history, and the primary diagnosis. It retains a verbatim quote for every extracted item, which supports traceability during grounding and validation.

Pertinent negatives are explicitly flagged to prevent incorrect assertion as positive findings. The primary diagnosis is captured separately to allow configurable hiding during benchmark creation.

3.2 Terminology Grounding

Extracted concepts are routed to target terminologies: SNOMED CT, LOINC, RxNorm, and CVX. Candidate retrieval uses SapBERT embeddings with a FAISS index, followed by an LLM reranker that can select a candidate or reject all candidates based on the source quote.

Deterministic checks enforce resource-compatible terminology and SNOMED semantic-axis constraints. Concepts that cannot be acceptably grounded remain as free text. This protects against invalid codes but also leaves 18.2% of distinct concepts uncoded in the final dataset.

3.3 FHIR Synthesis and Validation

The synthesis stage assembles grounded intermediate data into HL7 FHIR R4 resources, including Patient and Encounter plus clinical resource types relevant to diagnostic case context. The paper emphasizes diagnostic-case representation rather than comprehensive longitudinal EHR reconstruction.

Validation includes FHIR R4 schema conformance, FHIR invariants, formatting checks, and clinical consistency checks. Validation errors are fed back to the synthesis model for up to three repair attempts, followed by deterministic post-processing for missing resources, unit normalization, dates, and status fields.

3.4 Diagnosis Hiding

The dataset supports four diagnosis handling modes: NONE, HIDDEN, EXPLICIT, and FULL. NONE removes all diagnostic conclusions; HIDDEN removes the primary diagnosis; EXPLICIT retains only patient-stated conditions; FULL retains all extracted diagnoses.

In the diagnosis-suppressed modes, code and substring filters are followed by an LLM semantic scan over narrative fields to remove residual leakage through abbreviations, implied conclusions, and synonyms. The approach is useful but not a formal guarantee against all leakage.

4. MedCase-Structured

The dataset is built from MedCaseReasoning, an open-access collection of approximately 14,500 diagnostic cases from public case reports. Cases are excluded when they are non-human, include multiple patients, or require imaging details unsupported by the generator.

Of 14,489 original cases across train, validation, and test splits, only 1,784 are processed after exclusions, and 1,732 succeed. This is a high conversion rate among processed cases but a narrow retained subset of the original corpus.

4.2 Pipeline Failure Modes

Terminology coverage is the dominant limitation. Across 20,794 distinct extracted concepts, 3,775 are never coded and remain text-only. RxNorm has the lowest concept-level coverage at 74.5%, followed by LOINC at 77.6%, SNOMED CT at 83.5%, and CVX at 94.7%.

Separately, 52 processed cases are excluded because at least one extracted clinical element is dropped during grounding or validation. In 42 cases, the element could not be grounded to a semantic-axis-compatible code; in 10 cases, an assigned code failed FHIR or terminology validation.

4.3 Evaluation

The evaluation compares model accuracy on the FHIR-based MedCase-Structured dataset against corresponding plain-text MedCaseReasoning questions. The reported models are GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro, and Claude-Opus-4.6, tested with zero-shot, one-shot, and five-shot prompting over three runs.

All settings show lower mean accuracy on FHIR inputs than on plain text. The largest reported drop is GPT-5.4 five-shot, from 70.91% on text to 58.18% on FHIR, a 12.73-point decrease. Few-shot examples do not reliably close the representation gap.

5. Conclusion and Impact Statement

The conclusion restates the contribution as a reusable clinical narrative-to-FHIR pipeline and a synthetic benchmark for structured diagnostic reasoning. The authors argue that structured FHIR inputs introduce additional reasoning challenges and that CDSS benchmarks should use deployment-aligned data formats.

The impact statement is appropriately cautious: synthetic data can improve controlled evaluation but does not replace real-world clinical validation. The stated limitations include limited FHIR resource coverage, incomplete temporal modeling, lack of imaging support, and unresolved terminology-grounding challenges.

Main strengths

  • Directly addresses the mismatch between plain-text clinical benchmarks and structured EHR deployment contexts.
  • Uses terminology-aware validation rather than allowing unconstrained LLM-generated clinical codes.
  • Quantifies conversion outcomes, terminology coverage, uncoded gaps, and matched text-vs-FHIR accuracy differences.
  • Includes experimental prompts and a FHIR bundle sample, improving interpretability of the benchmark setup.

Main limitations

  • Case-report-derived synthetic data are not equivalent to real EHR data and do not capture full longitudinal workflow complexity.
  • Large exclusion of imaging-dependent cases narrows the diagnostic spectrum.
  • Terminology coverage gaps leave 18.2% of distinct concepts text-only.
  • Evaluation relies on an LLM judge, lacks uncertainty intervals, and provides limited model error analysis.
  • Diagnosis leakage detection is described but not quantified with an independent audit.
Do not overclaim. The paper shows that FHIR representation can reduce LLM diagnostic accuracy in this synthetic benchmark. It does not prove that FHIR is inherently worse for clinical reasoning, that any specific model is unsafe, or that benchmark performance predicts bedside CDSS behavior.

Implementation and research takeaways

Use as a stress testRun candidate clinical LLMs on both narrative and FHIR representations of matched cases to measure representation sensitivity before any workflow pilot.
Audit terminology groundingTrack coded, text-only, rejected, and axis-mismatched concepts by resource type; do not treat valid JSON as clinically valid content.
Separate leakage checksUse independent review for diagnosis leakage, especially abbreviations, synonyms, inferred conclusions, and diagnosis-bearing resource text.
Require human adjudicationPair LLM-judge accuracy with clinician review for representative samples and all disputed or high-impact cases.
Add real-world validationBefore operational use, test on local FHIR exports, noisy EHR timelines, medication histories, lab panels, imaging text, and incomplete records.
Govern benchmark driftFreeze model versions, prompts, few-shot examples, terminology snapshots, validator versions, and judge prompts for reproducible comparisons.
